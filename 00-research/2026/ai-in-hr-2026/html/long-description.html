AI in HR 2026

Executive Summary

AI is HR’s Top Priority

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the most critical HR-related issue going into 2026, according to new research conducted by the HR Research Institute (HRRI) in partnership with Darwinbox.

Even though AI has become a high HR priority, adoption remains uneven and its impact is still concentrated in chatbot-related efficiency gains rather than in strategic workforce decisions or agentic workflows.

When we asked HR professionals about the top five issues that will be important to their organizations headed into 2026, the top answer was “AI usage,” chosen by 38% of respondents.1 That puts it ahead of other critical HR issues such as employee retention, learning & development, performance management and even employee experience.

But Most Organizations Are Still Experimenting

HRRI followed up these findings with another series of pulse survey questions. These underscore how behind many HR functions are in terms of AI usage:

  • only a quarter report actively using AI in HR
  • another 14% are piloting systems
  • 8% say they are planning to adopt AI in 2026 (indicating a lack of concrete action)
  • 14% are only considering adopting AI in 2026
  • almost two-fifths (39%) report no AI use at all in HR2

In short, most HR professionals are not yet using AI for HR in their organizations, but over a third are piloting, planning or considering AI usage.

Among HR Users of AI, Expectations Are High

Expectations for productivity gains are high. Among HR professionals whose organizations plan to incorporate more AI into their HR technology stack, over three-quarters (77%) believe AI will increase productivity, driven primarily by task automation (71%). At the same time, fewer expect AI to meaningfully improve workforce planning (41%) or turnover prediction (31%).3

Across HR functions, AI is most often used to accelerate existing processes, including recruitment workflows, performance review drafting, learning content creation, and payroll administration.

There’s a Central HR Challenge in 2026

The central challenge for HR is no longer whether to adopt AI but how to use it productively and responsibly. This report examines how AI is reshaping productivity, decision-making, and risk across core HR domains, and where HR leaders can convert AI capability into sustainable organizational value.

The fast pace of AI change will make this challenge especially daunting. In 2026, the world is moving beyond the age of AI chatbots and into the age of “agentic AI,” meaning AI systems that can achieve complex goals. HR professionals must not only adapt to these new technological capabilities but also strive to proactively capitalize on them to maximize the performance of their organizations.

What this means for CHROs

  • AI will become a defining capability for HR competitiveness. As AI emerges as the top HR priority for many organizations, leaders must determine how it fits into long-term workforce strategy.
  • Automation alone will not create strategic advantage. Many organizations are currently using AI primarily for administrative efficiency, but the greatest value will come from applying AI to workforce insights and decision support.
  • The next phase of AI adoption will focus on workforce intelligence. CHROs who integrate AI into areas such as skills visibility, workforce planning, and talent decision-making will be better positioned to drive organizational performance.

About this Research

This report was created in partnership with Darwinbox, the AI-powered and cloud-based Human Capital Management platform.

The report synthesizes findings from the HR Research Institute’s (HRRI) 2025 State of the Industry research series. HRRI is the research arm of HR.com.

This body of research represents a comprehensive examination of the current Human Resources landscape. The research captures the perspectives of thousands of HR professionals globally, with a primary concentration of respondents located in North America, specifically the United States.

Respondents are primarily HR professionals, ranging from individual contributors to C-suite executives (CHROs, Chief People Officers).

To provide additional context on emerging priorities and artificial intelligence adoption, this report also incorporates insights from two HR Research Institute pulse surveys conducted among HR.com webcast registrants in late 2025 and early 2026.

Finally, the report integrates qualitative insights from a targeted global questionnaire on AI in HR adoption, conducted among HR leaders and practitioners across multiple regions, including India, Europe, and Africa. This questionnaire collected open-ended responses on how organizations are currently using AI in HR.

Major Research Findings

1. AI-driven productivity gains remain concentrated in administrative automation across HR

  • Over three-quarters of HR professionals (77%) who expect to incorporate more AI over the next two years foresee higher productivity, driven primarily by task automation (71%).
  • Far fewer of those HR professionals expect AI to improve workforce planning (41%) or turnover prediction (31%), indicating limited impact on strategic decision-making.
  • For now, AI use in performance management focuses on writing performance reviews (30%) and recommending training (29%).
  • Recruiters are also striving to boost their productivity. Time savings are the most cited benefit of AI in recruitment (70%), followed by process automation (45%) and screening efficiency (36%). Meanwhile, only 19% associate AI with improved hiring decisions, and just 12% link it to fairer hiring outcomes.

About Leaders and Laggards in This Report

Note that throughout this report, we refer to leader and laggard organizations. In most HRRI research reports, we break out respondents into two cohorts based on how well they perform in any given HR functional area.

For example, if HR professional respondents deem their career development programs to be excellent according to one or more outcome variable questions (often based on HRRI Maturity Models), then we consider them to be “career development leader organizations.” Organizations that do not perform as well are considered “career development laggard organizations.”

2. AI adoption in learning, career development, and retention centers on skills development and personalization

  • Among organizations using AI in learning, 83% apply it to content creation and 71% to personalization of learning, with 58% expecting AI to help predict future skill needs.
  • Career-development leader organizations are more likely than their laggard counterparts to use AI to identify and close skills gaps (23% vs. 6%) and to provide personalized career recommendations (17% vs. 2%).
  • The most common expected use of AI for retention is identifying skills gaps and recommending targeted training (53%). Predictive turnover modeling, however, remains limited (23%).

3. AI influences employee experience through workload reduction and communication support

  • Forty-five percent of HR professionals expect AI to support employee well-being through workload reduction.
  • However, a third cite job-loss-related stress as a well-being concern, and 18% report they are uncertain about AI’s impact on employee well-being.
  • Thirty percent of HR professionals use AI to generate employee communication ideas, and 25% use AI to summarize meetings and emails.
  • Communication leader organizations are more likely than laggard organizations to use AI for summarization and synthesis of internal communications (62% vs. 20%).

4. People analytics and governance distinguish AI maturity levels across organizations.

  • AI use in people analytics has expanded quickly, with only 26% reporting no use in 2025, down sharply from 60% in 2024.
  • AI use in people analytics includes generating narratives and summaries (35%) and analyzing unstructured text (30%).
  • People analytics leaders are more likely than laggards to use AI or machine learning platforms (52% vs. 18%).
  • Sixty-two percent believe AI will improve compliance monitoring.
  • Fifty-seven percent indicate the need for new technological competencies, and 51% indicate the need for guardrails and guidelines.

Yet, AI adoption remains fragmented and early-stage

To complement HRRI’s many wide-ranging State of the Industry reports, we also conducted a shorter set of poll questions to explore how organizations are, as of early 2026, using AI in HR operations and where they expect it to deliver value next.

Among 385 respondents, only a quarter report actively using AI in HR, but another 14% are at least piloting AI initiatives. Twenty-two percent say they are considering or planning adoption in 2026, but 39% report no AI use at all, indicating that AI adoption remains in a transitional phase across the HR function as a whole.5

This distribution suggests that AI adoption in HR is still in its earlier stages, with many organizations testing ideas but fewer embedding AI into regular workflows.

AI adoption concentrates in structured, process-driven HR functions

AI adoption is currently strongest in HR functions with structured workflows and measurable efficiency gains. Talent acquisition is the most common application (45%), followed by people analytics (32%), learning and development (28%), and onboarding (23%). Adoption patterns suggest that HR teams prioritize AI in functions where efficiency gains are visible and quantifiable. Talent acquisition naturally lends itself to automation because screening, scheduling, sourcing, and assessment follow repeatable processes.

Automation alone won’t make HR strategic

Finding: HR professionals have high hopes that AI will raise their productivity, largely driven by administrative automation

In The State of Today’s HR Technology Stack and Integrations 2025 research, HRRI asked respondents how they expect their technology to evolve over the next two years. Over a third (38%) stated that they expect to incorporate more artificial intelligence. Of that subgroup of respondents, the primary outcome of that adoption will be higher HR productivity, cited by 77%. These productivity increases seem most likely to be brought about by more task automation, chosen by 71%.

One HR Director in India explicitly noted the role of AI as a jump-start mechanism: “It acts as a starter and then customizing context becomes easy... It's fast, assistive."

In contrast, far fewer expect AI to improve people analytics (57%), workforce planning (41%) or turnover prediction (31%). This is aptly summarized by one UK C-suite executive who said AI is "used to enhance what we already do rather than innovate."6

HR.com’s AI Usage in HR: 2026 Poll

AI use in HR remains limited in scope. After removing “Don’t know” responses, the most commonly reported tasks performed by AI include answering questions without taking action (32%), analyzing HR data to identify risks or trends (28%), and initiating routine HR actions (25%).

More advanced AI capabilities, such as executing multi-step workflows across systems (15%) or maintaining context across employee interactions (9%), remain uncommon.

Notably, 25% of respondents report not knowing what AI systems currently do in their organization, highlighting a visibility and governance gap that could slow adoption.

This highlights a potential danger for the HR department. If organizations prioritize AI uses that reduce HR workload without enhancing analytics, prediction, and decision making, HR could become faster without becoming more influential.

The next phase of AI adoption must focus on:

  • predictive workforce intelligence
  • talent decision support
  • organizational design insights

How AI Is Leveraged

One German C-suite executive states that their company is using AI for “doing research, generating prompt for other AI, simplifying some repetitive tasks, summarizing large documents.”

Some indicate that AI is being used for some end-to-end processes but that these are the exceptions rather than the rule. For example, one HR manager in the Middle East writes, “In the Middle East, particularly Egypt and Gulf regions, HR organizations are adopting AI for multi-step tasks at a moderate level—around 30-40% in larger firms use basic automation, but full loops like end-to-end interview scheduling remain limited to 10-15% of advanced adopters. Most rely on partial AI for screening or reminders, not complete workflows, due to data privacy concerns and integration challenges in SMEs.”

Finding: Recruitment productivity gains outweigh perceived improvements in hiring judgment

In recruitment, the same focus on efficiency appears. Of the respondents who use AI in recruitment, 70% cite time savings as the primary benefit of AI.

AI is currently used to accelerate hiring processes, but its long-term value lies in improving talent quality and workforce capability. However, far fewer report improvements in hiring decisions (19%) or fairness outcomes (12%). This cautious approach likely reflects persistent concerns about bias (58%), depersonalization (51%), and legal risk (50%), which limit AI use in candidate evaluation and selection decisions.7

Organizations that apply AI to talent intelligence and skills matching will gain a competitive advantage in hiring.

AI's Role in Recruitment

An HR manager in the MENA region notes, “I'm currently working on customizing various AI-driven operational commands in my organization. This includes developing an Applicant Tracking System (ATS), an advanced skills level assessment system, and AI tools for reviewing company files to support ISO certification processes.”

Others use it more for analytical and governance purposes. For example, a C-suite level executive in the UK notes, "We use it to analyse engagement surveys, recruitment, analysing sick records. Assist writing policies."

Finding: High-volume recruitment leaders differentiate through AI-enabled engagement, not automated evaluation alone

In high-volume hiring, intent to use AI is widespread, with only a small minority reporting no plans to adopt AI in any way (5%). Chatbots lead intended use (55%), followed by automated screening (53%) and interview scheduling (48%).

High-volume recruitment leaders are more than twice as likely as laggards to use AI-powered chatbots to enhance candidate experience (57% vs. 24%) and to proactively engage candidates through platforms such as LinkedIn (46% vs. 19%). While automated screening is common across maturity levels, leaders extend AI use into interaction-heavy stages that shape candidate perception and conversion.8

The strategic value of AI in recruitment increasingly lies in managing candidate relationships at scale rather than replacing human judgment. Engagement-focused AI improves experience and responsiveness while avoiding many of the legal and ethical risks associated with automated evaluation.

Finding: AI use in performance management concentrates on documentation support rather than ongoing performance enablement

In performance management, planned use of AI concentrates on writing performance reviews (30%) and recommending training (29%). Nearly a third (32%) of organizations report uncertainty about how AI will be used in this domain. Adoption remains limited in the areas of continuous feedback or personalized performance feedback.9

AI helps managers start performance reviews, but does not fix weak feedback cultures. If performance reviews are generated by AI without manager ownership, employee trust in evaluations may decline.

HRRI Strategic recommendations

Based on our research consider the following suggestions:

  • Start with low-risk uses of AI: Begin with routine decisions such as scheduling, prioritizing tasks, or allocating workload before using AI in more complex decision-making areas.
  • Redesign a workflow end to end: When starting out with agentic AI projects, choose a high-volume process such as requisition approval or onboarding and redesign it so AI helps move the work forward automatically. Instead of using AI only to create reports, connect AI recommendations to clear next steps like routing approvals, triggering tasks, or flagging risks.
  • Measure both speed and results: Track efficiency improvements such as faster cycle times or reduced workload, but also measure outcomes such as quality of hire, early turnover, and employee performance improvement.
  • Define decision ownership explicitly: Define when AI provides input, when humans make decisions, and who is responsible for the final outcome.
  • Remove duplicate manual steps: Once AI-supported processes are working reliably, eliminate extra manual steps that slow work down or create confusion about accountability.
  • Use stronger oversight for higher-risk AI tools: Treat tools differently depending on their impact. Tools for scheduling or communication require less oversight than tools used for screening or candidate matching.
  • Check AI tools for bias regularly: Test AI screening and matching tools across different demographic groups using real hiring data. Repeat these checks regularly instead of treating them as a one-time step.

AI adoption grows in learning and career mobility, though maturity varies

Finding: Over 8 in 10 organizations that use AI use it for learning content development

Among organizations using AI in learning, 83% use AI to create content more quickly and 71% use it to personalize learning experiences. In addition, 58% expect AI to predict future skill requirements. Despite this momentum, only 8% of organizations report having a formalized approach to reskilling employees in response to generative AI, while 36% report having no plan to develop one.

Persistent system limitations continue to limit AI use in learning. A quarter cite poor system integration, insufficient customizability, and limited AI functionality as key weaknesses in learning platforms. These constraints reduce HR’s ability to operationalize personalized learning at scale.10

Finding: Career development leaders are several times more likely than laggards to use AI for skills visibility and mobility

More than half of organizations (53%) report no AI use in career development. In contrast, only 23% of career leader organizations indicate they do not use AI for career development. They are over twice as likely as their laggard counterparts to say employees are expected to acquire AI-related skills, and nearly five times as likely to say AI provides real-time feedback and coaching. They are also substantially more likely to use AI to identify and close skills gaps (23% vs. 6%) and to provide personalized career recommendations (17% vs. 2%).11

Leaders tend to treat AI more as a growth and mobility enabler, while laggards remain constrained by disconnected systems and incomplete skills data. The divide reflects differences in organizational readiness and data governance.

HRRI Strategic recommendations

Based on our research consider the following suggestions:

  • Focus learning on building skills, not just delivering courses: Design learning programs around helping employees gain skills they need for future roles and projects. Use AI to track what skills employees develop and suggest how those skills connect to new job opportunities or assignments.
  • Embed AI learning recommendations into daily workflows: Embed AI-powered recommendations into tools employees already use, such as during goal setting, project planning, and career discussions, rather than relying on separate learning portals.
  • Connect learning data to career and job opportunities: Integrate learning data with career frameworks, internal job boards, and talent profiles so employees clearly see how development supports promotions, lateral moves, and assignments.
  • Help managers use AI skill insights during development conversations: Provide managers with simple tools to interpret AI-generated skill data and apply it in development conversations, while keeping final decisions human led.

AI use in retention and well-being focuses on development and workload relief

Finding: Skill development is the most common way organizations expect AI to support retention

When asked how AI could improve employee retention, the most frequently cited use is identifying skills gaps and recommending targeted training (53%), followed by personalized learning and development (49%). Fewer organizations expect AI to support retention through predictive turnover modeling (23%).12

Retention Is Moving from Prediction to Development

Organizations are more comfortable using AI to support growth than to label employees as retention risks. This signals a shift toward development-driven retention strategies. For CHROs, the implication is clear: AI should strengthen career mobility and skill development, not just predict attrition.

HR.com’s AI Usage in HR: 2026 Poll

Most respondents (61%) expect no change in HR headcount due to AI adoption, while 25% expect decreases and 15% expect increases. AI is likely to reshape work by redistributing time away from administrative tasks and toward strategic, relational, and decision-focused activities.

For example, in a response to our separate HR.com questionnaire, an HR manager in Egypt stated, “AI automation of assessments and file reviews for ISO certification has reduced processing time from days to hours, allowing reallocation of HR efforts to strategic initiatives like skills gap analysis.”

In other words, by leveraging AI to help handle time intensive ISO assessments, HR professionals can spend more time engaged in the kinds of skills gaps analyses that may help the entire workforce to become more skilled and productive.

Finding: AI enhances well-being when positioned as augmentation

Organizations increasingly see AI as a way to improve employee well-being by reducing friction in daily work. Workload reduction through automation is the most cited benefit (45%).13

Nearly two-fifths of respondents (39%) believe AI will influence employee experience. Expected improvements are driven by greater automation (50%), enhanced communication (44%), and improved learning and development access (34%).14

Some organizations are leveraging AI to do things such as educating employees about benefit programs. One VP of HR in South Africa wrote that they "leverage an AI chatbot for employees to understand their benefits and respond to basic HR Shared Service queries."

At the same time, 33% cite job-loss-related stress and 18% express uncertainty about AI’s impact. These concerns highlight that outcomes depend on how AI is communicated and governed. When positioned as a tool that augments human capability rather than replaces it, AI can improve efficiency while supporting a positive employee experience.

HRRI Strategic recommendations

Based on our research, consider the following suggestions:

  • Build retention strategies around career and skill growth, not just turnover risk: Use AI to make skill gaps, adjacent roles, and development pathways visible to employees and managers, rather than relying solely on attrition risk indicators that may undermine trust.
  • Use AI insights to strengthen manager-led development conversations: Provide managers with AI-generated information about employee skills, performance trends, and development opportunities during regular check-ins. This helps managers have more informed career discussions while ensuring they remain responsible for coaching, goal setting, and employee growth decisions.
  • Use AI to increase feedback frequency, not replace judgment: Apply AI tools to remind managers and employees to schedule check-ins, summarize feedback from multiple sources, or highlight potential performance gaps. Final performance evaluations and decisions should remain the responsibility of managers to maintain fairness and accountability.
  • Position well-being tools as opt-in support mechanisms: Clearly communicate that AI tools designed to reduce workload or improve work balance are meant to help employees, not monitor them. For example, AI might recommend workload adjustments or suggest wellness resources, but employees should be able to choose whether to use these tools.
  • Measure trust alongside outcomes: Track employee perceptions of fairness, transparency, and psychological safety in parallel with retention, productivity, and performance indicators.
  • Audit AI outputs for privacy and bias risks: Conduct scheduled audits to confirm that AI recommendations do not expose confidential employee information or create unfair outcomes across demographic groups. For example, review whether AI-driven career or performance suggestions produce consistent opportunities across different employee populations.

AI maturity gaps appear in people analytics and governance

Finding: AI use in people analytics is expanding, but many organizations remain limited to descriptive applications

While 26% of organizations report not using AI in people analytics, this represents a substantial improvement from 60% in the previous year. Current AI use is concentrated on generating narratives and summaries (35%) and analyzing unstructured text (30%) rather than advanced predictive modeling (17%).

People analytics leaders, defined as organizations rating their analytics effectiveness as very or extremely effective, are nearly three times more likely than laggards (52% vs. 18%) to use AI or machine learning platforms. This contrast reinforces that AI-enabled analytics delivers value when paired with clear business questions, governance, and decision ownership. The primary divide in people analytics is not access to data but the capability to translate data into forward-looking insights.15

HR.com’s AI Usage in HR: 2026 Poll

When asked about AI’s impact over the next 12–24 months, respondents most frequently identified people analytics (39%), employee experience and engagement (39%), learning and development (36%), and talent acquisition (31%) as the domains most likely to benefit from AI systems.

Finding: AI increases compliance capability but also raises new accountability demands

Of the subgroup of HR respondents who anticipate that their organizations will incorporate AI into their compliance technologies over the next two years, a majority believe AI will help monitor legal compliance (62%).

Of this cohort, 57% also believe AI will require HR professionals to develop new technological competencies and establish new guardrails and guidelines (51%). As AI is increasingly used to support legal and compliance functions, accountability for errors remains human, not automated.16

AI can help manage regulatory complexity, but it also creates new compliance risks. If AI systems miss violations or introduce bias, organizations remain fully liable. Compliance maturity now depends on the ability to audit AI, not just apply policy.

HR.com’s AI Usage in HR: 2026 Poll

For organizations not yet using AI, adoption barriers are primarily related to readiness and risk management. The most frequently cited reasons include evaluating solutions before committing (29%), data privacy or compliance concerns (27%), budget constraints (20%), lack of technical expertise (18%), and concerns about AI inaccuracies (17%).

In a separate qualitative questionnaire, one VP of HR in South Africa pointed to concerns relating to “accuracy, confidentiality and risk.” They also cited “personalisation challenges.”

An HR manager in Egypt cited issues related to budget constraints: “Securing funding and resources for AI in HR has been challenging due to limited budgets in our SME environment, requiring bootstrapping through personal efforts rather than formal allocations.”

HRRI Strategic recommendations

Based on our research, consider the following suggestions:

  • Use AI to improve communication clarity: Apply AI tools to synthesize complex information into clear priorities and action items, especially during periods of change.
  • Make leaders accountable for using analytics in their decisions: Require leaders to document how analytics and AI insights influenced decisions, not just that they were reviewed.
  • Build internal capability to audit AI tools: Train HR and analytics teams to regularly review AI outputs to confirm accuracy, fairness, and consistency over time. Focus audits on areas with higher compliance or legal risk, such as hiring, compensation, or performance management.
  • Create formal governance for all workforce AI systems: Define who owns each AI system, how concerns or errors should be escalated, and how often systems should be reviewed. Establish consistent oversight processes for any AI tools that influence workforce decisions, such as hiring, pay, or performance evaluations.

Regional signals in AI adoption

One of the goals of this research is to gain a better understanding of how HR professionals in different regions of the world view AI. Throughout this report, we have interspersed quotes from these regional respondents.

In this section, we add other insights from various regions of the world. This is a combination of regional breakouts from our various research reports as well as more quotations from our global questionnaire.

The quantitative data was sliced by region to isolate specific sentiment trends. It is important to note that sample sizes (n) vary significantly by module and region. Due to the small sample size, the trends represent "early signals" rather than broad market surveys. In addition, we pull in some data points from secondary sources to balance out our analysis.

India: Scaling efficiency while pursuing personalization

Respondents in India tend to describe AI in HR as supporting high-volume recruitment processes. In HR.com’s Talent Acquisition report, 60% of Indian respondents reported using AI and automation tools, specifically citing chatbots and resume screening to manage applicant volume. This efficiency drive is paired with a strong focus on development: all Indian respondents in the Performance Management report are using AI for continuous performance management and training recommendations.

Together, these responses suggest AI adoption in India is driven primarily by workflow efficiency and operational support, with personalization and improved employee experience emerging gradually. In fact, a staggering 92% of employees in the technology sector use GenAI regularly, the highest rate in the world. Further, 97% of HR leaders in India expect humans to work alongside AI by 2027.

One consultant from India says that senior leadership has been “very supportive” for securing funding and resources for AI in HR.

Africa: Targeted experimentation in performance and well-being

The data from Africa signals a unique focus on holistic employee support. For example, in HR.com’s Career Development report, 57% reported using AI for real-time coaching and feedback. Furthermore, respondents in South Africa and Kenya specifically highlighted the use of AI for "mental health support" and "stress detection," suggesting a readiness to leverage technology for well-being that exceeds trends seen in other regions.

Europe: Compliance-driven augmentation

European respondents tend to describe AI adoption through a compliance and transparency lens. In HR.com’s Talent Acquisition report, 63% said they utilize AI for administrative tasks such as “writing job descriptions,” yet none reported using it for automated decision- making in candidate selection. Similarly, in HR.com’s Legal Compliance report, European respondents prioritized “bias detection” and “guardrails” over predictive analytics.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that Europe is adopting AI cautiously, prioritizing compliance, transparency, and augmentation over automation of decision-making.

Research from OECD reiterates this finding and points to strict laws (like the EU AI Act) and strong privacy concerns as one of the reasons companies are hesitant to roll out AI tools officially. In fact, only 13.5% of European companies have fully adopted AI tools, which is far lower than other regions.

MENA: Strategic precision and quality of hire

Respondents in the Middle East and North Africa indicate a preference for using AI to deepen decision-making rather than just speeding up processes. In HR.com’s Talent Acquisition report, all respondents from the region reported using AI-powered interview software and assessment evaluations, signaling a high trust in AI for vetting candidate quality. This strategic focus extends to performance, where 75% of respondents across the region leverage AI for continuous performance management, moving away from static annual reviews.

Together, these signals suggest that the MENA region is prioritizing quality and precision, using AI to enhance the accuracy of hiring and performance decisions rather than just automating administrative tasks.

Southeast Asia: Holistic growth and employee engagement

Data from Southeast Asia reveals a balanced approach that spans the entire employee lifecycle. All respondents in this region reported using AI for training recommendations, reflecting a strong “growth-first” mindset. Meanwhile, Singapore shows a blend of efficiency and experience, with 50% of respondents utilizing chatbots and interview scheduling to improve candidate engagement.

Other research supports this trend and estimate that nearly one in five organizations in Singapore have deeply embedded AI across their business operations, positioning Singapore as a front-runner in strategic AI use.

Unlike regions focused solely on efficiency, Southeast Asia appears to be adopting AI as a “holistic partner,” integrating it equally into operational workflows and employee development strategies.

United States: Operational efficiency and risk mitigation

Because the research has a primary concentration of respondents in the United States, their data heavily influences the global averages, revealing a dual focus on operational returns and risk management. A respondent from the U.S. noted, "Senior Leadership is excited about AI, but at the same time, they are looking forward to seeing how the investment returns to the organization in terms of operational efficiencies, etc."

This drive for efficiency is mirrored across multiple HR functions. For instance, 80% of U.S. respondents identified "Analytics and forecasting" and "Automation" as the top areas where AI will improve their payroll systems. Similarly, to help achieve strategic business goals over the next two years, an overwhelming 76% of U.S. HR professionals chose to "Increase automation of HR tasks." However, the U.S. is also highly risk-averse compared to its global peers. When examining the outcomes of applying AI to pay equity and transparency, U.S. respondents overwhelmingly flagged negative outcomes, specifically selecting "AI bias" (86%), "Legal risks associated with AI usage" (83%), and "Over-reliance on AI for complex decisions" (83%). Furthermore, the U.S. data uniquely highlights significant anxiety within the workforce; while 68% noted AI's ability to "automate repetitive tasks to reduce stressful workloads" , nearly half (45%) specifically indicated that AI could "lead to greater fear/stress about job loss and obsolete skills".

Some governments are playing a more active role. For instance, AI in the Middle East is poised to be transformative, with the region expected to gain US$320 billion in benefits by 2030 by strategically investing in AI and fostering a culture of innovation.

Conclusion

Across HR functions, AI is moving from experimentation to practical use, guided by human oversight. Organizations are using AI primarily to improve productivity, streamline workflows, and strengthen decision support rather than replace human judgment.

This reflects a clear human-in-the-loop approach. AI supports administrative and analytical work, while humans remain responsible for judgment, ethics, and employee relationships. Organizations are drawing a boundary between efficiency gains and decision accountability.

At the same time, the HR role itself is evolving. Most respondents (61%) expect no change in HR headcount, suggesting AI will reshape work rather than eliminate roles. As automation reduces administrative burden, expectations rise for HR professionals to develop technological fluency (57%), establish governance guardrails (51%), and translate analytics into business insight. HR’s value shifts from transaction processing to decision stewardship, workforce strategy, and ethical oversight.

The next phase of AI adoption in HR will depend less on automation levels and more on how effectively organizations integrate AI into decision processes while maintaining accountability and trust. Organizations that treat AI as augmentation rather than replacement will be better positioned to strengthen both performance and employee confidence.

The future of AI in HR is responsible co-existence. As capabilities expand, HR’s leadership responsibility expands with them.
