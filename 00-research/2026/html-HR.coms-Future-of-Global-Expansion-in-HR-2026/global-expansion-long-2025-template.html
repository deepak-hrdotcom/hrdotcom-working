HR.com’s Future of Global Expansion in HR 2026

Executive Summary

The death of cheap labor

Globalization is no longer driven primarily by low-cost labor. According to “HR.com’s Future of Global Expansion in HR 2026” research, organizations now prioritize accessing new customer markets (77%) over sourcing cheaper talent (23%). The focus has shifted toward resilience, agility, customer proximity, and specialized talent.

Diversifying beyond borders

To reduce supply chain risk and market dependence, organizations are expanding across multiple regions. Many have strengthened their presence in the European Union while increasing investments in East and Southeast Asia.

The friction of scale

Expansion brings growing complexity. More than half of organizations cite local labor law compliance as their biggest challenge, alongside payroll administration and managing distributed teams. This is increasing the need for stronger governance, integrated systems, and localized HR strategies.

Tech-enabled and human-led

Organizations are adopting EOR providers, global HRIS platforms, automated onboarding, and AI-driven analytics to support growth. However, experts emphasize that technology cannot replace local expertise and human judgment.

The road ahead for high growth and tight talent

Global hiring and international investments are expected to continue rising, while talent shortages in engineering and R&D remain a major concern. Leaders must build scalable, resilient workforce strategies to support global growth.

This report explores the workforce, technology, compliance, and operational challenges shaping global expansion and provides practical recommendations for building future-ready organizations.

About the Survey

“HR.com’s Future of Global Expansion in HR 2026” report combines insights from four research sources to examine how organizations are approaching international hiring, workforce expansion, compliance, workforce technology, and global workforce management.

  • The primary research source is “HR.com’s Future of Global Expansion in HR 2026” survey, which ran from February 2026 to May 2026. We gathered responses from 68 HR professionals across numerous industry verticals. Respondents are located around the world, although most reside in North America, particularly the United States.
  • To provide additional context on global expansion challenges and workforce trends, the report also includes findings from the HR Research Institute pulse survey conducted among 520 HR.com webcast registrants in early 2026.
  • In addition, the report includes a case study based on an interview with Antoine Boquen, Co-Founder & CEO at Remote People, exploring practical lessons, workforce risks, and operational considerations associated with global workforce expansion.
  • Finally, the report incorporates qualitative insights from HR leaders with expertise in global workforce management and international expansion.

Major Research Findings

1. Organizations are shifting global expansion priorities toward growth, resilience, and customer proximity.

  • The primary driver of international expansion is entering new customer markets or sales territories (77%).
  • Nearly three-quarters of internationally operating organizations report having operations within the European Union (73%), while many also operate across East Asia (69%), Southeast Asia (65%), the United Kingdom (65%), the United States (58%), and MENA, Oceania, and South Asia regions (50% each), suggesting that organizations are building a more distributed workforce.
  • Organizations plan to sustain this momentum: nearly three-quarters expect international hiring volumes to increase over the next two years (72%), and a similar proportion expect budgets for global expansion to grow (73%).
  • Organizations most commonly measure expansion success through meeting:
    • talent demand (58%)
    • global retention rates (54%)
    • internal stakeholder satisfaction (54%)
    • budget performance (50%)
  • Two-thirds of organizations report being effective at fostering shared culture across distributed teams.

2. Compliance and workforce governance remain some of the biggest barriers to global expansion.

  • More than half of organizations (54%) identify local labor law compliance as their biggest international expansion challenge.
  • Many also struggle with:
    • legal entity setup costs (42%)
    • international payroll administration (39%)
  • Nearly half of organizations anticipate shortages in engineering and R&D talent (48%), while many also expect shortages in data science, AI, machine learning, cybersecurity, and leadership roles.

3. Organizations are increasingly adopting blended workforce models to support international operations.

  • Seven in ten organizations use owned legal entities or subsidiaries to manage international workers.
  • Others rely on secondment or internal transfer models (39%), Employer of Record (EOR) providers (35%), joint ventures or franchise models (30%), and staffing agencies (30%).
  • When it comes to platforms/tools, nearly three-quarters of organizations managing international workers use global HRIS systems (74%) and two-thirds use local payroll providers (65%). However, 44% still rely on spreadsheets or manual processes.

4. AI adoption is steadily increasing across global workforce management activities.

  • Organizations are increasingly using AI for:
    • employee support chatbots (71%)
    • compensation benchmarking (71%)
    • onboarding automation (64%)
  • More than half expect increased automation in onboarding workflows (52%) and international payroll processing (52%) over the next two years.

Expansion Priorities and Global Operating Strategies

Finding: Entering new customer markets is one of the major drivers of global expansion

Among respondents whose organizations currently operate internationally, entering new customer markets or sales territories emerged as the leading driver of global expansion (77%). In contrast, fewer respondents identified access to lower-cost talent pools (23%) as a primary driver. This suggests that organizations are increasingly viewing global expansion as a long-term growth and resilience strategy rather than primarily a cost-reduction initiative. While labor-cost advantages still matter for some organizations, expansion decisions are now more strongly influenced by customer proximity, operational continuity, access to specialized talent, and the need to build more resilient and geographically distributed operations.

The qualitative findings reinforce this shift. Vivek Khanna, Chief Client Partner at Neeyamo Inc, notes that organizations must “balance revenue expectations with investment,” highlighting the growing need to carefully manage expansion costs alongside growth opportunities.

Similarly, another respondent emphasizes the growing influence of customer proximity and supply-chain considerations on expansion decisions. He explains that organizations are often driven by organic growth—specifically following existing customers and partners into new markets—as well as the need to hire and manage talent for regional supply chains and distribution networks.

The findings also indicate that workforce strategy and customer strategy are becoming increasingly interconnected. Erin Goodey, Director of People Services at Oyster, explains:

“Increasingly, companies hire locally because customers prefer working with people in their region.”

These findings align with broader research from KPMG International and Global Trade Magazine, which similarly highlight a shift away from purely lowest-cost globalization models toward approaches focused on resilience, agility, nearshoring, customer proximity, and operational continuity.

Finding: Nearly three-quarters of internationally operating organizations have a presence in EU markets

Among respondents whose organizations currently operate internationally, the most common regions of operation include the European Union member states (73%), East Asia (69%), Southeast Asia (65%), the United Kingdom (65%), and the United States (58%). The findings suggest that organizations are spreading operations across a wider range of regions to improve resilience, access specialized talent, strengthen supply chains, and reduce dependence on any single market. Expanding across both mature and emerging markets also helps organizations stay closer to customers while improving operational flexibility.

Research from DocShipper and Global Trade Magazine similarly highlights the continued growth of “China Plus One” strategies, with organizations increasing investment across Southeast Asia, India, Mexico, and other regional hubs to strengthen resilience and reduce geographic concentration risk.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Target markets based on customer demand. Choose expansion locations to get closer to new sales territories and customers, rather than relying strictly on finding cheaper labor. For instance, instead of just filtering for countries with the lowest wages, map out where your distribution partners are located or where your sales team is targeting organic growth, and build your hiring plan around those regions.
  • Balance growth with budget. Require your HR and finance teams to collaborate early to balance revenue expectations against the high investments required for expansion. As a practical step, before launching a job posting in a new country, hold a joint strategy session with the finance team to ensure the projected revenue properly balances against the HR support investments.
  • Diversify to mitigate geographic risk. The data shows a strong push into the EU and emerging markets across Asia. Rather than relying on a single foreign market for talent or production, build your expansion strategy around multiple regional hubs to ensure operational continuity and strengthen supply chain resilience.

Compliance, Operational Complexity, and Workforce Governance

Finding: More than half of organizations identify local labor law compliance as their biggest expansion challenge

Among respondents currently involved in international hiring or expansion activities, complexity of local labor laws and compliance emerged as the most common challenge (54%). Respondents also identified high costs associated with establishing legal entities (42%), international payroll and benefits administration challenges (39%) as major challenges.

Additional HR Research Institute pulse survey findings reinforce the scale of these concerns. Among the 277 respondents whose organizations have considered global expansion, 59% identified local labor laws as a major workforce compliance challenge, followed by payroll and tax requirements (46%), benefits administration (38%), and cultural and language barriers (36%).

Together, the data confirms that international expansion demands more than market opportunity and hiring capacity. It demands stronger governance, cross-functional coordination, and ongoing compliance oversight.

One respondent highlights the growing need for cross-functional coordination when navigating foreign regulations.

“Regulations may be very different outside the U.S. It’s crucial legal teams, HR teams, and finance teams understand at least at a high level what those regulations are and any restrictions they may have on operating there.”

Debbie McGrath, CEO of HR.com, echoes this sentiment, noting that to successfully expand over the next two years, organizations must develop a “deep, localized understanding of both culture and compliance for every region they enter.”

Research from PwC and the European Commission similarly highlights growing pressure on organizations to strengthen supply chain governance, regulatory coordination, ESG reporting, and compliance transparency across increasingly distributed operations.

Finding: More than one-third describe their global expansion function as world-class

Among respondents evaluating the maturity of their global expansion function, 35% describe it as world-class, while another 23% describe it as strategic.

At the same time, operational complexity remains a challenge for many organizations. Among respondents whose organizations currently operate internationally, 39% report that adding a new country creates a moderate degree of operational difficulty, while nearly a quarter report a high or very high degree of difficulty.

The findings suggest that although many organizations are building more mature global expansion capabilities, expanding into new regions still creates operational pressure related to onboarding, payroll setup, workforce coordination, systems integration, legal compliance, and process standardization.

Vivek Khanna emphasizes the importance of moving away from reactive expansion approaches, advising organizations to build a deliberate “strategy and plan rather than a reactive knee-jerk reaction.”

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Prepare for compliance challenges before entering new markets. Local labor laws and compliance remain the biggest challenge for international expansion. Conduct a comprehensive review of local employment regulations before budgeting for a new region. For example, have your legal team explicitly identify hidden compliance factors, such as mandatory 13th-month salary payments or restrictive termination limits for fixed-term employees.
  • Simplify global HR operations. Payroll setup, onboarding, and entity management can quickly become difficult as organizations expand into more regions. Standardize core processes, improve system integration, and reduce manual administrative work where possible. One way to do this is to document the specific tax, payroll, and regulatory requirements for every new region into a central playbook to ensure consistent governance.
  • Improve coordination across teams and regions. International expansion requires stronger collaboration across HR, legal, payroll, finance, and operations teams. Clarify responsibilities, improve communication, and create shared processes across regions.
  • Take a more proactive approach to expansion planning. Organizations with more mature global expansion functions appear better prepared to manage operational complexity. Use workforce planning, operational readiness assessments, and long-term expansion strategies before entering new markets.
  • Assess infrastructure before launching. Conduct a formal workforce readiness assessment to ensure your core HR processes can handle regional growth before entering a new market. Ensure that a formal assessment evaluating core HR infrastructure scalability is completed and approved by leadership before initiating operations.

Workforce Models, EOR Ecosystems, and Workforce Technology Infrastructure

Finding: Seven in ten organizations use their own legal entities to manage international workers

Among respondents whose organizations currently employ international workers, the most commonly used workforce model involves establishing owned legal entities or subsidiaries, used by 70% of respondents.

However, over a third of organizations also report using secondment or internal transfer models (39%) and Employer of Record (EOR) providers (35%). Research from The HR Digest and Forbes similarly highlights the growing adoption of blended workforce models, focusing heavily on the rise of fractional leadership and hybrid team structures designed to improve organizational agility and resilience.

The findings also suggest organizations increasingly view contractors, EORs, and flexible workforce arrangements as tools for faster market entry and greater operational agility. However, Erin Goodey, Director of People Services at Oyster, also warns that poorly managed contractor relationships can increase worker-classification risk and regulatory exposure.

Respondents also emphasize that the “successful global expansion” workforce model often depends on organizational size, expansion stage, and compliance risk.

One respondent explains that successful global expansion requires a tailored approach, as “there is no one way, and the right way looks different for different organizations and circumstances.” He further notes that workforce model decisions must evolve as organizations scale, pointing out that while an Employer of Record (EOR) is sometimes the most cost-efficient choice, higher headcount scenarios may demand alternative solutions.

Brent Skinner, Executive Community Lead at HR.com, explains that the choice of workforce model is heavily intertwined with risk mitigation and institutional capability. He notes that while large enterprises with deep institutional knowledge of local labor laws may “choose to 'go it alone' instead of turning to, for example, an EOR,” smaller organizations just embarking on global growth “are apt to need and find relief in the powerful risk mitigation that an EOR has to offer.”

Finding: More than half of organizations prioritize technology/user interface quality when selecting EOR providers

Among respondents currently using EOR platforms, the most important selection criteria include quality of the technology platform and user experience (56%), pricing transparency (44%), data privacy and security protections (44%), and guaranteed compliance or indemnification (44%).

Erin Goodey emphasizes that organizations must evaluate EOR partnerships through the lens of employee experience, noting that “the risk that gets underestimated most is the human one.” She further explains that selecting partners who treat the employee experience as a “genuine standard of care” rather than a mere checkbox is one of the most critical, yet underrated, risk management decisions an organization can make.

The findings also indicate that hybrid delivery models are becoming more common across EOR ecosystems. Among respondents using EOR providers, 67% report using hybrid delivery models involving both owned entities and local sub-vendors, while only 11% rely entirely on aggregator-only partner models.

This suggests organizations increasingly recognize that managing international workforces often requires balancing centralized governance with local expertise and regional operational support.

Finding: Nearly three-quarters of organizations managing international workers use global HRIS systems

Among respondents currently managing international workers, 74% use global HRIS systems, 65% use local payroll providers, 57% use expense management tools, 48% use collaboration software, and 44% use unified global payroll platforms.

However, 44% of respondents still rely on spreadsheets or manual administrative processes. This suggests many organizations are still moving from fragmented workflows toward more integrated workforce systems.

When asked to describe their overarching technology infrastructure for this international workforce, 44% report using an integrated hybrid model with automation between domestic HRIS systems and global workforce platforms. In comparison, 22% rely mainly on provider-led platforms offered by EOR or global workforce partners, while 17% use a domestic-first approach by adapting HQ HR systems to support international workers. Only 9% report relying primarily on fragmented or manual processes such as spreadsheets and email. The findings suggest organizations are placing greater importance on system integration, automation, and workforce visibility as global operations become more complex.

As Erin Goodey explains:

“Technology has transformed the operational layer... making it possible for lean teams to manage multi-country hiring at a scale that wasn’t achievable five years ago.”

At the same time, respondents consistently emphasize that technology alone cannot replace human expertise, local market knowledge, and workforce judgment. As organizations scale internationally, balancing automation with regional expertise remains critical.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Implement a blended workforce model. Use a mix of owned entities, Employer of Record (EOR) providers, internal transfers, and contractors depending on your specific expansion stage and compliance risk. As an illustration, you could use your own legal entities for established markets, while contracting with EOR providers to test the waters quickly in emerging regions.
  • Vet vendors on user experience. Select EOR and global workforce partners based on the quality of their technology platform and how well they treat the employee experience, rather than just basic compliance. When watching vendor demos, actively evaluate the platform based on its ease of use for the end-employee to ensure they do not feel like a secondary priority.
  • Eliminate manual spreadsheets. Transition your workforce tracking from fragmented manual processes to integrated global HRIS and unified payroll systems. A concrete move is to implement an integrated global HRIS platform that automatically syncs data between your domestic headquarters and international hubs.
  • Audit contractor classifications. Actively monitor your contractor and EOR relationships to avoid misclassification risks and regulatory exposure as your international headcount grows. Run routine audits on your global contractor arrangements to ensure compliance.

AI, Automation, and Future Workforce Capability

Finding: One-fifth of organizations report very high AI adoption in global workforce operations

Among respondents currently operating internationally, organizations report growing adoption of AI to support global workforce management. Although 24% report not using AI at all in global workforce operations and 10% are unsure, many organizations already report meaningful adoption, including 29% using AI to a high or very high degree.

The findings suggest AI adoption is steadily increasing, although organizations remain at different stages of maturity.

Among respondents already using AI for global workforce management, the most common use cases include chatbots and employee support (71%), compensation benchmarking and analysis (71%), and onboarding workflow automation (64%).

At the same time, respondents consistently emphasize that AI works best when combined with human expertise and local workforce knowledge.

Vivek Khanna explains:

“Availability of information, creation of business plans are enabled through AI. On-ground knowledge is still critical and requires humans to fulfill.”

Another respondent adds that while AI and technology are leveling the playing field by automating work previously done by lower-cost labor, this shift creates a new challenge: “organizations must now focus their hiring strategies on finding the specific talent capable of leveraging that technology.”

Similarly, Erin Goodey highlights the growing importance of workforce intelligence and data-driven hiring decisions:

“Companies that treat global hiring as a data problem get better at it faster than those that don’t.”

While tools are emerging to aid in this area, true global compliance monitoring remains a formidable challenge. Evaluating the current technology landscape, Debbie McGrath candidly notes she is “not sure anyone has the ability to track labor compliance globally yet so not sure if this is a risk.”

Research from SAP, Deloitte, and Forbes similarly highlights growing adoption of AI-driven analytics, automated workflows, and agentic AI systems across global operations.

Finding: More than half of organizations expect to increase automation in payroll and onboarding workflows

Among respondents evaluating future automation priorities, 52% expect increased automation in both international payroll processing and onboarding workflows. Organizations also expect increased automation across benefits administration (48%), expense reimbursements (44%), and time-tracking or workforce administration processes (44%).

The findings suggest organizations increasingly recognize that manual administrative work can limit operational responsiveness as global operations grow more complex.

At the same time, respondents consistently emphasize that automation alone is not enough. Workforce judgment, local expertise, and human oversight remain critical when managing compliance, employee experience, and regional workforce challenges.

Erin Goodey explains that the real differentiator in 2026 isn’t who has the best software, but rather the ability to combine digital precision with human experts who “genuinely understand employment law, local cultures, and the human stakes involved.”

The findings suggest organizations are moving toward more balanced workforce models in which automation improves efficiency and consistency while human expertise continues to guide workforce decisions, compliance management, and employee support across regions.

Adding to this, Brent Skinner emphasizes that successfully expanding globally requires “a command of artificial intelligence's role in global HCM and payroll.” However, he cautions that achieving this command requires “deep preparation beginning with an unflinching examination of internal processes to understand how the organization's own HCM ecosystem operates in fact, not just on paper.”

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Focus AI investments on high-impact workforce processes. Organizations are increasingly using AI to improve onboarding, employee support, compliance monitoring, and workforce analytics. Prioritize AI initiatives that reduce administrative burden while improving employee experience.
  • Mandate human oversight for AI. Ensure that AI applications are always paired with local HR professionals who understand regional cultures, employment laws, and the human stakes involved. As a safeguard, if you use AI to draft employment contracts, mandate that a local legal expert reviews the final output to catch cultural nuances or legal constraints.
  • Establish clear AI governance. Data privacy and security were already identified as major challenges by over a quarter of expanding organizations. Scaling AI across borders amplifies this vulnerability. Before deploying AI tools globally, document strict organizational guidelines covering data privacy, accountability, and human review protocols.
  • Build AI readiness across HR and leadership teams. AI is beginning to reshape workforce planning, hiring, and operational strategy. Invest heavily in teaching your HR leaders and workforce how to use AI tools effectively, as proficiency with these tools is becoming a major hiring differentiator. You might invest in formal AI literacy workshops and change management programs so leaders can utilize these tools responsibly.

Building Resilient and Future-Ready Global Workforces

Finding: Nearly three-quarters of organizations expect international hiring to increase over the next two years

Thirty-six percent of respondents expect the volume of international hires to increase a little over the next two years, while another 36% expect it to increase a lot. Similarly, 50% expect budgets for global expansion to increase a little and 23% expect them to increase a lot. Use of EOR providers also appears likely to grow, with 18% expecting usage to increase a little and 32% expecting it to increase a lot. This suggests organizations continue to prioritize global expansion despite growing operational complexity and economic uncertainty.

Finding: Nearly half of organizations anticipate shortages in engineering and R&D talent

Among respondents currently operating internationally, the most anticipated future talent shortages include Engineering and R&D talent (48%), Data Science, AI, and Machine Learning talent (44%), Cybersecurity and IT security talent (35%), and Executive leadership/management roles (35%). Research from Deloitte similarly highlights growing pressure on organizations to rethink strategic talent sourcing, experiential capability development, and early-career pipelines as traditional talent pools continue to tighten. Therefore, organizations may increasingly rely on global expansion to access specialized talent that may not be available locally.

Vivek Khanna summarizes the issue directly: “Talent is always the most critical.”

Erin Goodey also emphasizes how changing immigration conditions and AI-driven skill shortages are reshaping global talent strategy:

“The organizations that respond by building genuine capability to hire and retain talent in place—wherever that talent sits—will have a durable structural advantage.”

Overall, the findings suggest future workforce competitiveness will increasingly depend on organizations’ ability to build globally connected talent strategies rather than relying primarily on domestic labor markets.

Finding: Two-thirds of organizations report being effective at fostering culture across distributed global teams

Among respondents whose organizations currently operate internationally, 40% report being very effective at fostering shared company culture across distributed teams, while another 28% report being effective.

Notably, among respondents currently planning international expansion, 100% identified developing cultural integration and onboarding plans as their highest operational priority, reinforcing the growing importance of workforce integration during expansion.

Other research highlights growing concerns around culture fragmentation, mentorship erosion, and workforce cohesion across globally distributed and hybrid work environments.

The qualitative findings strongly support this trend. Erin Goodey emphasizes the importance of delivering a consistent employee experience across regions:

“Employees in Lagos, Manila, London, and New York should all feel equally supported when it comes to benefits, HR guidance, and overall employee experience.”

She also highlights the need for intentional workforce integration across distributed teams:

“Building trust, shared norms, and genuine belonging across time zones and cultures requires deliberate investment.”

Debbie McGrath, CEO of HR.com, reinforces that the true measure of a successful global expansion goes beyond operational metrics, defining it as “having productive, compliant employees who have a strong sense of belonging.” When asked for the single most important change organizations should make to improve their approach, McGrath emphasizes:

“Belonging. Making sure all employees understand that they are valued and belong.”

Brent Skinner expands on this definition of success, noting that a truly future-ready global strategy results in “an organization capable of absorbing and blending different cultures into an assimilated, single employer culture.” He adds that ultimate success requires “flexibility in the face of complexity and a continual, built-in, effective responsiveness to uncertainty and change.”

Finding: More than half of organizations measure global expansion success through talent and retention outcomes

Among respondents currently operating internationally, the most common measures of global expansion success include meeting talent demand for global roles (58%), global retention rates (54%), internal stakeholder satisfaction (54%), and budget versus actual spend variance (50%).

The qualitative findings reinforce this shift. Erin Goodey notes that true success requires achieving “compliance integrity without creating operational drag,” allowing organizations to hire confidently in new markets.

Similarly, another respondent highlights the growing importance of scalability and workforce flexibility in expansion success:

“A cost effective, scalable, compliant and flexible operating model for international expansion wins.”

Overall, the findings suggest organizations increasingly recognize that workforce stability, operational agility, employee experience, and compliance maturity directly influence long-term expansion success.

Finding: Half of organizations cite compliance, geopolitical instability, and distributed workforce management as major expansion risks

Among respondents considering global expansion, the most commonly identified risks include inability to guarantee legal and tax compliance (50%), political or economic instability in target regions (50%), and complexities associated with managing distributed teams across time zones (50%).

Vivek Khanna highlights the growing impact of regulatory changes on global workforce models over the next two to three years, predicting that “shifting global regulations and governments' increasing awareness of Employer of Record (EOR) models” will significantly disrupt how organizations approach expansion.

Similarly, another respondent emphasizes the increasing influence of tariffs and geopolitical shifts on expansion decisions:

“I think trade alliances and tariffs will continue to play a significant role in where companies expand and where they partner.”

Highlighting the human impact of these geopolitical shifts, Debbie McGrath predicts that one of the most significant upcoming disruptions to global expansion strategies will be “Political instability... people will look for safer places to live where they are valued.”

Brent Skinner also points to “spreading global instability” as a primary risk. He warns that regions with fluid and unstable political leadership “can see drastic shifts in their regulatory framework governing how to employ people,” which can severely disrupt operations and necessitate “closer relationships with partners versed in managing uncertainty.”

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Expand talent strategies beyond local labor markets. Many organizations expect growing shortages in technical and leadership talent. To address this, launch targeted international recruiting campaigns specifically designed to source these highly specialized professionals in alternative markets.
  • Use broader workforce and operational measures to evaluate expansion success. Organizations increasingly recognize that long-term expansion success depends on more than financial growth alone. Track workforce stability, employee experience, operational agility, retention, and compliance performance alongside traditional business metrics.
  • Standardize benefits to ensure fairness. Build equitable benefits structures across all regions so employees in lower-cost areas feel equally supported. An actionable way to handle this is to audit your global total rewards package to guarantee that workers in diverse regions receive meaningful, equitable benefits comparable to headquarters.
  • Invest in leadership and workforce capability development. Organizations increasingly need leaders who can manage distributed teams, cross-cultural collaboration, operational complexity, and AI-enabled work environments. Expand leadership development, reskilling, and cross-functional learning programs to prepare employees for future workforce demands.
  • Prioritize workforce culture and employee experience across regions. As organizations expand globally, inconsistent employee experiences can weaken trust, retention, and workforce cohesion. Standardize core employee support practices while adapting onboarding, communication, and engagement strategies to regional needs. An example of this in action is creating a formal policy that prevents employees in regions like Asia from consistently having to work overnight shifts to accommodate U.S. meetings (a critical employee experience challenge that is explored further in the Remote People case study)
  • Strengthen workforce coordination across distributed teams. Managing employees across time zones, cultures, and regions requires stronger communication and collaboration practices. Train managers to lead distributed teams effectively and create clearer processes for cross-regional coordination.

Case Study: Remote People – Redefining Global HR Expansion

Company background

Founded in 2018 as New Horizons Global Partners, Remote People is a global HR solutions provider managing roughly $500 million in salary payments, supporting nearly 7,000 workers across almost 180 countries, and operating legal entities in 70.

As Antoine Boquen, Co-Founder & CEO at Remote People, explained, “We built the foundations of the company on outputs, not the number of hours.”

“When you hire a person, I will not look at where the person is based. I will look at the skills and outputs.”

The challenge of global expansion

For U.S. companies expanding internationally, especially into Asia, fragmented HR operations built around multiple local vendors lead to:

  • slow communication due to time zone differences
  • limited payroll visibility
  • inconsistent service quality across countries
  • difficulty managing local laws and compliance

Many also strictly rely on the Employer of Record (EOR) model, which can potentially become restrictive at scale and create permanent establishment risks. Payroll is rarely the hardest part. It’s labor regulations, terminations, compliance and even culture that are the tough parts.

“Payroll itself is easy. The difficulty is understanding HR situations on the ground and preventing problems before they happen,” Boquen said.

“Customers are OK paying a premium to work with somebody who knows what they’re doing because it becomes an extension of their in-house HR team.”

The solution: A guidance-focused HR model

Rather than acting only as a payroll provider, Remote People positions itself as a global HR advisor. Depending on need, it may recommend:

  • An Employer of Record (EOR) solution
  • A Contractor of Record model
  • A lightweight local subsidiary with payroll support

Its local expertise helps manage:

  • Employment contracts
  • Maternity leave requirements
  • Workplace accidents
  • Employee terminations
  • Country-specific labor compliance

Boquen stated, “The expertise and the navigation are critical. If a provider only offers one solution, they are going to sell you that solution whether it fits your business or not.”

Key lessons for organizations expanding globally

1. Hire local leaders

Organizations tend to succeed more often when they hire local leaders instead of sending expatriates. Local hires better understand culture, business practices, and workforce dynamics.

“It’s much more successful to hire local people in each country rather than send Americans everywhere,” Boquen noted.

“If you can hire a British person in the UK, a German in Germany, a French person in France, and a Chinese person in China, you will have more opportunities to build a successful business.”

2. Understand labor laws before budgeting

Many underestimate local employment regulations. For example:

  • Countries such as Indonesia, Italy, and Spain often require 13th- and 14th-month salary payments.
  • Certain international labor laws make it difficult to terminate fixed-term employees immediately.

Without planning, these obligations create surprises.

3. Use background checks in remote hiring

Remote hiring fraud is rising, including credential misrepresentation and AI-assisted interviews. Remote People recommends low-cost background checks to verify:

  • Education
  • Employment history
  • Termination records
  • Identity

“It’s very important to do this background check. It costs $15, but when you have one bad apple, you actually pay much more than $15,” Boquen said.

4. Build fairness across global teams

Treating employees fairly regardless of location requires:

  • Creating equitable benefits structures
  • Establishing clear cross-time-zone collaboration guidelines
  • Avoiding unfair scheduling practices for employees in certain regions

Employees in Asia should not consistently work overnight, and workers in lower-cost regions deserve meaningful benefits comparable to those elsewhere.

“People may feel it’s unfair because they have to work every night to connect with their U.S. colleagues, but it’s never the opposite,” Boquen explained. “You need to offer the same benefits structure worldwide, otherwise people eventually feel they are treated unfairly.”

AI and the changing workforce

AI is reshaping workforce structures and reducing demand for junior and administrative roles, especially in market research, lead generation, and routine operations.

“AI is replacing many junior and entry-level positions because you can now do the same work using ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude,” according to Boquen.

On the other hand, employers increasingly value people who use AI well.

“As an organization, I will hire the person who is great at using AI tools rather than the person who does not want to use them.”

The future of global HR

One emerging model is “multi-employment”: professionals work for multiple non-competing organizations while keeping full-time benefits.

In this model:

  • Employees focus on outputs rather than schedules.
  • Income from multiple employers can be consolidated.
  • Workers keep benefits and protections.

As Boquen described, “In the future, more people will stop working based on hours and will instead be paid for outputs.”

He further predicted, “The future model will involve people being full-time employed while working for multiple non-competing companies.”

Conclusion

Global expansion is fundamentally shifting away from simply chasing the lowest labor costs. Today, it is about building resilient, scalable, and globally connected workforce ecosystems. Organizations are expanding internationally primarily to access new customer markets, diversify operations, and secure specialized talent.

However, managing a globally distributed workforce is becoming significantly more operationally complex. To navigate this landscape successfully, organizations must focus on three critical pillars:

  • Operational Maturity: Stricter compliance requirements, growing talent shortages, and the logistics of managing distributed teams are increasing the pressure on HR leaders to strengthen governance and strategic workforce planning.
  • Technological Agility: The adoption of AI, workflow automation, integrated HRIS platforms, and flexible workforce models are now essential tools for supporting rapid scale and operational agility.
  • Human-Centric Leadership: Despite rapid technological advancements, long-term expansion success still heavily relies on local market expertise, strong leadership, and fostering genuine workforce trust and employee experience across regions.

Ultimately, global workforce management is no longer just an administrative necessity; it has evolved into a core strategic capability. Organizations that can effectively balance scalable technology with region-specific human expertise and workforce resilience will be the best positioned to compete in an increasingly distributed global environment.
