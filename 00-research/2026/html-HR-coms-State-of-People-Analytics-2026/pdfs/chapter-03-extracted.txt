 Structural and behavioral barriers continue limiting impact
 The final Delphi round also asks experts how organizations should
 build this capability. Rather than recommending large-scale enterprise
transformation, experts favor a more focused and practical approach.
 Twenty-nine percent recommend concentrating first on solving a small
 number of major business problems, such as turnover or hiring, while
21% support building specialized centralized people analytics teams.
Another 21% advocate combining centralized expertise with targeted
business problem-solving.
The advisory board discussions reinforce this conclusion. Helen
 Friedman, Partner and Chief Executive Officer at Merit Analytics Group
 LLC, argues that organizations often focus too heavily on analytics
themselves while overlooking change management and leadership
adoption.   She notes that leaders frequently resist findings that challenge
personal perspectives or existing practices.
 Similarly, Shane McGowan, People Analytics Business Partner at
 LifeStance Health, explains that HR analytics often lacks the operational
 visibility routinely given to finance and sales functions.
This evolution points to a clear structural direction in the final survey
round. When forced to define the single primary role of the people
 analytics function over the next two years, 43% of experts select
Strategic Advisor (guiding executive strategy with workforce insights
and research). The remainder of the panel is evenly split at 29% each
 between acting as a Data Integrator (connecting HR, finance, and
 operations systems) and an Embedded Partner (delivering real-time
insights straight to managers and teams).

 HRRI Strategic Recommendations
 Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:
 1. Embed analytics into operational routines.   Organizations should involve analytics teams earlier in business
planning cycles rather than after decisions have already been made. Include people analytics teams in:
 ●   workforce planning discussions
 ●   operational review meetings
 ●   quarterly business reviews
 ●   succession planning conversations
 2. Start with business problems rather than reporting requests.   Analytics efforts create greater value when
they focus on solving operational and workforce challenges rather than producing additional dashboards.
Encourage analytics teams to frame projects around operational questions such as:
 ●   Which teams face the highest turnover risk?
 ●   Where are capability gaps slowing execution?
 ●   Which leadership behaviors improve retention?
 3. Create accountability for action.   This helps close the execution gap identified throughout the study. Many
 organizations generate insights but fail to establish ownership for follow-through. Consider:
 ●   assigning decision owners for major findings
 ●   requiring action plans tied to analytics insights
 ●   reviewing workforce actions during leadership meetings
 4. Build analytics fluency across leadership teams.   The goal is to make analytics part of everyday operational
thinking rather than a specialist activity. Organizations can strengthen adoption by:
 ●   training HR professionals and leaders on analytics interpretation
 ●   creating simple business-focused storytelling templates
 ●   developing shared definitions for key metrics
 ●   teaching leaders how to use analytics during planning discussions
 5. Measure business outcomes, not reporting activity.   This reinforces the idea that analytics exists to improve
decisions, not simply increase reporting volume. Evaluate people analytics based on outcomes such as:
 ●   turnover reduction
 ●   productivity improvement
 ●   stronger internal mobility
 ●   leadership effectiveness
 ●   hiring efficiency
