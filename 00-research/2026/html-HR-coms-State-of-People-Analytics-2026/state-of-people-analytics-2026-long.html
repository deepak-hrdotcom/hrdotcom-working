HR.com’s State of People Analytics 2026

Executive Summary

Organizations have access to more workforce data than ever, yet many still struggle to turn that information into meaningful business action. Findings from HR.com’s State of People Analytics 2026 report, informed by a three-round Delphi study and advisory board discussions with senior people analytics practitioners and industry experts, reveal a persistent execution gap. When asked which single barrier would unlock the greatest organizational impact if completely solved, 57% of experts identify this execution gap as the primary challenge facing the field.

The findings suggest that organizations no longer struggle primarily with data access or reporting capability. Instead, the larger challenge is decision activation, which refers to partnering directly with business leaders to turn workforce insights into concrete action plans, operational changes, and measurable follow-through. Half of the experts view this as the key to closing the execution gap.

The study also shows that people analytics increasingly gains influence through business relevance and operational integration rather than technical sophistication alone. Experts define high-impact analytics teams as those embedded into routine leadership and operational planning discussions, where workforce insights directly shape business decisions. At the same time, 50% of experts state that the biggest barrier keeping analytics teams out of operational planning meetings is lack of relevance, meaning workforce metrics are not tied closely enough to financial or operational goals.

The findings also reveal growing caution around large-scale AI expansion. While AI is expected to enhance analytics, experts stress the continued importance of human judgment and context. With limited resources, 92% recommend piloting AI in targeted use cases rather than scaling broadly.

Ultimately, 43% of experts conclude that the primary role of the people analytics function over the next two years must evolve into that of a Strategic Advisor that guides executive strategy with workforce insights and research. Future competitive advantage will depend more on helping organizations act effectively on the information they already have.

We examine these and other insights in our summary of key findings below.

Our Major Research Findings

1. The execution gap is the primary hurdle for people analytics.

  • When asked which single barrier would unlock the greatest organizational impact if completely solved first, 57% of experts selected the execution gap, defined as insights being produced but not translated into action.
  • Furthermore, experts cited fragmented systems (58%) and a lack of ownership (42%) as the most frequent points of process breakdown.

2. Business relevance is the new credibility.

  • The biggest barrier keeping analytics teams out of operational planning meetings is lack of relevance, with 50% of experts stating that workforce metrics are simply not tied closely enough to financial or operational goals.
  • In Round 2 choices, 58% of experts prioritize improving the quality of hire and time to productivity, and 58% prioritize building future skills and workforce capability, to demonstrate maximum business value.
  • When forced to choose a single outcome in Round 3, 35% of experts prioritize improving leadership effectiveness, which outranks workforce productivity (29%) and future skills building (29%).

3. High impact teams are embedded into operational routines.

  • In Round 2, experts generate a three-way statistical tie at 58%, identifying high-impact people analytics functions as those that:
    • embed analytics into routine leadership and operational planning meetings
    • start analytics efforts with specific business problems
    • measure success through business impact and follow-through rather than report usage
  • Given limited budgets and headcount, 75% of respondents state that organizations must aggressively scale the embedding of analytics into routine business decision-making.

4. Business acumen overtakes technical capability to close the execution gap.

  • To close the execution gap over the next two years, 67% of experts select decision activation, which means partnering directly with business leaders to turn insights into action plans, and 58% select business acumen, which means understanding operational priorities and linking analytics to financial outcomes.
  • These capabilities outrank technical or baseline proficiencies in the Round 2 data, including data quality and governance (50%), data storytelling (42%), data integration (42%), and predictive analytics (33%).

5. Artificial intelligence acts as an accelerator but requires human judgment.

  • AI is poised to revolutionize the function, with experts split evenly on its greatest long-term value, as 36% point to automating tedious data preparation while another 36% favor democratization, which means allowing managers to access and use workforce data without relying on analysts.
  • However, human judgment is more vital than ever. Experts cite over-reliance on AI without applying human judgment as the single greatest risk (50%). To counter this risk, 43% select decision accountability, which requires leaders to justify major AI-assisted decisions, as the single best safeguard.
  • To manage this transition safely, 92% of experts recommend piloting new AI tools in small areas rather than aggressively rolling them out enterprise wide.

6. The function must embrace the role of Strategic Advisor.

  • In the final round, 43% of experts conclude that the primary role of the people analytics function over the next two years is a ‘Strategic Advisor’ that guides executive strategy with workforce insights and research. This outranks the roles of Data Integrator (29%) and Embedded Partner (29%).

Methodology

The Delphi Approach

This report’s findings are based on a three-round Delphi study conducted to evaluate and achieve expert consensus on the future state of people analytics. The Delphi method is an iterative research framework that allows a panel of subject matter experts to collaboratively refine their perspectives over multiple survey rounds. Responses are summarized after each round to identify shared themes allowing the panel to progressively move toward a consensus view on industry priorities and challenges. Because this Delphi study targeted highly experienced, senior practitioners rather than entry-level personnel, the data represents a high-level qualitative alignment of industry leaders rather than a broad statistical survey.

Expert Panel Composition

The study relied on the professional judgment, observations, and informed viewpoints of a specialized expert panel comprising 18 distinct contributors. The panel captured diverse voices across the HR and analytics spectrum, ensuring a balanced evaluation of both technical and operational applications.

The participant pool included strategic leaders (CEOs, Founders, Managing Partners, and VPs of People Analytics) as well as hands-on practitioners (Senior Data Analytics Strategists, Directors of Data Engineering, and Workforce Planning Consultants). Experts represented a wide range of organizations, spanning large enterprise brands to specialized consulting and HR technology firms.

While the total pool consisted of 18 experts, participation varied slightly across the iterative phases as the consensus narrowed: 16 experts contributed to Round 1, 12 experts completed Round 2, and 14 experts finalized the consensus in Round 3.

Advisory Board Insights

In addition to the Delphi survey rounds, the analysis was heavily informed by qualitative discussions with a dedicated Advisory Board. During the advisory board discussion, industry leaders provided critical context to the survey data, deepening the report’s insights on organizational behavior, business alignment, and technological adoption.

Study Execution

Round 1: Exploratory Discovery

The initial round utilized open-ended questions to gather broad qualitative insights from the expert panel. Participants shared their professional experiences and observations to identify emerging trends and foundational challenges within the field.

Round 2: Evaluation and Prioritization

Qualitative insights from the first round were synthesized into distinct thematic categories. During this phase, the panel evaluated, prioritized, and refined these topics. Experts weighed the relative importance of various strategies and identified the most critical areas for future focus.

Round 3: Strategy and Execution

In the final phase, the panel was asked to make definitive choices to resolve closely matched priorities from the preceding survey. Experts selected single top priorities and recommended the most effective approaches to finalize group consensus.

People Analytics Needs to Evolve Beyond Reporting

Many organizations have improved workforce reporting capabilities through dashboards, AI-enabled tools, and expanded access to workforce data. However, the findings suggest that organizations are improving reporting capabilities faster than their ability to translate workforce insights into meaningful business action.

People Analytics (PA)

Also referred to as HR or talent analytics, people analytics refers to the process of collecting and analyzing people-related data for the purpose of producing insights that improve decision making and business outcomes. People analytics generally includes the use of investigative techniques, statistical methodologies, various technologies, and analytical expertise.

Across all three Delphi rounds, experts consistently emphasize that the future value of people analytics depends less on reporting sophistication and more on operational influence, business relevance, and decision support. Yet many organizations continue to struggle to translate insights into measurable business outcomes. Last year’s research on this topic shows that only 45% of HR professionals say their people analytics systems improve talent and business outcomes, continuing a multi-year challenge in gaining operational influence.

Experts emphasize the need for operational influence

In the first Delphi round, experts repeatedly argue that people analytics must evolve beyond historical reporting and become more closely integrated with organizational decision-making.

Several participants describe a future where people analytics functions act as “decision accelerators,” helping leaders respond faster to workforce risks, operational challenges, and business priorities.

Fiona Jamison, VP Research & People Analytics at People Results, describes this evolution clearly:

“The role will shift from primarily analyzing what has happened to proactively guiding leaders on what to do next, integrating AI-enabled insights, scenario modeling, and real-time signals to support workforce transformation, AI adoption, and organizational effectiveness at scale.”

Lisa I. Perez, SPHR, SHRM-SCP, Founder and CEO of HBL Resources, similarly emphasizes the importance of moving beyond fragmented reporting and toward operational visibility and accountability:

“The goal is to move from hindsight to near real-time visibility, so leaders can make decisions based on current conditions, not last month’s compiled reports, and take action faster with clear ownership, outcomes and accountability.”

Curtis Winegar, Sr. Data Analytics Strategist at Pella Corporation, adds:

“The goal is to move people analytics from reporting what happened to helping leaders and teams make better decisions, develop capabilities faster, and deliver results more consistently.”

This execution challenge becomes even clearer in the final Delphi round. When asked which single barrier, if completely solved first, would unlock the greatest organizational impact, the largest share of experts select the execution gap: insights are produced but not translated into action.

The second and third Delphi rounds explore solutions to closing this execution gap. In Round 2, 67% of experts identify decision activation as one of the top capabilities organizations should prioritize to close the execution gap in people analytics.

Decision Activation

Partnering directly with business leaders to turn insights into measurable action plans.

In the final Delphi round when asked to prioritize only one capability to close this execution gap, half of the panelists select turning analytics into useful business actions as the single most important capability organizations should prioritize over the next two years. By comparison, only 21% select connecting HR and business data systems, while 14% prioritize predictive workforce analytics.

High-impact teams embed analytics into operational workflows

When asked in Round 2 which factors most clearly define a high-impact function, the panel generated a three-way statistical tie at 58% each. Experts evenly select embedding analytics into routine leadership and operational planning meetings, starting analyses based on specific business problems and measuring success through business impact and follow-through rather than report usage.

These findings suggest that analytics influence depends heavily on proximity to decision-making. Teams that operate separately from operational planning cycles often struggle to influence business outcomes regardless of analytical sophistication.

By Round 3, experts identify relevance as the biggest barrier preventing people analytics from participating in operational planning meetings. Half of respondents say workforce metrics often fail to connect clearly enough to financial or business goals, while 21% point to insufficient business acumen within analytics teams.

Structural and behavioral barriers continue limiting impact

The final Delphi round also asks experts how organizations should build this capability. Rather than recommending large-scale enterprise transformation, experts favor a more focused and practical approach. Twenty-nine percent recommend concentrating first on solving a small number of major business problems, such as turnover or hiring, while 21% support building specialized centralized people analytics teams. Another 21% advocate combining centralized expertise with targeted business problem-solving.

The advisory board discussions reinforce this conclusion. Helen Friedman, Partner and Chief Executive Officer at Merit Analytics Group LLC, argues that organizations often focus too heavily on analytics themselves while overlooking change management and leadership adoption. She notes that leaders frequently resist findings that challenge personal perspectives or existing practices.

Similarly, Shane McGowan, People Analytics Business Partner at LifeStance Health, explains that HR analytics often lacks the operational visibility routinely given to finance and sales functions.

This evolution points to a clear structural direction in the final survey round. When forced to define the single primary role of the people analytics function over the next two years, 43% of experts select Strategic Advisor (guiding executive strategy with workforce insights and research). The remainder of the panel is evenly split at 29% each between acting as a Data Integrator (connecting HR, finance, and operations systems) and an Embedded Partner (delivering real-time insights straight to managers and teams).

Ultimately, the research points toward a substantial evolution in the mandate of people analytics. The function can no longer operate as a passive, defensive reporting center; instead, industry leaders demand a strategic advisory capability that helps executives interpret workforce signals, anticipate organizational risks, and drive measurable outcomes.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Embed analytics into operational routines. Organizations should involve analytics teams earlier in business planning cycles rather than after decisions have already been made. Include people analytics teams in:
    • workforce planning discussions
    • operational review meetings
    • quarterly business reviews
    • succession planning conversations
  • Start with business problems rather than reporting requests. Analytics efforts create greater value when they focus on solving operational and workforce challenges rather than producing additional dashboards. Encourage analytics teams to frame projects around operational questions such as:
    • Which teams face the highest turnover risk?
    • Where are capability gaps slowing execution?
    • Which leadership behaviors improve retention?
  • Create accountability for action. This helps close the execution gap identified throughout the study. Many organizations generate insights but fail to establish ownership for follow-through. Consider:
    • assigning decision owners for major findings
    • requiring action plans tied to analytics insights
    • reviewing workforce actions during leadership meetings
  • Build analytics fluency across leadership teams. The goal is to make analytics part of everyday operational thinking rather than a specialist activity. Organizations can strengthen adoption by:
    • training HR professionals and leaders on analytics interpretation
    • creating simple business-focused storytelling templates
    • developing shared definitions for key metrics
    • teaching leaders how to use analytics during planning discussions
  • Measure business outcomes, not reporting activity. This reinforces the idea that analytics exists to improve decisions, not simply increase reporting volume. Evaluate people analytics based on outcomes such as:
    • turnover reduction
    • productivity improvement
    • stronger internal mobility
    • leadership effectiveness
    • hiring efficiency

Business Relevance Has Become the New Credibility Challenge

The true limit to people analytics' corporate influence is a fundamental deficit in commercial alignment. The Delphi panel reveals that an analytics team's ultimate institutional weight hinges entirely on whether business leaders view workforce insights as directly relevant to their immediate operational and business outcomes.

Business Value

Within the context of this survey, 'business value' refers to outcomes that directly impact financial performance, operational continuity, or competitive advantage.

Business Acumen

Understanding operational priorities and linking workforce analytics to financial outcomes.

Strategic Advisor

Guide executive strategy with workforce insights and research.

Credibility increasingly depends on business impact

In Round 1, panelists consistently highlight the need to connect people analytics to operational and financial outcomes.

Several participants stress that analytics teams must move beyond reporting activity metrics and start explaining business implications.

Fiona Jamison notes that people analytics increasingly creates value by “translating workforce data into actionable insights” connected to organizational outcomes.

Lisa I. Perez reinforces this perspective:

“Decision-driven organizations have trusted, integrated data with clear definitions, start with a business question tied to outcomes leaders own, embed people metrics into leadership routines with named decision owners, and require action plans and follow-up so insights translate into measurable change.”

Several experts also emphasize that many organizations already possess substantial workforce data. The challenge is not access to information but rather making insights operationally useful.

Andrew Sallee, Sr. Workforce Planning Consultant at T-Mobile, observes that many challenges remain process-oriented rather than technical:

“Most of the challenges and opportunities still feel more process- oriented than technical in nature.”

Jeff Higgins, Founder and CEO of Lytiqs, emphasizes the need for standardized practices. He notes that the continuous rise of SaaS solutions and the proliferation of systems make data integration difficult, advocating for an equivalent of “GAAP accounting for HR.”

Business acumen is key to closing the execution gap

Round 2 reinforces this shift quantitatively. When asked which capabilities organizations should prioritize most to close the execution gap, business acumen ranks among the top responses at 58%.

These capabilities outrank predictive analytics, data integration, governance, and storytelling, suggesting that the primary limitation in people analytics is organizational, not technical.

Round 2 experts also identify fragmented systems (58%) and lack of ownership (42%) as the most common points of process breakdown.

The advisory board discussions reinforce this theme. Konstantin Tskhay, Founder and Managing Partner at Tskhay & Associates, explains that many organizations lack an effective operating layer between strategic leadership and technical analytics teams, limiting their ability to operationalize workforce insights.

Last year’s research on people analytics reinforces this pattern. While 54% of organizations reported good or very good capability in generating data, far fewer report the same high capability in making constructive changes based on analytics insights (34%) or measuring the impact of workforce decisions (31%).

Leadership effectiveness is a key measure of business value

In Round 2, improving quality of hire and time-to-productivity along with building future skills and workforce capability ranks among the top workforce outcomes to focus on to demonstrate maximum business value at 58%.

However, the forced-choice structure in Round 3 produces a different result. Improving leadership effectiveness emerged as the top priority (35%) while building future workforce capability and improving workforce productivity are tied at 29%.

This shift suggests that although organizations continue prioritizing talent acquisition efficiency, experts increasingly view leadership capability, workforce adaptability, and productivity as stronger indicators of long-term business performance and organizational resilience.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, consider the following suggestions:

  • Connect workforce metrics to operational KPIs. Analytics teams gain credibility when workforce insights are tied directly to operational and business outcomes rather than isolated HR reporting metrics. This helps leaders evaluate workforce decisions in a broader business context and strengthens the operational relevance of people analytics. Integrate workforce analytics with:
    • productivity metrics

    • customer outcomes
    • safety and quality metrics
  • Build stronger business acumen within analytics teams. Business acumen emerged as one of the highest-priority capabilities for closing the execution gap. Analytics teams create greater influence when they understand how workforce decisions affect organizational performance. Encourage analytics professionals to strengthen their understanding of:
    • operational priorities
    • financial drivers
    • organizational strategy
    • workforce risks
  • Translate workforce insights into executive language. Several experts emphasize that leaders need interpretation and business context, not simply more workforce data. The ability to explain “what should happen next” increasingly differentiates high-impact analytics teams. Help analytics teams strengthen their ability to:
    • explain business implications
    • frame recommendations clearly
    • communicate operational risks
    • support strategic decision-making
  • Focus on decision-critical metrics rather than dashboard volume. Many organizations continue overwhelming leaders with excessive dashboards and disconnected reporting. Clear priorities improve adoption, executive engagement, and decision quality. Prioritize a smaller set of workforce indicators tied directly to:
    • strategic priorities
    • operational performance
    • critical talent risks
    • workforce productivity
  • Improve integration between HR and business data systems. This improves the organization’s ability to connect workforce decisions to measurable business outcomes. Strengthen integration between HR data and:
    • finance systems

AI Is Automating Analysis While Also Increasing the Importance of Human Judgment

AI adoption within people analytics is accelerating rapidly, but organizational readiness appears to be evolving more slowly. Last year’s HR.com research on people analytics found a sharp decline in the percentage of organizations not using AI, signaling growing experimentation and investment in AI-enabled analytics tools. While many organizations are investing heavily in AI-enabled analytics tools, the Delphi findings suggest that AI alone will not close the execution gap without stronger decision-making, oversight, and business integration.

During the advisory board discussion, Adam Murphy emphasized that people analytics becomes strategic only when connected directly to business priorities rather than isolated HR metrics. He noted that effective analytics “starts with the business metrics, not the people metrics,” reinforcing the idea that AI-generated insights create value only when tied to operational decision-making.

Across all three rounds, experts repeatedly emphasize that AI is reshaping the role of people analytics rather than replacing it. As automation handles more technical and administrative work, analytics teams will increasingly differentiate themselves through interpretation, operational influence, contextual judgment, and decision support.

Experts describe AI as an accelerator, not a replacement

Over-reliance on AI

Leaders relying on AI without applying human judgment.

Democratization

Allowing managers to access and use workforce data without relying on analysts.

Automation

Automating tedious data preparation, cleaning, and scheduled reporting.

Decision Accountability

Require leaders to justify major AI-assisted decisions.

In the first Delphi round, panelists consistently describe AI as a tool that can accelerate people analytics work by reducing manual reporting, data preparation, and repetitive analytical tasks.

Fiona Jamison describes AI as an opportunity for people analytics teams to “move faster and go deeper,” helping leaders navigate increasing organizational complexity.

Brian Kasen, Director of Data Engineering & Analytics at Mitratech, highlights how AI can dramatically expand analytical capability:

“The opportunity is to deliver insights at a breadth, depth, and speed that we’ve never seen before.”

Viktor Mirovic, CEO of KeenCorp, suggests that AI simplifies work by automating time-consuming tasks and that this gives HR a unique, timely opportunity to leverage its speed and clarity to become a strategic part of the boardroom conversation.

Ranjan Dutta, Ph.D., Founder and Managing Partner of ThothProAI, cautions against viewing AI as a “monolithic concept,” emphasizing its rapid progression from GPT models to agentic frameworks. He argues that people analytics has a critical role in workforce planning for the “human plus AI workforce” by anticipating new roles, skills, and organizational process optimization.

This focus built on the foundational metrics established in Round 2, where experts initially identified automation and democratization as the most valuable day-to-day applications of AI technology.

When forced to select the single use of AI that will create the greatest long term value, Round 3 experts were split between automating tedious data preparation and reporting (36%) and allowing managers to access and use workforce data without relying on analysts (36%).

Over-reliance on AI emerges as the greatest risk

At the same time, participants repeatedly cautioned against treating AI as a replacement for organizational judgment. Curtis Winegar emphasized that easy access to AI-generated answers may create false confidence if organizations fail to apply critical thinking:

“When AI makes answers easy to generate, it can mask bias, remove context, or encourage action without judgment.”

The second Delphi round clarifies expert concerns further. When asked to identify the most critical risk associated with AI-enabled people analytics, the most common response was over-reliance on AI without applying human judgment.

This concern outranked:

  • algorithmic bias
  • data privacy risks
  • lack of transparency in AI systems

Ranjan Dutta notes that while data integration challenges are not new, AI is rapidly changing how organizations aggregate and analyze workforce data. He explains that modern AI tools can dramatically accelerate integration and analysis processes, increasing both the speed and complexity of workforce decision-making.

Jeff Higgins expresses deep skepticism regarding AI's current autonomous capability across platforms, noting that running AI across multiple, un-integrated HR systems frequently produces highly contradictory advice. He raises the crucial question of how AI prioritizes data to determine the “truth” when conflicting information from multiple HR systems is present.

Accountability becomes more important as AI adoption expands

By Round 3, the panel focused increasingly on governance and accountability. When asked which safeguard best prevents leaders from bypassing human judgment, about two-fifths of experts selected decision accountability requiring leaders to justify major AI-assisted decisions.

The panel also shows a cautious approach toward AI investment. When asked how to prioritize limited budgets in 2026, 92% of Round 2 experts recommended piloting and experimenting with new AI tools in small areas rather than aggressively rolling them out enterprise-wide.

Instead, 75% of respondents vote to aggressively scale both the integration of cross-functional business data and the systematic embedding of analytics into routine operational decision-making loops.

This clear consensus demonstrates that leaders recommend pausing platform expansion to focus squarely on solving their data related issues.

Taken together, the findings suggest that AI is fundamentally reshaping the role of people analytics. As automation increasingly handles reporting, summarization, and basic analytical tasks, the differentiating value of people analytics teams will increasingly come from:

  • interpretation
  • business integration
  • operational influence
  • contextual judgment
  • decision support

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, consider the following suggestions:

  • Define where human oversight is mandatory. The findings consistently emphasize that organizations should not rely entirely on AI-generated recommendations for high-impact workforce decisions. This helps prevent over-reliance on automated outputs and reinforces accountability. Establish clear guidelines identifying which decisions always require human review, including:
    • promotions
    • performance evaluations
    • terminations
    • compensation decisions
    • succession planning
    • high-impact hiring decisions
  • Pilot AI in focused, measurable use cases. Experts strongly favor experimentation in smaller, targeted use cases rather than large-scale transformation initiatives. Measure operational value before scaling AI initiatives further. Begin with applications such as:
    • turnover risk identification
    • workforce planning scenarios
    • automated reporting summaries
    • skills gap analysis
    • learning recommendations
  • Strengthen AI governance and transparency. The findings repeatedly highlight the importance of governance as AI adoption expands. Employees and leaders should understand how AI-generated insights are created, interpreted, and used. Develop governance frameworks covering:
    • data privacy
    • bias testing
    • decision accountability
    • AI usage policies
    • audit processes
    • communication standards
  • Build AI and analytics literacy across leadership teams. Organizations should invest in practical AI literacy training for HR leaders, managers, and business partners. As AI-generated insights become more common, leaders need stronger capability to:
    • question assumptions
    • recognize bias
    • interpret predictions carefully
    • evaluate contextual limitations
    • avoid treating AI outputs as absolute facts
  • Reposition analytics teams around interpretation and decision support. As AI automates technical reporting work, analytics professionals should spend more time on:
    • workforce scenario planning
    • strategic consulting
    • stakeholder engagement
    • organizational storytelling
    • operational decision support

    This helps ensure AI augments human judgment rather than replacing critical thinking and organizational context.

Conclusion

The era of building better dashboards is over. For 2026, the mandate is pure execution.

As AI automates basic reporting, the true value of people analytics will be measured entirely by its operational influence. To close the execution gap, analytics leaders must fundamentally change how they operate.

  • Embed in the business. Step out of the HR silo and join routine operational planning.
  • Focus on relevance. Prioritize workforce metrics that directly impact financial outcomes.
  • Protect human judgment. Champion accountability and human oversight as AI adoption scales.

Organizations that successfully navigate this shift will transform their analytics teams from passive reporting desks into strategic decision engines. The data is already in your systems. The ultimate competitive advantage now lies in the action you take next.
