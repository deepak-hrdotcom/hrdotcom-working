HR.com’s State of Frontline and Volume Hiring 2026

Executive Summary

A reliability crisis stems from strategy shortfalls

High-volume hiring faces a reliability crisis. Demand is strong and applicants plentiful, but many organizations suffer from a lack of strategic coordination that causes the recruitment funnel to crumble under its own weight.

About half (51%) of surveyed organizations currently engage in high-volume hiring, and 94% of these expect to do so within the next year. Yet only 56% report having a defined high-volume recruiting strategy, suggesting that hiring demand is often addressed reactively rather than through coordinated workforce planning.

That is, organizations often fail to convert applicants into reliable employees. In fact, three-fifths (61%) of respondents report interview or first-shift no-shows, making reliability breakdowns one of the most common operational challenges in frontline hiring.

Funnel friction undermines the success of hiring frontline employees

Reliability issues, hiring delays, and limited communication frequently cause candidates to disengage before hiring decisions are finalized. Respondents also report persistent challenges such as high turnover and post-offer ghosting.

Experts emphasize that high-volume hiring must be managed as a conversion system where speed, transparency, and responsiveness determine whether applicants ultimately become employees.

This especially affects frontline employees. In fact, 77% of organizations report that frontline roles make up most of their high-volume hiring. As a result of hiring problems, organizations are placing renew attention on pay, schedule stability, and visible career opportunities as critical drivers of retention.

This report examines the structural, operational, and workforce forces reshaping high-volume hiring. It provides HR leaders with practical insights to reduce candidate drop-off, strengthen hiring systems, and build more stable frontline workforces.

Research Methodology

This report integrates insights from two primary research approaches:

Delphi Expert Study

This research used the Delphi method, a structured approach designed to build expert consensus through multiple rounds of evaluation. The study was conducted between February and March 2026. The research included two primary rounds. In Round 1 (exploration), experts responded to open-ended questions to identify emerging trends and challenges in high-volume hiring. In Round 2 (prioritization), the responses were synthesized into key themes, which experts evaluated and ranked to identify the most critical issues shaping frontline hiring.

The Delphi panel included 12 senior talent acquisition leaders and subject-matter experts representing a range of industries and areas of specialization. Participants brought extensive experience in talent acquisition, workforce strategy, HR technology, and frontline workforce management, providing informed perspectives on the future of high-volume hiring. Throughout this report, we refer to these panelists simply as “recruitment experts.”

Practitioner Survey

HR.com’s “State of Frontline and Volume Hiring 2026” survey ran between December 2025 and March 2026. We gathered responses from 98 HR professionals in virtually every industry. However, because only about half of respondents engage in high-volume hiring, the number of respondents to most questions ranged between 31 and 37.

Major Research Findings

1. High-volume hiring demand remains strong, but many organizations lack a strategic and financial foundation.

  • 51% of organizations currently engage in high-volume hiring.
  • 94% of those organizations expect to conduct high-volume recruiting within the next six to twelve months.
  • Only 56% of organizations report having a defined strategy for high-volume recruiting.
  • Only 29% expect an increase in high-volume recruiting budgets over the next two years.

2. Reliability failures and candidate drop-off represent the most significant operational barriers in high-volume hiring.

  • For practitioners, the top challenges in high-volume recruiting are:
    • reliability issues such as interview or first-shift no-shows (61%)
    • high employee turnover or churn (58%)
    • ineffectiveness of hiring managers in executing the hiring process (50%)
  • 43% of organizations also report that they face a considerable or major problem from hires who fail to appear after accepting an offer.
  • Recruitment experts reinforce these operational concerns by emphasizing that candidate drop-off across the hiring funnel represents the greatest threat to frontline hiring success.

3. Recruitment technology adoption is expanding cautiously, with organizations prioritizing administrative automation over automated hiring decisions.

  • The technologies most effective for high-volume recruiting include:
    • applicant tracking systems
    • mobile-optimized processes
    • employee referral tracking tools
  • Planned technology adoption indicates growing interest in automation:
    • 69% plan to use AI in their hiring processes within the next year
    • The top four uses for AI will be to:
      • support interview scheduling and coordination
      • automate offer letters and onboarding processes
      • create job descriptions or job ads
      • reach out to prospects
  • Recruitment experts strongly support the use of automation for administrative coordination tasks:
    • 88% of experts identify automated scheduling and coordination as the most valuable use of AI in frontline hiring
    • At the same time, 88% emphasize that final hiring decisions should remain human-driven

4. The most prioritized recruiting metrics focus on hiring speed and retention, but data quality and workforce outcome alignment remain limited.

  • The recruiting metrics organizations most frequently prioritize include:
    • retention rate (69%)
    • time to hire or time to fill (57%)
  • However, the usefulness of recruiting analytics appears constrained by data quality challenges:
    • 58% say recruiting data is useful for decision-making
    • but only 30% report that recruiting data is accurate and complete
    • only 27% say recruiting metrics are aligned with post-hire workforce outcomes
  • The adoption of pre-hire assessments also remains limited with only 41% of organizations reporting using assessments in high-volume recruiting.
  • Recruitment experts emphasize that recruiting success should ultimately be measured through post-hire outcomes. The most important indicators identified by experts are:
    • 90-day retention rates (63%)
    • manager satisfaction with new hires (57%)

5. The most important drivers of frontline attraction and retention are competitive pay, predictable schedules, and visible career mobility.

  • Frontline roles dominate high-volume hiring activity. Over three-fourths (77%) of organizations report that frontline or hourly roles represent the majority of their high-volume hiring.
  • The incentives most commonly used to attract frontline candidates include:
    • referral bonuses (65%)
    • career growth opportunities (65%)
    • paid training programs (52%)
    • flexible or predictable scheduling options (48%)
  • Organizations most frequently evaluate frontline employee engagement using:
    • frontline retention rates (78%)
    • attendance and reliability metrics (66%)
    • manager feedback or performance reviews (63%)
  • However, advancement opportunities remain limited. In 42% of organizations, fewer than 10% of frontline roles have a defined promotion pathway within the first year.
  • Recruitment experts strongly reinforce the importance of compensation and stability:
    • nearly all expert panelists identify competitive hourly pay as the most important factor influencing the frontline employee lifecycle.
    • predictable schedules and career mobility opportunities are the next most important factors, each cited by 50%

The Scope, Strategy, and Sourcing of High-Volume Hiring

The Operational Reality

High-volume hiring remains a core workforce activity for many organizations, yet the operational foundations supporting it appear uneven. When asked if their organization currently engages in high-volume hiring, 51% of respondents report that they do. The remainder of the survey was only presented to respondents who said "yes" to this question.

Finding: High-volume hiring demand remains strong and is expected to continue

Among these respondents, 94% expect their organization will engage in high-volume recruiting within the next six months to one year. This indicates that the need to recruit frontline employees at scale is not a temporary issue but a sustained operational requirement. However, the financial and strategic preparation supporting this demand appears less consistent.

Finding: More than three-fifths of organizations believe they are effective at high-volume hiring

When asked whether their organization is effective at high-volume recruitment, a majority of respondents express general confidence in their capabilities. Specifically, 46% agree that their organization is effective at efficiently sourcing, recruiting, and onboarding high-volume candidates, while 16% strongly agree.

However, confidence is not universal. About a quarter of respondents (24%) neither agree nor disagree with the statement, and 13% disagree or strongly disagree. This distribution suggests that while most organizations feel reasonably capable of executing high-volume hiring, many remain uncertain about the overall effectiveness of their current processes.

Finding: Over two-fifths of organizations lack a defined high-volume recruiting strategy

Only 56% of organizations report having a defined strategy for high-volume recruiting. Among those that do, 55% say their strategy has been in place for two to five years, indicating that many approaches were developed under labor market conditions that may differ substantially from those organizations face today. As a result, a large portion of organizations may be relying on hiring frameworks that were designed before recent shifts in candidate expectations, labor shortages, and operational pressures. Sourcing strategies further illustrate this reliance on established approaches.

Finding: Budget expectations for high-volume recruiting are uncertain and inconsistent

When asked about budget expectations over the next two years, 29% expect an increase in their high-volume recruiting budget, 32% expect budgets to stay the same and 15% expect a decrease. Notably, 24% report that they either do not know or do not budget specifically for high-volume recruiting. This mixed outlook suggests that many organizations anticipate ongoing hiring demand without a clear or coordinated investment strategy to support it.

Finding: Traditional sourcing channels remain the dominant tools for volume hiring

When asked which sourcing channels they consider most effective for high-volume hiring, respondents most frequently cite:

  • job boards or aggregators (67%)
  • employee referrals (58%)
  • company careers page (56%)

While these channels continue to play an important role in recruiting infrastructure, their prominence suggests that many organizations are relying on traditional sourcing approaches rather than diversifying their talent pipelines.

Finding: Nearly half of organizations manage high-volume hiring entirely in-house

When asked about external partnerships, 49% of organizations say they never outsource high-volume recruiting. Among those that do engage external support, the most commonly used partners include job boards or aggregators, temp agencies and staffing agencies.

Taken together, these findings suggest that many organizations are preparing to scale high-volume hiring without substantially modernizing the strategies used to execute it. Hiring demand remains strong, yet strategic approaches often reflect practices developed several years earlier, before the current shifts in labor market dynamics and candidate expectations.

Tracing the Consensus Across Delphi Rounds

Finding: Increasingly competitive labor markets are exposing breakdowns in the frontline hiring funnel

Insights from the Delphi panel of experts reinforce this interpretation and provide additional context for the operational pressures organizations are experiencing.

Round 1 (Exploration):

Panelists describe a labor market that has become increasingly difficult for employers attempting to recruit frontline workers. Robin Schooling defines the current labor market as a “stagnation squeeze" where the candidate pool isn't just smaller, "it's more selective and harder to sell to.” Martin Mathe notes there has been “aggressive competition for labor from other sectors like logistics and manufacturing.” Lance Haun says that candidates are also more astute: “They see a place that brings in anyone with a pulse, they look around the room and wonder - do I really want to work at a place like this?’”

Round 2 (Prioritization):

The second Delphi round asked experts to prioritize the challenges most likely to affect frontline hiring success in the coming year. Experts identified the following issues as having the greatest impact:

  • Candidate drop-off during the hiring process
  • Competition from other frontline-intensive industries
  • Hiring manager capacity and decision delays
  • Lack of workforce forecasting leading to reactive hiring
  • Wage competitiveness relative to cost of living

These priorities highlight an important shift in the nature of high-volume hiring. The central challenge is no longer simply generating applicant volume. Instead, organizations must manage conversion, responsiveness, and workforce competitiveness within increasingly constrained labor markets.

As a result, high-volume hiring now requires organizations to go beyond processing large numbers of applicants. Employers increasingly need to persuade stable, currently employed workers that transitioning to a new frontline role offers meaningful advantages in pay, schedule reliability, working conditions, or career opportunity.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following recommendations:

  • Establish a clear high-volume hiring strategy. Treat high-volume hiring as a strategic workforce capability rather than a reactive recruiting activity. HR leaders should define clear processes for sourcing, screening, hiring, and onboarding at scale, ensuring that responsibilities, timelines, and decision authority are clearly established across HR and operational leaders.
  • Regularly review and update hiring approaches. High-volume hiring strategies should evolve along with changes to labor market conditions, technologies, and candidate expectations. Conduct periodic reviews of recruitment practices, hiring workflows, and candidate experience to ensure that existing approaches remain effective and aligned with current workforce realities.
  • Expand sourcing beyond traditional recruiting channels. Relying solely on job boards and career sites can limit access to talent. HR professionals should develop broader sourcing pipelines by building relationships with community organizations, workforce development programs, vocational schools, and local referral networks. Hyperlocal recruiting strategies can help reach candidates who may not actively search through conventional platforms.
  • Strengthen workforce planning to anticipate hiring needs. Collaborate with business leaders to forecast frontline staffing needs well in advance. Effective workforce planning allows HR teams to build talent pipelines before vacancies occur, reducing the pressure of last-minute recruiting and improving the quality of hires.
  • Ensure hiring resources match operational hiring demand. High-volume hiring requires sufficient resources, including recruiter capacity, technology tools, and hiring manager support. HR leaders should ensure that recruitment infrastructure is scaled appropriately to handle large applicant volumes while maintaining a positive candidate experience.
  • Develop a compelling frontline employee value proposition. Frontline candidates increasingly evaluate employers based on pay transparency, schedule stability, workplace conditions, and opportunities for growth. HR teams should clearly communicate these elements throughout the hiring process and ensure that job expectations are transparent from the start.
  • Focus on improving hiring conversion, not just applicant volume. Large applicant pools do not guarantee successful hiring outcomes. HR professionals should analyze where candidates disengage during the hiring process and take needed actions such as streamlining application steps, accelerating decision-making, and improving communication to ensure interested candidates successfully move through the hiring funnel.

Funnel Friction and the Ghosting Epidemic

The Operational Reality

Organizations engaged in high-volume hiring are increasingly facing reliability challenges across the hiring funnel.

Finding: Reliability failures across the hiring funnel are the most frequently reported operational challenge

When asked to identify their top high-volume recruitment challenges in the past year, respondents most frequently cite reliability issues such as no-shows at interviews or first shifts, reported by 61% of respondents. This is followed by high employee turnover or churn (58%) and the ineffectiveness of hiring managers (50%).

Finding: Post-offer ghosting represents a major operational problem for many organizations

Over two-fifths of respondents (43%) report that new hires who are failing to appear after accepting an offer represent a considerable or major problem. This suggests that candidate disengagement can continue even after organizations believe hiring decisions have been finalized.

These patterns point to a broader operational issue. High-volume hiring processes often involve multiple steps, coordination between recruiters and hiring managers, and rapid decision-making. When communication slows, hiring managers delay decisions, or candidates receive limited updates, applicants may disengage before organizations finalize hiring.

Tracing the Consensus Across Delphi Rounds

Recruitment experts emphasize that the candidate experience functions as a conversion mechanism rather than simply a branding exercise. In high-volume hiring environments, the ability to move candidates efficiently from application to offer plays a critical role in securing reliable employees.

Finding: Candidate drop-off during the hiring process is the largest risk to frontline hiring success

Round 1 (Exploration):

During the first round of the Delphi study, experts consistently emphasized that friction within the hiring process significantly reduces the number of candidates who successfully convert from applicants to employees.

Martin Mathe stated, "In high-volume frontline hiring, candidate experience is a conversion and retention lever, not a branding exercise." Matt Charney highlighted that “career site conversion is often under 15%, and multi-step forms drive twice the drop-off.” Lance Haun pointed out the absolute need for "quality at speed." Tara Turk-Haynes expressed frustration at the lack of tracking visibility, stating, "I can't believe that, to this day, we can't do the Domino's pizza tracker or Uber live map with candidate experience."

Round 2 (Prioritization):

Experts officially ranked candidate drop-off during the hiring process as the top challenge. Furthermore, when asked to indicate the extent to which they agree that specific initiatives should be standard practice, 50% strongly agreed that hiring decisions must be made within 72 hours to remain competitive.

This consensus reflects the reality that frontline candidates frequently apply to multiple jobs at the same time. When organizations delay interview scheduling, feedback, or final decisions, candidates often accept competing offers or disengage from the hiring process entirely.

Taken together, the survey data and Delphi insights suggest that organizations must manage high-volume hiring as a conversion system rather than simply an applicant generation process. Reducing friction in applications, accelerating hiring decisions, and improving communication throughout the hiring journey are critical steps for converting interested applicants into reliable employees.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following recommendations:

  • Treat the hiring funnel as a conversion system. High-volume hiring should be managed as a pipeline where each stage is designed to move candidates efficiently from application to start date. HR leaders should map the full hiring funnel, identify where candidates disengage, and redesign steps that create unnecessary friction or delays.
  • Reduce application and process complexity. Long or multi-step applications can discourage candidates from completing the hiring process. HR teams should simplify application workflows, prioritize mobile-first design, and eliminate unnecessary steps that slow down candidate progression through the hiring funnel.
  • Accelerate hiring decisions to remain competitive. Frontline candidates often apply to multiple employers simultaneously and make decisions quickly. HR leaders should establish clear service-level expectations for hiring managers, including defined timelines for reviewing applications, conducting interviews, and making hiring decisions.
  • Improve communication throughout the hiring process. Lack of communication is a major driver of candidate disengagement. HR teams should ensure that candidates receive timely updates on application status, interview scheduling, and next steps. Automated messaging and status notifications can help maintain engagement while reducing administrative workload.
  • Strengthen hiring manager accountability. Hiring managers play a critical role in moving candidates through the hiring process. HR leaders should provide clear expectations, training, and tools that help managers respond quickly to candidate applications and complete hiring decisions efficiently.
  • Address reliability challenges early in the hiring process. No-shows and post-offer ghosting often reflect mismatched expectations or weak engagement during the hiring journey. HR teams should ensure that job requirements, schedules, and workplace conditions are clearly communicated early so candidates understand what the role involves before accepting an offer.
  • Extend engagement between offer acceptance and the first day of work. Candidate engagement should continue after the offer is accepted. HR teams should implement pre-boarding communication, reminders, and onboarding preparation steps that maintain candidate connection and reduce the likelihood of first-day no-shows.

Balancing Automation and Human Judgment

The Operational Reality

Technology plays a central role in enabling organizations to manage the scale and complexity of high-volume hiring. In this section, we drill down on specific technologies, including artificial intelligence.

Finding: Applicant tracking systems remain the foundational technology for high-volume hiring

When asked which tools and technologies they consider most effective for high-volume recruiting, respondents most frequently cite the applicant tracking system (ATS), selected by 75% of respondents.

In contrast, only 14% of respondents report that AI is currently among the most effective technologies used in high-volume recruiting. This low level of perceived effectiveness suggests that many organizations are still experimenting with AI tools or have yet to integrate them meaningfully into hiring workflows.

Finding: Nevertheless, most organizations plan to expand AI use within the next year

When asked how they intend to leverage AI technologies over the next year, 69% of respondents report plans to use AI in some capacity. However, the planned uses focus primarily on administrative efficiencies rather than candidate evaluation. Organizations most commonly plan to use AI for automating offer letters and onboarding processes (36%) and scheduling interviews (36%).

At the same time, 31% of respondents report that they do not plan to use AI at all in their hiring processes, highlighting a clear divide in how organizations view automation in recruiting.

Tracing the Consensus Across Delphi Rounds

Finding: Final hiring decisions are widely viewed as a responsibility that should remain human-driven

Round 1 (Exploration):

During the first round of the Delphi study, experts repeatedly emphasized that while automation can improve efficiency, it must be implemented in ways that preserve transparency and human judgment.

Robin Schooling noted, “Today's applicants understand that technology plays a role in screening - they're not naive about algorithms... What they won't tolerate is being evaluated by invisible criteria.” She also stated, “We've created recruitment systems optimized for corporate efficiency rather than candidate experience.”

Kevin Grossman stated that “even with the need for automation... the lack of human contact in the hiring process” is his biggest concern. Matt Charney noted that “automation works, but opaque automation does not.”

Round 2 (Prioritization):

In the second round of the Delphi study, experts were asked to identify the most valuable uses of AI in frontline hiring. A large majority of experts (88%) identify automated scheduling and coordination tasks as the single most valuable application of AI in frontline hiring.

Experts were also asked which parts of the hiring process should remain primarily human-driven. A strong majority of panelists (88%) indicate that final hiring decisions should remain in human hands.

This near-unanimous agreement reflects the belief that while technology can support high-volume hiring by improving speed and efficiency, human judgment remains essential for evaluating interpersonal skills, reliability, and cultural fit. These qualities are particularly important in frontline roles that require direct interaction with customers, colleagues, and the public.

Taken together, the survey data and Delphi insights suggest that the most effective hiring systems will combine automation with human judgment. Automation can streamline administrative tasks and improve operational efficiency, while human involvement remains essential at the points in the hiring process where trust, evaluation, and relationship-building are most critical.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following recommendations:

  1. Use automation to remove administrative friction, not human judgment. HR teams should prioritize automation in areas that create operational bottlenecks, such as interview scheduling, candidate reminders, application tracking, and onboarding documentation. Automating these tasks allows recruiters and hiring managers to focus their time on evaluating candidates and building relationships.
  2. Preserve human involvement at key decision points. Even in high-volume hiring environments, certain stages of the hiring process should remain human-driven. Key interviews, final hiring decisions, and offer discussions are critical moments where candidates evaluate the organization and where managers assess qualities that are difficult to measure through automated systems.
  3. Ensure transparency when using AI in hiring processes. Candidates increasingly expect to understand how hiring decisions are made. HR leaders should clearly communicate when automation or artificial intelligence is used, what role it plays in the hiring process, and how decisions are ultimately determined. Transparent processes help maintain candidate trust and reduce perceptions of unfair or opaque evaluation.
  4. Establish governance for responsible AI use in recruiting. As organizations expand their use of AI, HR leaders should implement clear guidelines that define where AI is appropriate and where human oversight is required. Establishing governance frameworks helps ensure that automation supports efficiency while maintaining fairness, accountability, and compliance.
  5. Train hiring managers to work effectively with hiring technologies. Technology alone cannot solve hiring challenges if managers do not understand how to use it effectively. HR teams should provide training that helps hiring managers leverage applicant tracking systems, automation tools, and communication platforms while maintaining strong candidate engagement.
  6. Design hiring processes that balance efficiency with candidate experience. Automation should streamline processes without making the hiring journey feel impersonal. HR leaders should evaluate each stage of the hiring process to ensure that technology improves speed and coordination while still allowing meaningful interaction between candidates and hiring managers.

Metrics, Pre-Hire Assessments, and Data Quality

The Operational Reality

Organizations engaged in high-volume hiring rely heavily on performance metrics to evaluate recruiting outcomes. But which metrics are most useful? In this section, we look at which metrics matter most to survey respondents and recruitment experts.

Finding: Retention and hiring speed are the most prioritized metrics in high-volume recruiting

When asked which high-volume recruiting metrics matter most, respondents most frequently prioritize retention rates (69%) and time to hire and/or time to fill (57%). This reflects the operational pressures organizations face in frontline hiring. Employers must both fill roles quickly and ensure that new hires remain in their positions long enough to maintain operational stability. However, the effectiveness of these metrics depends heavily on the quality and integration of the underlying data.

Finding: Recruiting metrics are widely used for decision-making but often lack accuracy and alignment with workforce outcomes

While 58% of respondents report that recruiting data is useful for decision-making, only 30% say that the data is accurate and complete. Even fewer organizations (27%) report that recruiting data is aligned with post-hire performance outcomes.

This gap indicates that many organizations track recruiting activity but lack the integrated data systems needed to evaluate whether hiring decisions actually produce successful employees. Without clear connections between recruiting metrics and workforce outcomes, organizations may struggle to identify which hiring practices lead to stronger performance and retention.

Finding: Two-fifths of organizations leverage pre-hire assessments for high-volume recruiting

Only 41% of organizations report using pre-hire assessments for high-volume recruitment. Among these adopters, the most common assessment type is job knowledge or competency testing, used by 71% of organizations that conduct assessments.

In some cases partner organizations that handle high-volume recruiting use these assessments but among the 47% of organizations that do not use a high-volume recruitment assessment tool, the most frequently cited reason is skepticism regarding its effectiveness. Fifty-three percent of these respondents indicate that they do not believe assessments would work for their organization or the roles they are hiring for.

These findings suggest that while many organizations see assessments as a tool for improving hiring accuracy, others remain uncertain about their value or concerned that they may introduce additional barriers into already complex hiring processes. Organizations should determine the value of assessments for their particular use cases.

Tracing the Consensus Across Delphi Rounds

Finding: Post-hire outcomes are viewed as the most reliable indicators of hiring success

Experts emphasize that hiring success should ultimately be measured using post-hire outcomes rather than focusing solely on top-of-funnel recruitment efficiency. When organizations rely heavily on time-to-hire metrics without linking recruiting data to employee performance and retention, they risk optimizing for speed rather than hiring effectiveness.

Round 1 (Exploration):

During the first round of the Delphi study, experts raised concerns about the overuse of automated assessments and the limitations of data-driven screening.

Lance Haun cautioned that “some AI driven assessments are an example of rigor for no reason.” Robin Schooling noted that “algorithmic screening will never capture what a genuine conversation can reveal.” Martin Mathe warned that “artificial intelligence can create false precision by overvaluing measurable data while missing qualities like reliability, coachability, and work ethic.”

Round 2 (Prioritization):

In the second round of the Delphi study, experts were asked to identify the most important metrics organizations should use to evaluate frontline hiring effectiveness. Experts most frequently prioritize 90-day retention rates (63%) and manager satisfaction with new hires (57%) as the most important metrics for evaluating hiring effectiveness.

These priorities reinforce the idea that recruiting success cannot be measured solely through hiring speed or applicant volume. Instead, organizations must evaluate whether new hires remain in their roles and perform effectively after joining the organization.

Taken together, the survey data and Delphi insights suggest that organizations should strengthen the connection between recruiting analytics and workforce outcomes. By linking hiring data to retention, performance, and manager feedback, organizations can better understand which recruiting practices produce reliable and successful frontline employees.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following suggestions:

  • Connect recruiting metrics to workforce outcomes. HR teams should move beyond tracking hiring speed and applicant volume by linking recruiting data to post-hire outcomes such as retention, performance, and manager satisfaction. Integrating recruitment analytics with HRIS and performance systems allows organizations to identify which hiring practices produce successful employees.
  • Improve the accuracy and completeness of recruiting data. Reliable decision-making depends on high-quality data. HR leaders should review how recruiting data is collected, stored, and reported to ensure that metrics are accurate, consistent, and accessible to both HR and operational leaders responsible for hiring decisions.
  • Prioritize outcome-based hiring metrics. Organizations should focus on metrics that reflect the long-term effectiveness of hiring decisions rather than only measuring process efficiency. Tracking indicators such as early retention, supervisor satisfaction with new hires, and job performance can provide more meaningful insights into hiring success.
  • Use pre-hire assessments selectively and purposefully. Assessments can support hiring decisions when they are directly related to job requirements and candidate capabilities. HR teams should ensure that assessments are relevant to the role, validated for effectiveness, and designed to complement rather than replace human evaluation.
  • Avoid adding unnecessary barriers to the hiring process. Complex assessments or screening tools can discourage candidates from completing the hiring process. HR professionals should evaluate whether each assessment step adds meaningful predictive value or simply increases friction in the hiring funnel.
  • Combine data insights with managerial judgment. While data analytics and assessment tools can support hiring decisions, they should not fully replace human evaluation. Hiring managers and recruiters should use data as a guide while still relying on professional judgment to evaluate interpersonal skills, motivation, and role fit.
  • Continuously evaluate the predictive value of hiring tools. HR leaders should regularly review whether existing assessments, metrics, and screening methods accurately predict employee success. Periodic evaluation allows organizations to refine hiring practices and ensure that recruiting systems are aligned with actual workforce performance.

Frontline Expectations, Incentives, and Growth

The Operational Reality

Frontline or hourly roles dominate high-volume hiring, cited by 77% of organizations. What can organizations do to attract and retain frontline workers?

Finding: Referral bonuses and career growth messaging are the most common incentives used to attract frontline workers

Referral bonuses (65%) and career growth opportunities (65%) are the incentives most frequently used by organizations to attract frontline candidates. Paid training is offered by half of surveyed organizations. Flexible or predictable scheduling options and tuition assistance are also commonly offered, cited by 48% of respondents. These incentives suggest that organizations recognize the importance of both financial rewards and long-term development opportunities when recruiting frontline employees.

Finding: Organizations primarily track retention and reliability to measure frontline workforce engagement

To evaluate engagement and workforce stability, respondents most commonly rely on the retention rate of frontline employees (78%), attendance and reliability rates (66%), and manager feedback or performance reviews (63%). These measures indicate that organizations focus heavily on operational indicators of stability and performance when evaluating the effectiveness of frontline hiring and retention strategies.

Despite emphasizing career growth as a recruitment incentive, many organizations lack clearly defined advancement pathways for frontline employees.

Finding: Most frontline roles lack a defined promotion pathway within the first year

When asked what percentage of frontline roles have a defined career growth or promotion pathway that employees can achieve within 12 months, the results indicate that in over two-fifths (42%) of organizations, less than 10% of frontline roles have a clearly defined advancement path. This gap suggests that while organizations frequently promote career growth as part of their employer value proposition, the operational structures needed to support early advancement are often missing.

This disconnect between recruitment messaging and operational reality may contribute to retention challenges. When employees are attracted by promises of advancement but encounter limited opportunities after joining, they may quickly begin searching for alternative employment.

More broadly, frontline workers increasingly behave like informed consumers in the labor market. They evaluate job opportunities not only based on pay but also on scheduling stability, workplace culture, and opportunities for long-term career development. Organizations that emphasize growth opportunities during recruitment but fail to provide visible progression pathways risk undermining employee trust and accelerating turnover.

Tracing the Consensus Across Delphi Rounds

Round 1 (Exploration):

During the first round of the Delphi study, experts highlighted the importance of compensation, expectations management, and workplace benefits in influencing frontline employee retention.

Lance Haun delivered a stark warning regarding base compensation: “If you're paying within a couple bucks of minimum wage like many of these roles are to start, you are going to continue to face enormous challenges in just keeping your business running. It is unavoidable.” Haun also warned, “Employers complain about day 1 no shows but I'm way more concerned about day 7 no shows. That points to a serious onboarding or expectations failure.”

Providing a nuanced view on what drives candidates beyond pay, Emily Walsh stated, “They care more about benefits offered (both healthcare related along with wellness) than the actual job and/or pay. We are seeing a generational shift, so the key is meeting and tailoring expectations while still keeping employees accountable.”

Round 2 (Prioritization):

In the second round of the Delphi study, experts were asked to identify the factors with the greatest impact on the frontline employee lifecycle, from offer acceptance through retention. Most panelists (88%) identify competitive hourly pay as the most important factor influencing the frontline employee lifecycle.

Predictable schedules (50%) and career mobility opportunities (50%) tied as the next most critical factors.

These insights reinforce the survey findings indicating that frontline workers evaluate employment opportunities holistically. Compensation, scheduling reliability, and visible advancement opportunities collectively shape whether employees remain engaged and committed after joining an organization.

Taken together, the survey data and Delphi insights suggest that organizations must align recruitment messaging with operational realities. When compensation is uncompetitive, schedules are unstable, or career growth pathways remain undefined, organizations are likely to experience continued frontline turnover and workforce instability.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following takeaways:

  • Align recruitment messaging with operational reality. HR teams should ensure that incentives promoted during recruitment accurately reflect the opportunities employees will experience after hiring. If career growth is presented as a key attraction strategy, organizations must clearly define advancement pathways and communicate realistic timelines for progression.
  • Strengthen early career pathways for frontline roles. Frontline employees are more likely to remain engaged when they can see visible opportunities for advancement. HR leaders should work with operational managers to design structured pathways that outline how employees can progress into higher-skilled roles, supervisory positions, or specialized functions.
  • Focus on the first 90 days of the employee experience. Retention challenges often emerge within the first weeks or months of employment. HR teams should design onboarding and early development programs that clarify job expectations, introduce growth opportunities, and help new hires build relationships with supervisors and peers.
  • Treat scheduling stability as a retention strategy. Predictable schedules help employees manage work alongside other responsibilities such as childcare, education, or second jobs. HR leaders should collaborate with operational leaders to improve schedule consistency and provide advance notice of shifts whenever possible.
  • Expand the frontline value proposition beyond wages. While competitive pay remains essential, frontline workers also evaluate benefits, workplace culture, and development opportunities. HR teams should ensure that the full range of employment benefits, including wellness programs, learning opportunities, and recognition systems, are clearly communicated throughout the hiring and onboarding process.
  • Use engagement metrics to identify early warning signs of turnover. Tracking retention, attendance, and manager feedback can help organizations detect patterns that signal disengagement. HR teams should analyze these indicators regularly and intervene early when signs of dissatisfaction or instability emerge.
  • Equip frontline managers to support retention. Frontline supervisors play a central role in shaping the employee experience. HR leaders should provide managers with training and tools that help them communicate expectations clearly, support employee development, and address workplace issues before they lead to turnover.

The 2026 Managerial Roadmap

The Delphi panel of recruitment experts emphasized that organizations must prioritize their frontline hiring improvements carefully due to limited resources and operational constraints. Rather than attempting to overhaul every aspect of the hiring process at once, experts recommend approaching improvement initiatives at different speeds depending on their maturity, potential impact, and organizational readiness.

Finding: Experts recommend scaling mobile-first applications and SMS communication immediately

When asked how organizations should prioritize hiring initiatives, experts most strongly support aggressively scaling mobile-first applications and SMS-based candidate communication. In both cases, 38% of panelists recommend organization-wide rollout. Mobile-first application processes help reduce candidate friction and improve accessibility, while SMS communication provides faster and more reliable updates compared to traditional email-based communication.

Finding: Onboarding innovation and manager accountability should be piloted and tested

Several initiatives receive strong support as pilot programs rather than immediate large-scale implementations. A majority of experts (63%) recommend piloting onboarding programs focused on cultural assimilation and workplace integration rather than limiting onboarding to administrative paperwork. In addition, 50% suggest experimenting with mechanisms that establish clearer manager accountability for employee retention.

Experts also recommend testing structured 30-day post-hire follow-ups, supported by 50% of panelists. These early check-ins can help organizations identify potential issues related to job expectations, training gaps, or workplace integration before they lead to early turnover.

Finding: Experts advise deprioritizing pre-hire workplace tours in high-volume hiring environments

When evaluating lower-priority initiatives, 63% of experts indicate that organizations should not pursue pre-hire workplace tours as a standard hiring practice. Panelists suggest that in high-volume hiring environments, these tours often require significant coordination and time while providing limited return compared to digital communication or virtual updates.

Taken together, these recommendations highlight the importance of focusing on operational improvements that reduce friction, accelerate communication, and strengthen early employee engagement. By prioritizing initiatives based on their potential impact and feasibility, organizations can make targeted improvements to frontline hiring systems without overextending limited resources.

Conclusion

The combined insights from practitioner data and Delphi recruitment expert analysis show that high-volume hiring in 2026 is no longer simply a matter of generating large numbers of applicants. Organizations must manage the hiring funnel as a coordinated workforce system that balances speed, transparency, and reliability.

Technology plays a critical role in enabling organizations to process large applicant volumes efficiently. Mobile-first applications, automated scheduling, and digital communication tools can significantly reduce administrative friction and accelerate hiring timelines. However, the research also makes clear that automation alone cannot solve the challenges associated with frontline hiring. Experts consistently emphasize the importance of maintaining human interaction at key decision points. Conversations with hiring managers, clear communication of expectations, and early relationship-building remain essential for establishing trust and ensuring that candidates fully understand the role they are accepting.

At the same time, organizations must align recruitment promises with operational reality. Competitive pay, predictable schedules, visible career pathways, and supportive frontline managers are all essential elements in attracting and retaining frontline workers. When these elements are missing, even well-designed hiring systems struggle to convert applicants into stable employees.

Ultimately, organizations that succeed in high-volume hiring will be those that integrate operational efficiency with human-centered hiring practices. By combining streamlined digital processes with transparent communication, meaningful employee incentives, and strong frontline management, employers can build more reliable frontline workforces and reduce the costly cycle of turnover and rehiring.
