 Finding: Most PM processes include both
positive feedback and critique
 The most common features of performance management processes
are balanced feedback and a discussion of goals. Two-thirds include a
conversation about development.
Fewer than half say their PM process is easy to understand and only 32%
say the process accurately portrays employee performance, which is one
of the primary goals of the process.
Part of the gap may be that development conversations, even when
they happen, stop short of giving employees a concrete picture of what
success looks like. As Kimberly Hodge, Talent Development Manager at
MWM DesignGroup, describes it:
“With every development plan we have, we also have a success
plan now—what does success actually look like for that person?
 That extra step helps managers go beyond ʼhereʼs the skill thatʼs
missingʼ to ʼhereʼs what you need to do to be successful.”
 — Kimberly Hodge
 Talent Development Manager at MWM DesignGroup
In other words, pairing a development plan with a success plan may be one
way to close the distance between having a PM process and having one
that employees feel actually captures their performance.

 HRRI Strategic Recommendations
 Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:
 1. Modernize performance tools to reflect real contributions.
 Only 32% of organizations say their PM process accurately captures actual performance. Closing that
gap means investing in tools and metrics that map more closely to what people actually do, not just
 whatʼs easy to measure.
 2. Reposition performance management as a growth conversation, not a compliance task.
 Half of organizations already say PM strengthens legal protection, but far fewer say it improves output
 quality or organizational performance, and two-thirds of managers who donʼt see PM as relationship-
 building describe it as an obligation to get through. Give managers the frameworks and prompts to
turn reviews into real development conversations, documenting fairly and defensibly without letting
compliance crowd out growth.
 3. Normalize frequent, informal career conversations.
 Building both into the regular cadence of work strengthens team dynamics and manager-employee
relationships alike, but only if managers have the skills to lead these conversations well.
