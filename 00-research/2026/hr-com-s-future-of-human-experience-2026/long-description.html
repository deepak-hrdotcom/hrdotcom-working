HR.com’s Future of Human Experience 2026

Executive Summary

HR professionals view employee experience as a perennial priority, but most continue to struggle with the issue. They have ranked it among the top five most important HR-related issues since 2022. Despite that, just two-fifths give their organizations high grades when it comes to employee experience.

Some of the problems boil down to specifics. For example, fewer than half of study respondents believe their employees feel recognized for their contributions (42%) or empowered to share honest opinions (41%). What's more, heavy workloads and burnout continue to wear people down.

Many HR professionals link their organization’s employee experience problems to poor leadership. These organizations are far more likely to cite insufficient leadership buy-in, lack of leadership awareness, and a lack of managerial interest in employee experiences. They are also 10 times more likely to cite their culture as a hindrance to good employee experiences.

Organizations with high employee experience levels, by contrast, are more likely to create environments where employees feel supported, heard by management, and positively connected with coworkers.

What will the future bring? Asked to look two years into the future, responding HR professionals were most likely to point to artificial intelligence (AI) as a factor affecting employee experience. Participants see AI as affecting the employee experience through increased automation, higher productivity, improved learning and development, and increased communication. Only 26% predict that AI will increase employee stress due to increased job losses.

We hope their optimism is warranted, but we should also note that just 25% say they are currently actively using AI systems in HR day-to-day. As this number grows over the next two years, we will gain a better understanding of AI’s positive and negative impacts on employee experience.

About This Study

HR.com’s “Future of Human Experience 2026” survey ran from December 2025 to March 2026. We gathered responses from 188 HR professionals in virtually every industry vertical. Respondents are from all over the world, with the majority from North America, especially the United States.

The participants represent a broad cross-section of employers by number of employees, ranging from small businesses with fewer than 50 employees to enterprises with 20,000+ employees. Sixty-eight percent of the respondents represent midsized and large organizations.

The State of Employee Experience Today

Finding: Forty percent of respondents say employees would give their employee experience a high rating (at least 8 out of 10)

Forty percent of respondents say employees would rate their experience highly, with scores between 8 and 10. This is the cohort we label as “experience leaders” (see sidebar).

At the same time, 30% say employees would rate their experience in the 1 to 6 range. This is the cohort we label as “experience laggards.”

Experience-based cohorts

To better understand how to create a great human experience in the workplace we divided respondents into two cohorts:

  • Experience leaders: those who perceive their employees would rate their experience as an eight or above on a 10-point scale.
  • Experience laggards: those who perceive their employees would rate their experience between one and six on the same scale.

Of course, correlation is not the same as causation, but studying the difference between these cohorts can certainly help us identify some of the best practices in the employee experience area.

Employee experience over the years

We survey HR.com webcast registrants every year to determine what the top HR-related issues are for the year. The research shows employee experience has been cited as a top five most important HR-related issue since 2022. So, it’s concerning that 60% do not believe their employees would give their organization a high rating in employee experience.

Finding: Employee voice and recognition show the largest gaps in employee experience

The data on how employees feel shows that some aspects of employee experience are positive, but they are not strong across the board. More than half of HR professionals believe employees feel comfortable being themselves at work (56%) and supported by their manager (53%). These results are only fairly positive because the numbers are not that far over half, showing that in many organizations employees do not feel that way.

The biggest gaps are in voice and recognition. Only 41% say employees feel empowered to share honest opinions, and just 42% say employees feel recognized for their contributions. These lower scores point to a lack of trust and openness in many organizations. External research supports this pattern. A longitudinal study by Gallup found just 19% of employees strongly agree when asked if they trust leadership as of August 2025.

Defining small, midsize and large organizations

Throughout the report, we look at the findings based on organizational size. We deem organizations with 1 to 99 employees as “small,” those with 100 to 999 as “midsize,” and those with 1,000 or more employees as “large.”

Differences based on organization size

Employees in small organizations (60%) are far more likely to feel recognized for their contributions compared to those in midsize (36%) or large (31%) organizations. Recognition seems harder to sustain at scale.

Finding: Experience leaders are nearly five times more likely than experience laggards to report that employees feel their well-being is valued

There exists a large gap between leaders and laggards on employee sentiment related to their experiences at work. Over two-thirds (68%) of experience leaders report that employees feel their well-being is valued, compared to just 15% of experience laggards. This highlights well-being as a key differentiator in how employees experience the organization.

Large gaps are also seen in everyday experiences. Experience leaders are far more likely to report that employees feel supported by their managers (80% vs. 30%) and comfortable being themselves at work (81% vs. 30%), pointing to stronger cultural and interpersonal environments.

Similar patterns emerge in recognition (69% vs. 11%), access to resources (68% vs. 28%), and openness in sharing opinions (66% vs. 6%). Together, these differences suggest that experience leaders are more effective at translating their practices into more positive employee sentiments.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that experience leaders are significantly more likely than experience laggards to say employees in their organizations have positive sentiment regarding listed experience-related issues.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Make recognition a regular practice rather than an occasional one. Even small but consistent acknowledgments can shift how valued employees feel over time. Build simple routines such as weekly or monthly recognition moments and integrate recognition into team meetings and performance check-ins.
  • Do not assume that your employees feel valued or that they feel they can express their honest opinions. Use surveys, exit interviews, stay interviews and more in order to determine how they really feel.
  • Pay close attention to the degree to which employees feel supported by their managers. If they typically do not, then your organization may need to do a better job of developing solid managerial skills. Equip managers with training on feedback, active listening, and coaching, and hold them accountable through employee experience metrics.
  • Pay close attention to the everyday factors that shape how employees feel, particularly the quality of manager relationships, since the data shows this is where leaders and laggards diverge most sharply.

Factors Shaping Employee Experience

Finding: Positive relationships with coworkers is by far the most common factor driving a good employee experience

Positive relationships with coworkers is the most widely cited driver of a good employee experience, selected by 69% of respondents. This is well ahead of other factors, with a sense of meaning and purpose (49%) and opportunities for learning and growth (48%) trailing in second and third place.

Beyond these top factors, responses are more spread out, suggesting less agreement on what matters most. One notable gap is well-being.

For example, only about one in five respondents identify a strong focus on employee well-being as a top factor, though it’s not clear whether this is less valuable or just less common.

Differences based on organization size

Small organizations (59%) are more than twice as likely as large organizations (28%) to cite a positive, non-toxic workplace culture as a top factor positively impacting employee experience, with midsize organizations (34%) in between. This suggests that culture is more visible and tangible in smaller settings, while large organizations may struggle to maintain it consistently across the whole organization.

Finding: Heavy workload is the most commonly cited factor negatively impacting employee experience

What aspects most negatively impact the employee experience? The three most commonly cited negative factors are the closely related issues of heavy workloads (48%), employee burnout (45%), and inability to grow within the organization (45%). Burnout is of special concern because not only is it a moral issue, but it also suggests that a formerly productive employee has been made unproductive due to poor management.

In contrast, fewer respondents point to poor relationships with immediate supervisors as a top issue, selected by about one in five. This suggests that, at least for now, structural and workload-related pressures may be more widespread than interpersonal challenges with direct managers.

Finding: Experience laggards are nearly five times more likely than experience leaders to report that employees do not feel heard by leaders

Experience laggards are nearly five times more likely than leaders to report that employees do not feel heard by leaders (62% vs. 13%). A similar gap appears in organizational transparency, cited by 39% of laggards compared to 16% of leaders.

These factors point to weaker communication and limited employee voice in laggard organizations, where employees are less likely to feel acknowledged or informed.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that the experience leaders are significantly less likely than the laggards to say the listed factors negatively impact employee experience in their organizations.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Create structured, regular opportunities for employees to share their views such as regular pulse surveys, skip-level meetings, and anonymous feedback tools.
  • Be transparent about what you do with that feedback. Employees who see no action taken will quickly stop speaking up.
  • Train managers to listen actively in one-on-ones rather than treating those meetings purely as status updates. The data consistently shows that the manager relationship is central to whether employees feel heard.
  • Provide safe channels for honest input. Use anonymous surveys or feedback tools where trust is low, and reinforce that feedback will be handled constructively.

Finding: Almost two-thirds of organizations offer hybrid work

Flexible work arrangements are common. Almost two-thirds of organizations offer hybrid work and more than half offer remote work. However, more flexible or less traditional options are far less common. Only 11% offer sabbaticals and even fewer (7%) offer job sharing.

Finding: Experience leaders are more than twice as likely as experience laggards to offer flextime

Experience leaders are more likely than experience laggards to offer remote work (63% vs. 41%) and flextime (57% vs. 26%), indicating a broader commitment to flexibility. These differences suggest that experience leaders are more intentional in enabling flexibility in both where and when work happens, while experience laggards continue to offer more limited options.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that experience leaders are significantly more likely than experience laggards to offer listed flexible work arrangements.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Review the flexibility of current work arrangements. For example, offering remote work without flexibility in working hours might only address part of what employees need to stay productive.
  • Where full flexibility is not possible, identify which roles or teams could pilot more flexible arrangements and use those results to inform a broader approach.
  • Frame flexibility not as a perk but as a tool for productivity and retention. Leaders in this space are not offering flexibility out of generosity; they are doing it because it works.

Understanding Barriers and Accountability in Employee Experience

Finding: Budget constraints are by far the most common factor hindering organizations from enhancing the employee experience

HR departments typically have a range of ideas for enhancing the employee experience. However, to be implemented, any such idea needs a budget and this can be hard to come by. Most respondents (59%) cited budget constraints as a factor hindering the organization from enhancing employee experience. If HR does want to implement new programs, it will have to draw up a compelling business case.

Other barriers are less common but still notable. Lack of leadership awareness is cited by 36% of respondents, and about one in four point to lack of interest from managers. These results suggest that beyond budget, alignment and engagement from leaders and managers remain ongoing challenges.

Recent HRRI research on workplace culture and connection found there’s a disconnect between what executives believe and what lower-level managers believe in terms of executive involvement in improving the work environment. While executives may believe they are supporting improvements, managers are less likely to see the same level of involvement. Closing this gap is critical to moving from intent to action.

Finding: Experience laggards are over three times more likely than experience leaders to cite lack of leadership buy-in as a barrier

Experience laggards are over three times more likely than experience leaders to cite lack of leadership buy-in as a barrier (65% vs. 20%) to enhancing employee experience.

Sizeable differences also appear in leadership awareness (44% vs. 23%) and culture not supporting employee experience (40% vs. 4%), highlighting deeper cultural and alignment issues.

Gaps also extend to execution. Experience laggards are more likely to report infrastructure limitations (48% vs. 23%) and lack of manager interest (39% vs. 14%), suggesting challenges in translating intent into action across the organization.

Together, these differences suggest that experience leaders operate with stronger leadership alignment and cultural support, while experience laggards remain constrained by foundational issues that limit progress.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that experience laggards are significantly more likely than experience leaders to say that listed factors act as barriers to enhancing employee experience in their organizations.

Finding: Three-quarters of respondents say top leadership is responsible for employee experience to a high or very high extent

Creating the employee experience is a shared responsibility. A majority of respondents said that every group has high or very high responsibility for employee experience.

However, while responsibility for employee experience is shared, it appears the onus is on immediate supervisors and top leaders. Three-quarters of respondents point to top leadership as having a high or very high responsibility, and 81% point to immediate supervisors as having a high or very high responsibility.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Build a clear business case for employee experience investment that connects directly to outcomes leaders care about, such as retention costs, productivity, and customer satisfaction.
  • If cultural resistance is a barrier, start small. Pilot one initiative, measure it carefully, and use the results to open the door to broader conversations.
  • Address manager disengagement directly by incorporating employee experience responsibilities into manager expectations and performance conversations.
  • Close the loop visibly. When employee feedback leads to a change, say so explicitly and attribute it to the feedback. This single habit does more to build trust in listening channels than almost anything else an organization can do.

Understanding and Measuring Employee Experience: Data, Tools, and Competitive Insights

Finding: Organizations still have limited visibility into employee experience comparison with competitors

Only one in five organizations report having a good understanding of how their employee experience compares to competitors. This lack of visibility limits organizations’ ability to benchmark performance and identify gaps. Despite employee experience being a top HR priority, many organizations rely primarily on internal data, which makes it difficult to assess how they perform relative to others.

Finding: Experience leaders are 9 times more likely than experience laggards to understand how they compare to industry peers

Experience leaders are nine times more likely than laggards to understand how their employee experience compares to industry peers (37% vs. 4%).

This suggests that experience leaders are more likely to use benchmarking to guide decisions and improve their practices. In contrast, experience laggards may rely more on internal views, which can limit their ability to identify gaps and stay competitive.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that experience leaders are significantly more likely than experience laggards to say they have a very good understanding of how they compare in terms of employee experience levels to other similar organizations/industry peers.

Finding: Organizations primarily rely on employee feedback and retention data to measure experience

Turnover, retention, and absenteeism data are the most commonly used measures of employee experience (73%), followed by employee feedback from conversations (69%) and engagement or survey data (67%). This suggests many organizations are still measuring employee experience reactively rather than proactively. Surveys and 1:1 conversations are more forward-looking, but their near-equal usage alongside attrition data implies they may be used more as damage control than as genuine listening tools.

Fewer use business-linked measures. Only 45% use performance and productivity data despite its clear connection to organizational outcomes. This may reflect challenges in linking experience to performance or a lack of analytical capability.

Differences based on organization size

Large organizations (84%) are significantly more likely than midsize (66%) or small (55%) ones to use employee engagement and survey data to measure experience. However, the reverse is true for employee feedback from conversations: small (78%) and midsize (76%) organizations lead large ones (57%) in this area. The takeaway here is that large organizations lean on formal surveys while small organizations rely on direct conversation.

Finding: A large majority of organizations use exit interviews to gauge the employee experience

Exit interviews are the most used method to gauge employee experience (73%), well ahead of other approaches. This reinforces a reactive pattern, as organizations focus most on understanding the employee experience when it is already too late to retain them. One-on-ones follow closely at 70% which is encouraging, considering managers are arguably closer to employees than anyone else in the organization, making those conversations a natural and timely channel for surfacing how people are really feeling.

However, the drop to other methods suggests that more proactive and continuous listening is less embedded. Only 42% use pulse surveys and 33% use open feedback tools, indicating that structured, ongoing feedback channels are not as widely adopted. Even fewer organizations use advanced approaches such as internal communications analytics (14%) or AI and machine learning tools (10%), limiting their ability to track patterns or anticipate issues at scale.

Together, these results suggest that while organizations are listening to employees, they continue to rely more on retrospective insights than on forward-looking, data-driven approaches that could help address issues before they lead to disengagement or turnover.

Differences based on organization size

Large organizations (67%) are far more likely than midsize (40%) or small (24%) ones to use pulse surveys to gauge employee experience. This likely reflects both the resources to run them and the need for formal listening mechanisms when informal conversation doesn't scale.

Technology-Driven Improvements in Employee Experience

Finding: Employee recognition and feedback tools dominate the employee experience tools

When it comes to technology for improving employee experience, recognition and reward tools and survey and feedback tools top the list, both at 45%. The fact that these two lead the pack is telling: organizations seem to understand that employees want to feel heard and appreciated and are investing in tools that address those needs directly.

Collaboration tools come in second at 43%, a figure that likely reflects the lasting influence of hybrid and remote work on how organizations think about the employee experience. Keeping people connected is no longer assumed; it requires deliberate investment.

Nearly a third (34%) say learning and development tools are most useful in improving the employee experience. This aligns with our most recent employee retention research showing that organizations with stronger retention approaches are much more likely than those with weaker approaches to use learning and development to improve retention (51% vs. 13%). Furthermore, a Deloitte study on Millennials and Gen Z employees found learning and development was a top three reason they chose to work for their employer. Organizations that invest here are arguably addressing the experience at its root rather than simply measuring or responding to it.

One number worth pausing on is the 14% who use no technologies for this purpose at all. While this is a minority, this group may find themselves at a growing disadvantage in attracting and retaining talent as expectations from employees continue to rise.

Finding: Over half of organizations are at least open to investing more in acquiring technologies to influence the employee experience

More than half of organizations are open to investing in employee experience technology, but many still have no plans to act. More than a quarter of organizations plan to acquire new tools, with 14% aiming to do so within the next year and a further 13% are planning beyond that. Combined with the 31% who are not yet committed but actively thinking about it, that means over half of organizations are at least open to spending more in this area.

The remaining 42% have no plans and are not considering it. This split highlights a divide between organizations that are moving toward more structured, technology-enabled approaches and those that continue to rely on more informal or manual methods.

Organizations that do not invest are likely to face growing challenges in keeping pace with employee expectations, particularly as experience becomes more data-driven and continuous.

Differences based on organization size

Small organizations (55%) are far more likely than midsize (32%) or large (18%) to have no plans to acquire new technologies for gauging or influencing employee experience. Investment in HX technology grows sharply with size.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Identify two or three external benchmarks relevant to your industry, such as Glassdoor ratings or sector-specific engagement surveys, and review them on a regular basis.
  • Talk to vendors, speak to peers in similar organizations about what technology they are using, and get clear on what problem you are actually trying to solve before making any decisions. Buying technology without that clarity is one of the most common reasons implementations fail to deliver.
  • When presenting employee experience data to leaders, include a comparison to peer organizations to give the numbers context and urgency.

Outcomes of Experience-Enhancing Initiatives

Finding: Stronger organizational culture and increased employee engagement are the biggest improvements

We asked respondents what improvements have resulted from experience-enhancing initiatives. Increased employee engagement tops the list at 44%, followed closely by stronger organizational culture (43%) and improved retention (41%). These three outcomes are deeply connected: when employees feel more engaged, they tend to stay longer and contribute to a healthier culture. Seeing them cluster at the top suggests that experience initiatives, when they work, tend to create a positive ripple effect rather than improving just one thing in isolation.

In contrast, outcomes tied more directly to business performance are reported less often. Higher productivity and better employee well-being are each cited by 30%, indicating a gap between expected and realized impact.

A notable 18% report that employee experience has not improved at all. This likely reflects the gap between having programs in place and actually implementing them well.

Future Trends and the Role of AI

Finding: About four in 10 organizations are actively using or piloting AI for HR

As mentioned earlier, we survey HR.com webcast registrants. The data from an early 2026 post-registration survey found that AI adoption in HR is gaining momentum, with nearly 40% of organizations already using or piloting AI. However, its application remains concentrated in areas such as talent acquisition (44%) and people analytics (31%), with far less use in core employee experience areas like engagement and experience management (19%).

Despite this, expectations for AI’s impact on employee experience are high. AI is the most commonly cited factor expected to influence employee experience over the next two years (42%).

In addition, 36% believe employee experience will improve overall, and a nearly equal share expect better ways of measuring it.

At the same time, expectations for deeper transformation are more moderate. Only 25% anticipate more personalized experiences, and 22% expect experience initiatives to become more continuous and real-time.

Overall, organizations are optimistic about the role of AI, particularly in improving measurement and insights, but expectations for more advanced, experience-level transformation are still emerging.

Finding: AI is most likely to influence the employee experience over the next two years through automation

We asked a follow-up question to those who believe the employee experience will get better, regarding how AI will play a role in influencing the employee experience over the next two years. About three-quarters believe it will increase automation. Over half also believe it will raise productivity (59%) and improve learning and development (51%).

It is also worth noting that not all responses are positive. Fully 41% are worried it will cause privacy and monitoring concerns. And about a third (32%) are worried it will cause more security risks. These concerns highlight the importance of governance and trust as AI adoption increases. A smaller group (14%) believe AI will have no impact on employee experience in the next two years.

Overall, organizations expect AI to improve efficiency and capability, but concerns around data use and risk may influence how quickly and confidently it is adopted.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaway 1: Shift from reactive to proactive measurement.

The most widely used measure of employee experience is still turnover and absenteeism data, meaning most organizations only take notice after something has gone wrong. Pair this with more forward-looking tools like pulse surveys and regular one-on-ones to build a fuller, timelier picture. Investing in structured stay conversations gives you the same depth of insight while there is still an opportunity to act on it.

Key Takeaway 2: Prioritize the relationships that matter most.

Positive relationships with coworkers are by far the most cited driver of a good employee experience, selected by over two-thirds of respondents. Creating conditions for genuine connection at work, whether through team structures, shared spaces, or collaborative projects, is one of the highest-return investments an organization can make. Managers play a central role in closing this gap through consistent, day-to-day interactions. Three-quarters of respondents say top leadership bears high or very high responsibility for employee experience, yet fewer than half of employees feel empowered to share honest opinions. Closing that gap starts with how managers show up day-to-day.

Key Takeaway 3: Take burnout seriously as a management failure, not just an employee problem.

Heavy workload and burnout are the two most common factors negatively impacting employee experience, and they are closely linked. To address this, organizations should audit workload distribution, set realistic performance expectations, and monitor capacity at the team level. Managers should be trained to identify early signs of burnout and adjust priorities accordingly. Regular check-ins should include discussions on workload and well-being, not just performance. In addition, organizations should review whether growth expectations, staffing levels, and timelines are aligned with available resources.

Key Takeaway 4: Close the gap between having programs and making them work.

Nearly one in five organizations report that their experience-enhancing initiatives have produced no improvement at all. Having programs in place is not the same as implementing them well. To address this, organizations should define clear success metrics for each initiative and track outcomes regularly. Collect targeted employee feedback to assess whether programs are reaching the intended audience and delivering value. Hold managers accountable for implementation at the team level and adjust or discontinue initiatives that do not show measurable impact. Communicating what is working and what is changing can also reinforce credibility and engagement.

Key Takeaway 5: Build the business case for leadership investment.

Budget constraints are the single biggest barrier to improving employee experience, cited by 59% of respondents. HR teams that want to move forward need to be able to articulate the cost of inaction, through retention data, productivity losses, and engagement scores, as clearly as they articulate the cost of new programs. Present findings in business terms that align with leadership priorities, and track outcomes over time to reinforce the case for continued investment.

Key Takeaway 6: Approach AI with intent, not just enthusiasm.

About two-fifths (42%) of respondents expect AI to significantly influence employee experience over the next two years, particularly through automation and improved learning and development. But over 40% also worry about privacy and monitoring risks. To address this, define clear use cases where AI adds value to employee experience, such as improving feedback or personalization. Establish governance frameworks that set boundaries for data use and ensure compliance. Communicate transparently with employees about how AI is used and what data is collected. Pilot AI applications in targeted areas, measure impact, and scale only where benefits are clear and trust is maintained.

Key Takeaway 7: Don't mistake "good" for "good enough".

While 40% of organizations rate the employee experience as very positive, 30% consider their employee experience subpar. A positive rating is encouraging, but it can breed complacency. The difference between good and excellent is where organizations lose talent to competitors who are doing more. Regularly ask whether the experience you offer is genuinely better than what they could find elsewhere.

Key Takeaway 8: Use external benchmarks, not just internal ones.

A sizeable majority of organizations (80%) have no visibility into how their employee experience compares to competitors, and fewer than one in five actively seek out that information. Internal data tells you how things are changing over time, but it cannot tell you whether you are falling behind. Even basic benchmarking, through industry surveys, Glassdoor data, or peer conversations, gives the kind of context that internal metrics alone cannot provide. Present findings in business terms that align with leadership priorities, and track outcomes over time to reinforce the case for continued investment.
