 Finding: Nearly two-thirds of organizations
evaluate L&D value primarily through
immediate knowledge or skill improvement
 Organizations continue to rely heavily on short-term indicators when
measuring the success of learning initiatives. The most common measures
 of L&D value are knowledge or skill acquisition (65%), compliance
 outcomes (61%), and learner satisfaction (41%). While these metrics
provide useful insight into whether employees completed training and
gained new knowledge, they do not necessarily indicate whether learning
translated into improved performance or organizational results.
Measures tied more directly to business outcomes remain less common.
Only a third assess behavioral change on the job, scalability and cost-
effectiveness. Most notably, just 28% calculate return on investment (ROI),
 suggesting that relatively few organizations formally quantify the business
 value generated by learning initiatives.
This measurement imbalance makes it harder for L&D teams to
demonstrate strategic value. As executives increasingly prioritize
productivity improvements and ROI, organizations may face growing
pressure to adopt more outcome-focused measurement approaches.

 HRRI Strategic Recommendations
 Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:
 1. Focus technology investments on integration, not expansion
 Many organizations already have a broad mix of learning technologies in place. Before adding new
platforms, evaluate whether existing systems work together effectively and support a seamless
learner experience.   Consider:
 ●   reviewing connections between learning, HR, and talent systems
 ●   reducing duplicate tools and overlapping functionality
 ●   improving data sharing across platforms
 ●   prioritizing technologies that support a unified employee experience
 2. Connect learning data to talent and business outcomes
 Learning data becomes more valuable when it is linked to workforce and business metrics.
Organizations should move beyond tracking course completions and begin connecting learning
activity to broader talent outcomes. Consider:
 ●   linking learning data to performance and career progression
 ●   tracking the impact of development efforts on critical skill gaps
 ●   integrating learning data into workforce planning discussions
 ●   using skills data to support talent decisions

 3. Use technology to support capability development, not just compliance
 Learning technologies are often highly effective at delivering mandatory training but less effective at
developing critical workforce capabilities. Organizations should evaluate whether their technology
investments are helping employees build the skills needed for future success. Consider:
 ●   strengthening skills-based learning pathways
 ●   using technology to support personalized development
 ●   identifying technologies that help employees apply learning on the job
 ●   aligning learning tools with workforce capability priorities
 4. Measure the impact of learning, not just participation
 As expectations for workforce development increase, organizations need stronger evidence that
learning investments are delivering value. Measurement approaches should focus on outcomes as
well as activity. Consider:
 ●   evaluating behavioral change and skill development
 ●   tracking workforce readiness for critical roles
 ●   measuring internal mobility and promotion outcomes
 ●   assessing the business impact of learning initiatives
