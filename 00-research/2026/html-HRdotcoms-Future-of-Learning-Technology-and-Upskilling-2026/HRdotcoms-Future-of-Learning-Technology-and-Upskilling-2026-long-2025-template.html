HR.com’s Future of Learning Technology and Upskilling 2026

Executive Summary

Organizations understand they must build new skills to remain competitive. The question is no longer whether upskilling matters, but whether organizations are equipped to deliver it effectively.

“HR.com’s Future of Learning Technology and Upskilling 2026” survey reveals a growing gap between ambition and execution. Two-thirds of organizations expect employee upskilling to become more or much more important over the next two years, yet only 30% rate their learning and development (L&D) efforts as highly effective.

Skills gaps continue to affect every level of the workforce, with mid-level managers emerging as the group most in need of development. At the same time, learning remains disconnected from broader talent strategies and daily work for many organizations. Only 17% report full alignment between learning and talent management, while just 37% say their primary learning platform is fully integrated with their HR system.

Artificial intelligence is adding new urgency to this challenge. Organizations are rapidly adopting AI to improve access to knowledge, automate learning tasks, and personalize development. However, only one-quarter of organizations are developing an AI reskilling strategy, and relatively few have established a mature approach to preparing employees for an AI- enabled workplace.

The findings suggest that organizations will need to build learning ecosystems that align with business priorities, integrate learning into everyday work, use technology more strategically, and give employees a continuous path to develop new skills.

About this Research

“HR.com’s Future of Learning Technology and Upskilling 2026” report combines insights from three research sources:

  • The primary research source is “HR.com’s Future of Learning Technology and Upskilling 2026” survey, which ran from April 2026 to June 2026. We gathered responses from 94 HR professionals across virtually every industry vertical. Respondents are located around the world, although most reside in North America, particularly the United States.
  • To provide additional context on adoption of learning technology the report also includes findings from the HR Research Institute (HRRI) pulse survey conducted among 141 HR.com webcast registrants in June 2026.
  • Finally, the report incorporates qualitative insights from HR leaders with expertise in learning technology and upskilling.

Our Major Research Findings

1. Organizations recognize that upskilling is a strategic priority, but most have yet to build highly effective learning functions.

  • Only 30% rate their L&D efforts as highly effective.
  • Two-thirds expect employee upskilling to become more or much more important over the next two years.
  • Only 17% report that learning is fully aligned with talent acquisition, succession planning, and performance management.
  • Nearly one-third (32%) say upskilling is not a priority for top leadership, highlighting a disconnect between business needs and executive commitment.

2. Skills gaps remain widespread, yet learning continues to be reactive rather than continuous.

  • 56% report frequent skills gaps among mid-level managers, the highest of any employee group.
  • 58% say employees upskill only when an immediate need arises.
  • Only 18% report that employees engage in continuous learning.
  • Just 37% say employees are given dedicated time to learn during the workday.

3. Learning technology is widely adopted, but fragmented systems limit its strategic value.

  • 67% use a Learning Management System (LMS) or Learning Experience Platform (LXP).
  • AI-powered learning tools are one of the fastest-growing learning technologies with 37% currently adopting them versus 15% last year.
  • Only 37% report their primary LMS is fully integrated with their HR system and just 44% say learning systems integrate well with HR and other enterprise platforms.
  • The biggest technology weaknesses are lack of integration into the flow of work (39%), lack of personalization (37%), and poor integration with other systems (32%).

4. Learning technologies perform well for administration and compliance but fall short in building workforce capability.

  • 88% rate their learning technologies as effective for compliance and regulatory training.
  • Only 13% say their learning technologies are extremely effective at closing skill gaps and just 14% say the same about delivering personalized learning experiences.
  • Nearly two-thirds (65%) measure learning success through immediate knowledge or skill improvement, while only 28% formally calculate learning return on investment (ROI).
  • Just 11% say learning is fully embedded into employees' daily workflows.

5. AI is transforming workplace learning, but organizational readiness remains in the early stages.

  • 72% use AI to help employees search and access organizational knowledge.
  • 56% use AI for administrative automation or content generation, while 52% provide personalized learning recommendations.
  • 26% report their AI reskilling strategy is still under development.
  • Only 17% have a formal AI reskilling strategy in place and 19% still have no strategy.
  • 74% develop AI skills through supported hands-on experimentation, yet only 17% are actively researching or evaluating agentic AI for L&D.

Strategic Priorities, Executive Alignment, and Skill Gaps

Finding: Only 30% rate their organization's L&D efforts as highly effective

When asked to what degree their organization’s L&D efforts produce meaningful results such as improving performance or closing skill gaps, only 30% of respondents rate the overall effectiveness of their efforts at 8 or higher on a 10-point scale, while 55% assign ratings between 5 and 7. This suggests that although many organizations believe their learning programs are producing acceptable results, relatively few view them as a strong driver of organizational performance.

Although the 2026 survey measures effectiveness rather than quality, the results continue a pattern observed over the past several years. In 2023, just 14% rated the quality of their L&D efforts highly, increasing to 21% in both 2024 and 2025. In 2026, 30% rate their L&D efforts as highly effective, suggesting some improvement over time. Across all four years, most respondents have consistently placed their organizations in the middle of the performance scale rather than at the high end.

Finding: Almost three-fifths report that skills gaps occur often or extremely often among mid-level managers

Mid-level managers are the employee group most likely to experience skills gaps, with 56% of respondents reporting that these gaps occur often or extremely often.

Global data shows that up to 60% of frontline and mid-level managers receive no formal leadership training after promotion. At the same time, Gartner's latest workforce research shows that automation has slowed entry-level hiring, increasing organizational reliance on mid-level talent while weakening traditional leadership pipelines. As a result, many managers are stepping into strategic roles without adequate preparation.

When asked where organizations most need to focus their reskilling efforts to drive future success, experts heavily emphasized automation and real-time operational agility. David Creelman, CEO of Creelman Research, notes that automation is a critical priority, while Matthew Casey-Sudvary, PhD, specifies that organizations must prioritize “Identifying redundant tasks that can be automated.”

JD Dillon, Advisor, Speaker, and Author, highlights a shift in necessary core competencies.

“As work becomes increasingly tech-driven and organizations continue to push for short-term gains, employees must develop their ability to foster relationships, build trust, adapt to ongoing change, and develop new capabilities to execute evolving systems and processes. Change management is out, adaptation and readiness are in.” - JD Dillon

Becky Willis, Chief Learning Officer at Tractus, points to specific target roles across the enterprise that require immediate focus, stating that organizations must prioritize upskilling efforts for managers, IT, L&D/TD, data architects, and most office workers.

Finding: A third of respondents say that upskilling is not a priority for top leadership

Perceptions of the importance of upskilling vary across stakeholder groups. Employees place the greatest urgency on development, with 35% ranking upskilling as their top priority or among their top three priorities. Executives report a similar level of urgency, with 35% also placing upskilling within their top three priorities, although 32% do not consider it a priority at all.

One possible explanation is that many leaders remain focused on immediate operational and financial priorities, making investments in workforce development more difficult to justify despite growing evidence that skill shortages and technological change are increasing the need for continuous learning. HR professionals appear somewhat less likely to view upskilling as a top-tier issue, with fewer ranking it among their highest priorities.

These findings suggest that while a segment of leaders recognize the strategic importance of upskilling, employees may feel a stronger need for development opportunities and clearer pathways for skill growth. Mercer's comprehensive evaluation outlines a structural “triple misalignment” among employees, HR, and the C-suite. Their data confirms that most employees are deeply concerned about lacking future-ready skills, to the extent that 63% of workers would willingly trade a 10% pay raise for guaranteed upskilling opportunities.

Organizations that successfully position upskilling as a business priority rather than an employee benefit may be better equipped to secure executive support and sustained investment.

Finding: Thirty percent describe their L&D efforts as mostly aligned with broader talent management strategies

While 30% of respondents report that L&D efforts are mostly aligned with broader talent management strategies, relatively few organizations have achieved full alignment. Just 17% say that learning, talent acquisition, succession planning, and performance management share common goals and data.

At the same time, 26% report that L&D operates largely independently of broader talent strategy, and 25% say the two functions are not aligned at all. Together, this means that more than half of organizations report only limited alignment or outright separation between learning and talent management.

This lack of integration can limit an organization's ability to identify, develop, and deploy talent effectively. When learning, recruitment, performance management, and career development operate separately, organizations may struggle to connect skill development with workforce planning and internal mobility goals. To address this strategic misalignment, McKinsey & Company recommends aligning business, HR, and technology leaders around critical skills rather than managing learning in functional silos.

Finding: Two-thirds of respondents expect employee upskilling to become more important over the next two years

Upskilling has become an established workforce priority rather than a temporary response to disruption. Two-thirds of respondents (66%) expect upskilling to become either more important or much more important over the next two years, while only 4% expect it to become less or much less important. Coursera’s research shows that organizations have stopped treating Generative AI–driven upskilling as a crisis to manage and started treating it as the new normal. As the report puts it, "acceleration is now the baseline."

Longitudinal analysis reveals a consistent pattern. Since 2023, the percentage of respondents who expect upskilling to become more important or much more important has decreased slightly, but remains high, ranging from 69% to 77%. The modest decline may track the steady rise in perceived L&D effectiveness over the same period, as organizations that feel better equipped to deliver on upskilling may feel less pressure to flag its importance as sharply.

Pulse Poll Finding: Leadership support is viewed as the single most important factor separating organizations that successfully leverage learning technologies from those that struggle to realize value

A companion HR Research Institute poll of 141 HR professionals found active leadership support (57%) to be the strongest differentiator between organizations that realize value from learning technology and those that don't, ahead of content quality (44%) and dedicated learning time (43%), reinforcing this report's core finding that leadership engagement drives results.

Organizations are more likely to realize value from learning technologies when leaders actively champion learning initiatives, align learning with business priorities, and create the conditions that enable employees to develop new skills.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

1. Connect learning initiatives to business priorities

Learning programs are more likely to gain leadership support when they address specific business challenges. Rather than building programs around available content, focus development efforts on the skills and capabilities that will have the greatest impact on organizational success. Consider:

  • identifying the top workforce challenges facing the organization
  • prioritizing development efforts that address critical skill gaps
  • aligning learning objectives with business goals
  • regularly communicating the business value of learning initiatives

2. Make manager development a top priority

Managers have a significant influence on employee performance, engagement, and growth. Investing in manager capability can improve team effectiveness while strengthening the overall learning culture. Consider:

  • providing leadership development for new and experienced managers
  • strengthening coaching and feedback skills
  • helping managers identify and address skill gaps within their teams
  • equipping managers to support employee development conversations

3. Align learning with talent management processes

Learning should not operate independently from hiring, performance management, succession planning, and career development. Connecting these processes helps ensure development efforts support broader talent goals. Consider:

  • partnering with talent acquisition and talent management teams
  • aligning learning pathways with career progression opportunities
  • incorporating skill development into performance discussions
  • using workforce planning data to inform learning priorities

4. Measure whether learning is making a difference

Tracking course completions and participation rates is important, but it does not show whether learning is improving workforce capability. Focus on measures that demonstrate impact. Consider:

  • assessing whether critical skill gaps are decreasing
  • tracking internal promotions and mobility
  • evaluating manager and leadership readiness
  • gathering feedback on how learning is being applied on the job

Workplace Culture, Learning Integration, and Operational Constraints

Finding: Almost three-fifths of respondents report that employees engage in upskilling on an on-demand or as-needed basis

Employee learning is driven primarily by immediate needs rather than ongoing development. Almost three-fifths (58%) report that employees engage in upskilling on an on-demand or as-needed basis, while only 18% say employees participate in continuous learning. Smaller percentages report learning taking place multiple times a year (10%) or according to a more structured schedule.

While on-demand learning can help employees address immediate skill needs, it may do little to build future capabilities or prepare for emerging workforce demands. At the same time, growing workloads and rising expectations leave employees with little time to pause, reflect, or engage in deeper learning.

Encouraging more continuous skill development could help employees build capabilities over time while improving organizational readiness for future changes in skills and work.

Finding: About eight in ten respondents say that employees frequently teach one another work-related skills

Peer learning remains one of the strongest aspects of workplace learning culture. Seventy-eight percent of respondents agree or strongly agree that employees frequently teach one another work-related skills, making it one of the highest-rated learning culture measures in the study.

Longitudinally, agreement has stayed steady at 77% in 2024 to 79% in 2025 and 78% in 2026, suggesting that employees are increasingly relying on colleagues as an important source of knowledge sharing and skill development. Research shows that soft skills matter now more than ever and tools such as peer feedback and mentoring are essential to developing these skills.

At the same time, organizational support for learning remains inconsistent. Only 37% of respondents agree or strongly agree that employees are provided dedicated time for learning during the workday. According to Forbes, heavy daily workloads and rising performance targets leave employees with virtually zero time or energy left for training.

Managerial support is another gap with only 45% reporting positive sentiments about managerial support for employees to develop their skills. This tension may help explain why overall satisfaction with learning culture remains relatively modest. Only 28% say employees are satisfied with their organization's learning culture and 25% are satisfied with internal career opportunities.

Finding: Forty-five percent of respondents report that learning is only partially integrated into employees' daily workflows

Learning in the flow of work remains more aspiration than reality for many organizations. While 45% of respondents report that learning is partially embedded into daily workflows, only 11% say learning is fully integrated into the tools and processes employees use every day. At the same time, 33% indicate that learning remains largely separate from daily work, and another 10% report that their organizations do not support learning in the flow of work at all.

These findings suggest that many organizations still rely on traditional learning approaches that require employees to step away from their work to learn. However, research suggests learning is most effective when it occurs in the flow of work, making integration into existing tools and workflows increasingly important.

Industry experts reinforce the data showing that learning must transition from isolated events into structural daily tools. Matthew Casey-Sudvary emphasizes that the future of effective learning platforms requires systems that are “highly personalized and more performance-support” focused. JD Dillon critiques traditional platform boundaries, arguing that learning technology must integrate directly into the digital workspace.

“Learning tech as a concept must break free of the LMS category and shift information access / sharing into the tools used to do the job. This requires a headless approach to information architecture to reach people when and where they need support.” – JD Dillon

Becky Willis echoes this critical need for structural workflow integration and modernized architecture. According to Willis, next-generation platforms need AI at the core, not added in. She points to workflow integration, personalization, and collaborative learning as the pillars that will define next-generation platforms.

Finding: Twenty-nine percent of respondents estimate that between 26% and 50% of employee learning is delivered through technology

Organizations continue to rely on a mix of learning approaches rather than a technology-driven model. Half of respondents estimate that half or less of employee learning is delivered through technology, while only 18% report that more than three-quarters of learning occurs through digital platforms and tools.

Although learning technologies have expanded access to training, improved scalability, and increased flexibility for learners, they appear to complement rather than replace other forms of development.

Finding: One-third of respondents expect technological trends to alter between 1% and 20% of roles within their organizations over the next two years

Organizations appear divided in their expectations regarding the impact of technological change on the workforce. While 43% anticipate little or no disruption, a substantial 25% expect more than 40% of roles to undergo significant change. This wide distribution highlights considerable uncertainty about the pace and scale of workforce transformation. The fact that 18% of respondents remain unsure about the potential impact further reinforces this uncertainty. BCG’s newest operational data projects that over the next two to three years, 50% to 55% of all corporate job roles will be completely reshaped by AI.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

1. Create more opportunities for continuous learning

Many employees develop skills only when an immediate need arises. Organizations can build workforce readiness by making learning a regular part of the employee experience rather than an activity reserved for specific projects or skill gaps. Consider:

  • encouraging ongoing skill development throughout the year
  • incorporating development goals into regular employee conversations
  • providing employees with visibility into future skill requirements
  • supporting self-directed learning aligned with career growth opportunities

2. Build on the strength of peer learning

Employees are already teaching and learning from one another. Organizations can amplify these behaviors by creating more structured opportunities for knowledge sharing and collaboration. Consider:

  • establishing mentoring and peer coaching programs
  • creating communities of practice around key skill areas
  • recognizing employees who share expertise with others
  • encouraging cross-functional learning and collaboration

3. Give employees time and support to learn

Learning is difficult to sustain when employees are expected to develop new skills on top of full workloads. Organizations should ensure employees have both the time and managerial support needed to learn effectively. Consider:

  • dedicating time for learning during the workweek
  • encouraging managers to discuss development regularly
  • setting realistic expectations for learning participation
  • helping managers balance operational demands with employee growth

4. Bring learning closer to the flow of work

Employees are more likely to develop new skills when learning resources are available at the moment they need them. Embedding learning into everyday work can improve both participation and knowledge application. Consider:

  • integrating learning resources into commonly used tools and systems
  • providing just-in-time learning opportunities
  • connecting learning content to real work challenges
  • making it easy for employees to access learning without leaving their workflow

5. Create clearer development pathways

Employees are more likely to invest in learning when they understand how skill development supports career growth. Organizations should help employees connect learning activities to future opportunities. Consider:

  • defining skill requirements for key roles
  • communicating potential career pathways
  • linking learning opportunities to advancement opportunities
  • helping employees identify development priorities

Software System Integration, Architecture Failures, and Performance Gaps

Finding: Over two-thirds of organizations use a Learning Management System (LMS) or Learning Experience Platform (LXP)

Traditional learning platforms continue to serve as the foundation of most learning technology ecosystems. Over two-thirds of organizations (67%) report using an LMS or LXP, making these platforms the most widely adopted learning technologies in the study. Although learning platforms remain dominant, longitudinal findings suggest that organizations are gradually expanding their technology portfolios beyond traditional systems. LMS adoption stood at 72% in 2023 and 75% in 2024 and dropped to 51% in 2025, indicating that growth has largely plateaued as organizations explore additional learning technologies.

The most notable change in the technology landscape is the rapid growth of AI-powered learning tools. Adoption increased from 14% in 2024 and 15% in 2025 to 37% in 2026, making AI one of the fastest-growing categories in the learning technology stack. Although AI can accelerate content creation and summarization, excessive reliance on these capabilities risks promoting standardized thinking rather than reinforcing organizational strengths.

As workforce development becomes increasingly dynamic, organizations may need to evaluate whether their technology investments align with how employees actually learn and develop skills.

Finding: Only 37% of organizations report that their primary LMS is fully integrated with their core HR system

Organizations that use LMS reveal a fragmented approach to learning system integration. Organizations are divided almost evenly between those with fully integrated systems (37%), partially integrated systems (32%), and systems that are not integrated at all (32%).

Without integration, organizations often struggle to connect learning with performance, retention, internal mobility, and workforce planning outcomes. When learning and talent data reside in separate systems, visibility and decision-making suffer. As expectations for learning ROI increase, integration between learning and talent systems will become increasingly important for demonstrating business value.

Finding: Almost nine in ten respondents rate their learning technologies as moderately or extremely effective for compliance and regulatory training

There is a gap between the administrative and developmental capabilities of learning technologies. Compliance and regulatory training remain the area where learning technologies perform best, with 88% of respondents rating their systems as moderately or extremely effective.

However, effectiveness declines sharply when organizations evaluate outcomes that are more closely tied to workforce capability. Only 13% rate their learning technologies as extremely effective at closing skill gaps, while 14% say the same for personalizing learning experiences.

Finding: Sixty-three percent of respondents agree that most employees know how to find and access learning technologies

Employee access to learning technologies appears to be a relative strength for many organizations. Over six in ten (63%) respondents agree or strongly agree that employees know how to find and access learning technologies and 57% say their learning technologies work well across employees' preferred devices. This suggests that awareness and basic accessibility are not the primary barriers to learning technology adoption. Organizations also have improved capabilities in tracking learning data (64%).

However, the data reveals a gap between accessibility and integration. Many organizations continue to struggle with connecting learning technologies to one another and to broader "enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems." Under two-fifths (37%) of respondents agree or strongly agree that their learning technologies integrate effectively with one another, while under half (44%) agree or strongly agree that learning systems are well integrated with HR, ERP, and other business platforms. This disconnect may help explain why organizations continue to face challenges translating learning activity into workforce insights and business outcomes.

Finding: Two-fifths identify lack of integration into the flow of work as a weakness of learning technologies

Lack of integration into the flow of work is the most frequently cited weakness of learning technologies (39%). In addition, 37% identify lack of personalization and 32% say poor integration with other systems are challenges. Together, these findings indicate that organizations are concerned less with access to learning and more with how seamlessly learning fits into employees' day-to-day work experiences.

Research by the Harvard Business Review and the American Psychological Association suggests that frequent switching between applications creates a significant "toggle tax" that reduces productivity and fragments attention, making seamless learning integration increasingly important.

Longitudinal data highlights how organizational priorities have evolved. Lack of personalization was the leading concern in 2023 at 50% but declined to 22% in 2025 before reaching 37% in 2026. While personalization remains important, workflow integration has emerged as a leading challenge, suggesting that organizations are increasingly focused on making learning easier to access, apply, and sustain within the context of work.

Finding: Nearly two-thirds of organizations evaluate L&D value primarily through immediate knowledge or skill improvement

Organizations continue to rely heavily on short-term indicators when measuring the success of learning initiatives. The most common measures of L&D value are knowledge or skill acquisition (65%), compliance outcomes (61%), and learner satisfaction (41%). While these metrics provide useful insight into whether employees completed training and gained new knowledge, they do not necessarily indicate whether learning translated into improved performance or organizational results.

Measures tied more directly to business outcomes remain less common. Only a third assess behavioral change on the job, scalability and cost- effectiveness. Most notably, just 28% calculate return on investment (ROI), suggesting that relatively few organizations formally quantify the business value generated by learning initiatives.

This measurement imbalance makes it harder for L&D teams to demonstrate strategic value. As executives increasingly prioritize productivity improvements and ROI, organizations may face growing pressure to adopt more outcome-focused measurement approaches.

Experts agree that successful organizations distinguish themselves by shifting their focus away from surface-level engagement metrics toward tangible employee outcomes and integrated ecosystems. David Creelman advises organizations to “rationally assess the impact of the technology without falling for tools that ‘sound good’ or get good scores on unreliable metrics (e.g., smile sheets).”

Matthew Casey-Sudvary notes that the ultimate differentiator for a successful learning stack is simply whether it yields “successful outcomes for employees.” JD Dillon explains that market leaders do not view learning tech through functional silos.

“The organizations that foster continuous readiness and adaptation expand their perspective on ‘learning technology.’ They do not rely on isolated systems owned by specific functions. Instead, they adopt an ecosystem approach that activates the full range of tools, tactics, and technologies that enable people to improve their performance and drive outcomes—regardless of pre-defined categories or functional owners.” – JD Dillon

Expanding on this ecosystem perspective, Becky Willis underscores that realizing true value from technology depends heavily on organizational culture and leadership alignment. She notes that top-performing organizations are distinguished by a culture of support for TD (Talent Development), high employee engagement and retention, leadership support and value, alongside TD leaders able to increase learning maturity.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

1. Focus technology investments on integration, not expansion

Many organizations already have a broad mix of learning technologies in place. Before adding new platforms, evaluate whether existing systems work together effectively and support a seamless learner experience. Consider:

  • reviewing connections between learning, HR, and talent systems
  • reducing duplicate tools and overlapping functionality
  • improving data sharing across platforms
  • prioritizing technologies that support a unified employee experience

2. Connect learning data to talent and business outcomes

Learning data becomes more valuable when it is linked to workforce and business metrics. Organizations should move beyond tracking course completions and begin connecting learning activity to broader talent outcomes. Consider:

  • linking learning data to performance and career progression
  • tracking the impact of development efforts on critical skill gaps
  • integrating learning data into workforce planning discussions
  • using skills data to support talent decisions

3. Use technology to support capability development, not just compliance

Learning technologies are often highly effective at delivering mandatory training but less effective at developing critical workforce capabilities. Organizations should evaluate whether their technology investments are helping employees build the skills needed for future success. Consider:

  • strengthening skills-based learning pathways
  • using technology to support personalized development
  • identifying technologies that help employees apply learning on the job
  • aligning learning tools with workforce capability priorities

4. Measure the impact of learning, not just participation

As expectations for workforce development increase, organizations need stronger evidence that learning investments are delivering value. Measurement approaches should focus on outcomes as well as activity. Consider:

  • evaluating behavioral change and skill development
  • tracking workforce readiness for critical roles
  • measuring internal mobility and promotion outcomes
  • assessing the business impact of learning initiatives

AI Deployment Use Cases, Reskilling Strategy, and Autonomous Frontiers

Finding: Nearly three-quarters of organizations use AI primarily for knowledge discovery and search across internal documents

The role of AI in L&D is evolving rapidly. In 2025, the most common AI application was content generation, with organizations primarily using AI to create learning materials. In 2026, among organizations using AI for learning, knowledge discovery and search emerged as the leading use case (72%), suggesting that organizations are increasingly using AI to help employees access, navigate, and retrieve information from existing knowledge repositories.

This shift reflects a broader move toward supporting learning at the point of need. Organizations are increasingly using AI to help employees find answers, solve problems, and access information within their daily work. Administrative automation (56%), automated content generation (56%) and personalized learning recommendations (52%) also rank among the most common use cases, reinforcing the view that current AI investments are largely focused on improving efficiency and accessibility.

Global framework by Synthesia AI confirms that while AI adoption is sky-high, it remains structurally “shallow.” Most L&D teams have normalized AI for fast administrative execution (drafting quizzes, generating voiceovers), but they lack the strategic infrastructure to drive outcome-focused practices.

The qualitative data strongly mirrors the quantitative shift toward using AI for localized, in-the-flow information retrieval rather than just raw content generation. David Creelman expects AI to fundamentally reshape delivery. He says, “It will dominate learning, in particular offering excellent just-in-time, in the flow of work, training.”

Matthew Casey-Sudvary sees AI’s primary immediate value in “Creating individualized recommendations/paths.” JD Dillon points out that AI will democratize tool creation, moving power away from centralized L&D departments.

“AI will shift learning and enablement closer to the work, empowering people like managers, SMEs, and employees to build/deploy support tools without requiring centralized support every time.” – JD Dillon

Willis pushes this integrated view of AI even further, arguing that its greatest potential lies in total ecosystem connection rather than standalone features. She explains,

“Massive AI needs to be the tool that brings all of the business data together with the skills, development and integrates with other apps NOT added in.” – Becky Willis

Finding: A quarter of organizations report that their AI reskilling strategy is currently under development

As AI adoption accelerates, workforce readiness is emerging as a key barrier to transformation. The challenge is no longer recognizing the need for AI skills, but building the processes and learning infrastructure required to develop them at scale.

When asked about their organization’s current approach to reskilling employees in response to AI, a quarter (26%) report that their AI reskilling strategy is currently under development, making it the most common response. Many organizations are likely still in the early stages because AI technologies, required skills, and business use cases continue to evolve rapidly, making it difficult to identify future skill needs and develop comprehensive, organization-wide reskilling programs.

An additional 23% report having a mix of formal and informal initiatives in place, while 17% have established a formal strategy and 19% have no strategy at all. Together, these findings suggest that most organizations have begun taking steps to prepare their workforces for the impact of AI, even if many efforts remain in the early stages of maturity.

Longitudinal data reveals a significant shift in organizational attitudes toward AI workforce readiness. In 2024, 44% of organizations reported having no AI reskilling strategy and no plans to develop one. That figure declined to 38% in 2025 and just 19% in 2026. This sharp decline suggests that AI workforce preparation is increasingly viewed as a business necessity rather than an optional initiative.

A study of more than 500 global enterprise leaders highlights a significant gap between AI ambitions and workforce readiness. While 82% provide some form of AI training or resources, only 35% have a mature, organization-wide AI upskilling program.

Finding: Three-quarters of organizations deliver AI training through hands-on experimentation supported by the organization

Organizations that have some strategy for reskilling employees in response to AI, overwhelmingly favor experiential learning approaches when developing AI skills (74%) and peer-to-peer learning (67%). This reflects the practical nature of AI adoption, where proficiency is often built through experimentation, iteration, and real-world application rather than traditional classroom instruction.

Despite the emphasis on hands-on learning, more traditional approaches remain common. Internal online courses are used by 54% of organizations, and 49% rely on external learning platforms and content libraries.

Finding: Under a fifth of organizations are actively researching or evaluating how agentic AI can support L&D

Agentic AI remains an emerging concept for most organizations. Unlike traditional AI tools, agentic AI can autonomously plan, execute, and coordinate tasks. Experts suggest it could transform L&D by providing personalized guidance, identifying skill gaps, and embedding learning directly into daily work.

While 17% of respondents are actively researching or evaluating potential applications, 19% report being unfamiliar with agentic AI, 13% are unsure of its implications, and another 15% are aware of the technology but have no plans to explore it.

Industry analysts suggest that while organizations are actively exploring agentic AI, adoption remains cautious due to legal, privacy, and operational risks associated with autonomous systems. Although adoption is still relatively low, a subset of organizations has begun moving beyond exploration and into practical experimentation through pilot programs (6%).

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

1. Build a structured AI workforce readiness strategy

Many organizations recognize the importance of AI but are still developing formal approaches to workforce preparation. A clear strategy can help ensure employees develop the skills needed to work effectively alongside AI. Consider:

  • defining AI capabilities required across different roles
  • creating role-based learning pathways
  • establishing workforce readiness goals
  • aligning AI skill development with business priorities

2. Focus AI on workforce capability, not just productivity

Many organizations are successfully using AI to improve efficiency and access to information. The next opportunity is using AI to strengthen skills, support career growth, and improve workforce readiness. Consider:

  • identifying workforce challenges AI can help address
  • supporting personalized learning and development
  • exploring AI-enabled coaching and learning support
  • evaluating workforce outcomes alongside productivity gains

3. Create opportunities for employees to learn by doing

AI capabilities are often developed through experimentation and real-world application. Employees need opportunities to explore tools, test ideas, and share successful practices. Consider:

  • providing safe environments to test AI tools
  • encouraging peer learning and knowledge sharing
  • incorporating practical exercises into AI training
  • showcasing successful AI applications across the organization

4. Strengthen governance as AI adoption expands

As AI becomes more embedded in workplace processes, clear policies and oversight become increasingly important. Governance helps organizations manage risk while supporting innovation. Consider:

  • establishing guidelines for responsible AI use
  • clarifying data privacy and security expectations
  • defining accountability for AI oversight
  • reviewing emerging risks and opportunities regularly

5. Prepare for emerging AI capabilities

While many organizations are still exploring agentic AI, workforce leaders can begin building the knowledge and skills needed to evaluate future applications. Consider:

  • monitoring emerging AI technologies and use cases
  • building AI literacy among HR and learning teams
  • evaluating where autonomous systems may add value
  • assessing potential workforce implications of advanced AI

Conclusion

Organizations increasingly recognize that employee skill-building is essential for keeping pace with changing business needs and new technologies. However, many are still struggling to translate that commitment into meaningful results. Despite growing investments in learning technology and AI, learning often remains disconnected from business priorities and day-to-day work.

Future success will depend less on offering more training and more on helping employees continuously build and apply new skills. Organizations that focus learning on real business challenges, support managers in employee development, and make learning easier to access during the workday will be better prepared to address skill gaps and adapt to change.

Technology is also changing how employees learn. Looking ahead, David Creelman, CEO of Creelman Research, predicts that learning will become much more conversational and immediate.

“Most learning will come directly from chatting with a large language model with only a bit of direction from L&D on how to engage with the model.” – David Creelman

However, the experts caution that simply producing more content with AI is unlikely to create better outcomes. Instead, L&D professionals must rethink how learning is delivered and measured. As Creelman advises to “invest in discovering how AI transforms how learning can be delivered, don't just use AI to generate a lot more learning content.”

Similarly, Matthew Casey-Sudvary emphasizes the importance of starting with business outcomes rather than technology adoption. He says, “Define the problem and how you will know your program/tech is a success then measure and report.”

Perhaps the biggest shift will be the evolution of L&D itself. JD Dillon argues that capability building can no longer operate as an isolated function:

“Break out of the L&D silo and adopt an enablement mindset in collaboration with functions like communications, knowledge management, IT, and operations.” – JD Dillon

Becky Willis provides pragmatic advice for teams trying to update their infrastructure, warning professionals to stop getting stuck in legacy upgrade cycles: “Stop looking for a new LMS. Find a new solution that can either hit all the AI checkmarks by itself or add it on top of your dinosaur LMS.” She also cautions leaders to beware of red flags from vendor AI claims and strongly advises them to get help when navigating this shifting market.

As the pace of change continues to increase, the role of L&D will shift from delivering training to helping employees learn, adapt, and perform more effectively every day. Organizations that embed learning into daily tools, hold managers accountable for team development, and measure skill gaps instead of course completions will close capability gaps faster than those still waiting to act.
