HR.com's State of Legal and Compliance 2026

Executive Summary

Only 32% of organizations take a proactive approach to employment law compliance, yet 61% say they are very or completely confident they are fully compliant. This gap between confidence and capability highlights a central risk facing HR leaders today: many organizations believe they are prepared for regulatory scrutiny without the systems needed to stay ahead of it.

At the same time, compliance demands continue to intensify. HR teams are navigating expanding regulations around benefits and leave, pay transparency, data privacy, and remote work arrangements while devoting substantial time to compliance activities. Nearly two-fifths of HR departments spend more than a quarter of their workweek managing compliance-related issues, yet fewer than half report having automated processes or strong analytics capabilities to support these efforts.

The growing importance of compliance is also reflected in broader workforce trends. LinkedIn’s Skills on the Rise 2026 report identifies employment law and compliance as the fastest-growing skill in the HR profession, highlighting how regulatory complexity is reshaping the capabilities organizations expect from HR leaders.

The risks are tangible. Over one-third of organizations report facing at least one employment-related enforcement action in the past year. Organizations with proactive compliance practices (compliance leaders) are far better positioned to avoid these outcomes, with stronger funding, more frequent audits, and greater use of automation and analytics.

This report examines where organizations currently stand in their compliance maturity, what differentiates compliance leaders from laggards, and the practical steps HR leaders can take to close the gap between perceived readiness and true compliance capability.

About the Survey

HR.com’s “State of Legal and Compliance 2026” survey ran from December 2025 to March 2026. We gathered responses from 191 HR professionals in virtually every industry vertical. Respondents are from all over the world, with the majority from North America, especially the United States. Participants span diverse industries, with over two-thirds coming from midsize or large organizations.

Major Research Findings

1. A confidence gap persists between perceived compliance readiness and proactive maturity.

  • Only 32% of organizations operate with proactive monitoring and legal risk assessment practices.
  • 61% report being very or completely confident that they are in full compliance.
  • 79% agree their organization is well prepared to deal with labor and employment compliance concerns.
  • 34% report facing at least one employment-related enforcement action over the past year.

2. Benefits, leave, and pay transparency requirements dominate compliance priorities and difficulty.

  • Top tracking priorities include:
    • benefits and leave-related compliance (63%)
    • employee classification and wage/hour compliance (44%)
    • privacy and data security requirements (43%)
  • Laws most difficult to comply with are:
    • pay equity and pay transparency requirements (44%)
    • family and medical leave requirements (40%)
    • remote, hybrid, and multi-state compliance (37%)

3. Legal action, fines, and regulatory fragmentation remain the primary compliance risks.

  • Top concerns related to non-compliance are:
    • legal action or lawsuits (58%)
    • financial penalties or fines (45%)
    • reputational damage (43%)
  • Major barriers to keeping up with compliance changes are:
    • changing interpretations of employment laws (36%)
    • fragmentation between federal and state requirements (34%)
    • growing scope of HR-related mandates (34%)

4. Compliance consumes substantial HR time, while automation and reporting capabilities remain limited.

  • 42% of HR teams spend 11% to 25% of their workweek on compliance.
  • 23% spend 26% to 50% of their workweek on compliance.
  • 14% spend more than half of their workweek on compliance activities.
  • 49% report that key compliance processes are automated.
  • 56% report that their compliance technologies are part of an integrated system.
  • 62% report using up-to-date compliance technologies.
  • 41% say it is quick and easy to create useful compliance-focused reports.
  • 38% say their reporting and analytics produce actionable insights.

5. Resource gaps persist across budget, tools, and training.

  • 54% say compliance-related initiatives are well funded.
  • 48% report that stakeholders receive sufficient tools and technologies.
  • 53% report sufficient education and training.
  • 40% report sufficient budgetary support.
  • 23% rate their organization six or lower out of 10 in maintaining an up-to-date understanding of employment compliance developments.

6. Artificial intelligence adoption in compliance is accelerating, particularly in monitoring and data analysis.

  • 42% expect to incorporate AI into compliance-related technologies within the next two years.
  • 47% expect AI to ease gathering and analyzing compliance-related data.
  • 43% expect AI to enhance the ability to monitor legal compliance.
  • 39% anticipate the need to develop new guardrails and guidelines related to AI usage.
  • 31% expect AI adoption to require additional safeguarding of employee privacy.

7. Compliance leaders outperform laggards across preparedness, funding, knowledge, technology, and reporting capability.

  • Compared to laggards, compliance leaders are:
    • nearly 2x more likely to report being well prepared to manage compliance concerns (98% vs. 59%)
    • more than 2x more likely to report that compliance initiatives are well funded (67% vs. 28%)
    • less likely to report facing an enforcement action in the past year (28% vs. 36%)
    • more than 2x more likely to rate their understanding of compliance developments at eight or above on a 10-point scale (85% vs. 40%)
    • nearly 3X more likely to report that key compliance processes are automated (45% vs. 15%)
    • more than 3x likely to operate integrated compliance systems (67% vs. 21%)
    • 2x more likely to say they can quickly generate useful compliance reports (59% vs. 29%)
    • more than 2x more likely to report that their analytics produce meaningful insights (55% vs. 25%)

Closing the Compliance Readiness Gap

Because a majority of HR.com members are based in the U.S., this report primarily focuses on U.S. legal and compliance issues.

Finding: Only about one-third of organizations take a proactive approach to legal and compliance

After excluding respondents who were unsure of their organization’s compliance status, just 32% report operating with proactive monitoring and risk assessment practices in place. The remaining 68% rely on approaches that may limit their readiness for regulatory change. Nearly a quarter of organizations (26%) report having systematic monitoring processes but no proactive legal risk assessments. Another 18% maintain documented procedures that are not regularly updated through structured monitoring or audit. A further 13% operate with formalized but incompletely documented processes, while 12% continue to address legal issues reactively.

This distribution closely mirrors last year’s results, suggesting limited progress in moving from administrative compliance toward forward-looking legal risk management.

Editor’s note: The original question in the survey was “Which of the following best describes employment and labor law compliance processes in your organization? (select the one that best applies).” In the original data, 2% of respondents stated that they “don’t know.” We removed those responses and recalculated the numbers, so this only shows percentages for those who answered the question. .

Compliance cohort classification

To take a closer look at what differentiates organizations with more mature compliance processes from those with less mature compliance processes, we separated our sample into two cohorts:

  • Compliance leaders: Those who are at the “proactive” stage of employment and labor law compliance maturity.
  • Compliance laggards: Those who are at the “reactive,” “formalized,” or “documented” stage of employment and labor law compliance maturity.

Of course, correlation is not the same as causation. While we cannot state that any particular practice will lead to success in managing legal compliance, we do see intriguing relationships that might result in greater success.

Finding: Over three-fifths of organizations are confident in compliance despite limited proactive maturity

About 61% of respondents report being either very or completely confident that their organization is in full compliance with employment and labor laws.

This level of confidence contrasts with the earlier finding that only 32% of organizations operate with proactive compliance practices that include monitoring and legal risk assessment. While 48% report being very confident and 13% completely confident, 30% remain only moderately confident, with a further 9% expressing low confidence in their compliance status.

“Being compliant on paper is not the same as being audit-ready.” — Emily Langdon

Taken together, these findings point to a potential confidence gap. Many organizations appear assured of their current compliance position despite relying on systematic or documentation-based approaches that may not fully account for emerging legal risks

“HR professionals often don’t know what they don’t know, which leads to overestimation of compliance readiness.” — Lara Makinen

Differences based on organization size

Confidence levels vary across organizational size. Midsize organizations (66%) are most likely to report being very or completely confident in their compliance status, compared to small (61%) and large organizations (54%). This may be because large organizations often deal with greater legal complexity due to larger or more geographically dispersed workforces.

Finding: Benefits and leave-related compliance top HR compliance concerns

HR’s legal compliance priorities show a shift this year, with benefits and leave-related compliance emerging as the most frequently cited area of concern at 63%. This marks a change from last year, when wage and hour laws ranked as the top compliance priority.

Employee classification and wage and hour compliance remain a leading concern at 44%, alongside privacy and data security requirements (43%). Anti-discrimination and EEO obligations also rank prominently at 41%, suggesting continued attention to workplace equity and fair employment practices.

Despite this, some regulatory areas receive comparatively less focus. Just 23% identify immigration-related compliance among their top concerns, and only 13% cite union and collective bargaining issues. Notably, 9% report no major compliance concerns across any of the listed areas.

In light of the earlier finding that many organizations rely on systematic rather than proactive compliance approaches, uneven attention across legal domains may increase exposure in areas that are monitored less consistently.

Finding: Pay equity and transparency requirements are now the most difficult U.S. employment laws to comply with

Pay equity and pay transparency requirements are cited by 44% of respondents as among the most difficult U.S. employment-related laws to comply with, followed closely by family and medical leave requirements at 40%.

Compliance challenges related to remote, hybrid, and multi-state work arrangements also rank prominently at 37%, reflecting the legal complexity introduced by a dispersed workforce.

Viewed alongside earlier findings that benefits and leave compliance is the most actively tracked legal area, these results suggest that frequently monitored domains may also be those where compliance complexity is most acutely experienced.

Finding: Most organizations feel prepared for compliance, but fewer than half report adequate funding

Perceived preparedness for labor and employment compliance remains strong, with 79% agreeing that their organization is well prepared to manage compliance concerns. This aligns with last year’s findings, suggesting continued confidence in internal compliance capabilities.

However, only 54% report that compliance-related initiatives are well funded, consistent with last year’s pattern in which about half of respondents indicated insufficient funding support. This gap between preparedness and resourcing appears to persist over time.

Nearly one-third (34%) report that their organization faced at least one employment-related enforcement action over the past year.

“Everyone's vulnerable to a compliance action even if you're totally compliant. You can still be challenged and the question is are you going to be able to withstand it or what the consequence will be?” — Robert Boonin

Taken together with earlier findings on limited proactive compliance maturity, the continued disconnect between perceived readiness and investment may contribute to ongoing enforcement risk.

Finding: Compliance leaders are better funded and less likely to face enforcement action

Most compliance leaders (95%) feel well prepared to deal with labor and employment compliance concerns, compared to just 58% of laggards. Greater access to funding may contribute to this gap, with 67% of leaders reporting that compliance-related initiatives are well funded, versus only 28% of laggards.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that compliance leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say their compliance-related initiatives are well-funded and they are well prepared to deal with labor and employment compliance concerns.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Move beyond documentation to risk planning. Many organizations have compliance processes in place but do not actively assess legal risks. In addition to maintaining policies, build a regular process to review where legal changes could create risk in areas such as pay practices, leave administration, or employee classification. Use these reviews to decide what needs to change before problems arise.
  • Make compliance a funded business priority. Organizations that invest in compliance initiatives are more likely to feel prepared and less likely to face enforcement actions. Include compliance-related tools, training, and staffing needs in annual planning rather than addressing them after issues emerge.
  • Balance attention across legal areas. HR teams often focus on the most visible compliance requirements while giving less attention to others. Review which employment laws are monitored closely, and which are not. Add lower-priority areas to internal audit or training plans to reduce future exposure.
  • Prepare for high-complexity requirements. Pay transparency, leave requirements, and multi-state work arrangements can be difficult to manage consistently. Provide managers with simple guidance or decision trees to ensure these requirements are applied the same way across teams.
  • Turn legal updates into operational changes. Staying informed about new regulations is not enough. When laws change, update policies, employee communications, and manager practices within a defined timeframe to make sure changes are implemented in day-to-day work.

Managing Compliance Knowledge and Resources

Finding: One in four organizations report difficulty keeping compliance knowledge up to date

Organizations report mixed effectiveness in maintaining an up-to-date understanding of employment compliance developments. About 23% rate their organization six or lower on a 10-point scale, indicating ongoing difficulty keeping pace with regulatory change. At the higher end, 59% rate their organization at eight or above, suggesting that most perceive their processes for tracking legal developments to be reasonably effective.

“With employment laws constantly evolving and expanding, staying fully up to date can be challenging. As a result, legal requirements are often addressed reactively rather than through proactive monitoring. In addition, policies and procedures are often created after an enforcement action has occurred.” — Lara Makinen

This distribution remains broadly consistent with last year’s findings, when a similar proportion reported challenges in keeping compliance knowledge current.

Without stronger integration between legal tracking and implementation processes, organizations may remain vulnerable to compliance gaps even when changes are identified.

Finding: Compliance leaders are over twice as likely as laggards to be confident in their compliance knowledge

A clear divide exists between compliance laggards and leaders in compliance knowledge. Among compliance laggards, 38% rate their organization's understanding of compliance developments as six or less out of 10, suggesting large gaps in staying current.

In contrast, compliance leaders rate their knowledge much higher, with 85% scoring eight or more out of 10. This stark contrast underscores the importance of investing in proactive compliance strategies to bridge the gap and reduce legal risks.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that compliance leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to rate up-to-date understanding of all employment compliance developments highly (8 to 10 on 10-point scale).

Finding: Nearly two-fifths of HR teams spend more than a quarter of their workweek on compliance

Compliance activities continue to represent a substantial operational demand for HR teams. About 42% report spending between 11% and 25% of the typical workweek addressing regulatory and legal compliance issues, consistent with last year’s findings.

An additional 23% spend between 26% and 50% of their time on compliance-related work, while 14% dedicate more than half of their workweek to these responsibilities.

For HR professionals, this may limit the time available for strategic workforce planning or development initiatives.

Finding: Most organizations audit their employment law compliance annually

Across all compliance categories, most organizations conduct audits at least annually, but the frequency varies by topic. Benefits and health plans are the most consistently reviewed area, with 65% conducting audits annually and another 10% reviewing more often than once per year.

Workplace safety also receives strong monitoring attention, with 28% auditing more often than once per year and 41% annually.

“Conducting regular wage and hour audits can 'eliminate or greatly lessen' exposure to liquidated damages.” — Robert Boonin

Finding: Compliance leaders audit more frequently across every regulatory area

Across all compliance areas, leaders report higher audit frequency, often at close to twice the rate of laggards.

  • Workplace safety: 39% vs. 18%, just over 2 times higher
  • Labor and employment laws: 33% vs. 18%, nearly 2 times higher
  • Non-discrimination laws: 28% vs. 13%, just over 2 times higher
  • Payroll and compensation: 26% vs. 13%, 2 times higher
  • Benefits and health plans: 14% vs. 8%, nearly 2 times higher

The largest proportional differences appear in safety, non-discrimination, and payroll, where exposure to legal and financial risk is typically higher. Even in benefits administration, where overall audit frequency is lower, leaders still maintain a clear advantage.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that compliance leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say they conduct compliance audits more often than annually.

Finding: Fewer than half report sufficient tools and budget for employment-law-related issues

Perceived access to compliance-related resources remains limited across organizations. Less than half report that key stakeholders receive sufficient tools and technologies (48%) or support such as budget (40%) to manage employment-law-related issues.

Education and training is rated somewhat more favorably, with 53% indicating sufficiency.

These levels are broadly consistent with last year’s findings, when resource adequacy also remained at or below the halfway mark across categories.

This continued shortfall in tools, training, and budget may limit the ability to translate preparedness into sustained compliance capability.

Finding: Compliance leaders are more likely than laggards to report greater access to tools, training, and budget

Compliance leaders are more likely than laggards to report that stakeholders receive sufficient tools and technologies (65% vs. 37%), education and training (80% vs. 36%), and budgetary support (64% vs. 30%).

Greater access to these resources may strengthen implementation of compliance processes across the organization.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that compliance leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say their organization provides sufficient amounts of listed resources to stakeholders.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Make compliance tracking part of your governance structure. Compliance should not rely on informal updates or individual effort. Set up a regular review process to monitor legal changes and assign clear responsibility for deciding what actions are required. Keep a shared tracker of required updates and review major risks with senior leaders so compliance becomes part of ongoing organizational oversight.
  • Establish a consistent audit schedule. Move away from reviewing compliance only when a problem arises. Create a clear audit calendar that covers key areas such as payroll, benefits, safety, and discrimination policies. Document findings, assign follow-up actions, and revisit them to ensure gaps are corrected. Regular audits help identify issues early rather than after complaints or enforcement actions occur.
  • Redesign compliance work to reduce HR overload. If compliance consumes a large portion of HR’s time, review how work is structured. Standardize recurring tasks, clarify responsibilities across the HR team, and use consistent workflows for areas like leave management and classification reviews. Reducing duplication and inconsistency frees up time for more strategic HR priorities.
  • Invest intentionally in compliance support. Confidence alone does not prevent risk. Ensure HR teams and managers have access to the tools, training, and budget needed to apply compliance requirements consistently. Consider whether staffing levels and resources reflect the complexity of your legal environment.
  • Turn legal knowledge into operational action. Staying informed about new laws is only the first step. When regulations change, update policies, revise forms, communicate changes to managers, and confirm that practices have been adjusted across departments. Build a simple process that ensures legal updates are consistently implemented, not just discussed.

Strengthening Compliance Through Technology and Analytics

Finding: A majority report using up-to-date compliance technologies

Adoption of modern compliance technologies remains uneven. A majority (62%) agree that their HR compliance technologies use up-to-date capabilities. A similar proportion (61%) agree that their compliance technologies ensure secure and safe processes. Fewer (56%) report that these tools are part of an integrated system, while only 49% indicate that key compliance processes are automated.

Limited automation and integration may help explain why many organizations continue to devote substantial time to compliance activities without corresponding improvements in proactive compliance maturity.

Finding: Compliance leaders are more likely than laggards to use up-to-date, secure, integrated and automated technologies

Compliance leaders are more likely than laggards to report that their technologies automate key compliance processes (70% vs. 50%) and are part of an integrated system (67% vs. 21%).

Leaders are also more likely to report using up-to-date compliance technologies (73% vs. 50%). This may improve their ability to track legal changes in real time and reduce manual compliance workload.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that compliance leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say their compliance-related technologies are secure and safe, part of an integrated system, are up-to-date and highly automated.

Finding: Only two-fifths say they can quickly generate useful compliance reports

Reporting and analytics capabilities related to HR compliance remain limited. Just 41% agree that it is quick and easy to create useful compliance-focused reports, and only 38% indicate that their reporting and analytics capabilities produce actionable insights. These proportions are broadly consistent with last year’s findings, suggesting little progress in strengthening compliance-related reporting over time.

Gaps in reporting and analytics may reduce the ability to translate compliance data into timely risk mitigation or policy adjustments.

Finding: Compliance leaders are roughly twice as likely as laggards to generate useful and actionable compliance insights

Three-fifths of compliance leaders (59%) agree that it is quick and easy to create useful compliance-focused reports, compared to just 26% of laggards.

Similarly, 55% of leaders report that their reporting and analytics capabilities produce actionable insights, versus 25% of laggards.

This may enable earlier identification of compliance risks and more timely policy or process adjustments.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that compliance leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say their HR-compliance-related reporting and analytics enable quick and easy to create useful compliance-focused reports and result in meaningful insights.

Finding: The most common technological improvement expected in the next two years is incorporation of more AI

Organizations anticipate continued investment in compliance-related technologies over the next two years, with 42% expecting to incorporate artificial intelligence into their compliance tools or processes. This represents a notable increase from 22% in 2024 and 10% in 2023.

Other commonly expected changes include increasing employee self-service (36%), upgrading or replacing outdated technologies (39%), and improving the employee user experience (34%).

Finding: AI is expected most to improve compliance data analysis and monitoring

Organizations most often expect AI to influence their legal and compliance processes by easing the gathering and analysis of compliance-related data (47%) and enhancing the ability to monitor legal compliance, such as pay equity (43%). A further 39% anticipate the need to develop new guardrails and guidelines related to AI usage, while the same proportion expect improvements in the quality or accuracy of compliance-related information.

About one-third identify anticipated impacts on bias monitoring (36%), HR technological competencies (38%), and legal contract review (33%). As organizations anticipate greater use of AI to support monitoring and data analysis, parallel investments in governance and workforce capabilities may be required to manage emerging risks.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Develop a clear compliance technology roadmap. Rather than making isolated technology upgrades, create a multi-year plan for improving automation, integration, and reporting. Identify where manual compliance tasks create delays or errors and prioritize systems that reduce duplication and improve visibility across payroll, benefits, and workforce management.
  • Improve system integration before adding new tools. Fragmented systems limit visibility and increase HR workload. Focus on integrating compliance-related systems so data flows consistently across HR, payroll, and reporting platforms. Strong integration reduces manual reconciliation and improves accuracy.
  • Strengthen compliance reporting and analytics capability. Compliance data should support decision-making, not just recordkeeping. Define the key compliance metrics your organization needs, build standardized dashboards, and ensure HR leaders can quickly generate reports that highlight emerging risks or inconsistencies.
  • Use AI to enhance monitoring, not replace oversight. As AI adoption increases, apply it strategically to improve data analysis, pattern detection, and compliance monitoring. At the same time, establish clear governance guidelines for how AI tools are used, reviewed, and validated to reduce bias and protect employee privacy.
  • Invest intentionally in compliance technology skills and governance. Technology improvements require capable users. Provide HR teams with training on data interpretation, AI-enabled tools, and system capabilities. Establish clear policies for data security, privacy protection, and AI oversight to ensure technology adoption strengthens rather than weakens compliance control.

Managing Legal Risk in a Complex Regulatory Environment

Finding: Almost half plan to increase the importance of legal compliance over the next two years

Nearly half of respondents (48%) agree or strongly agree that their organization will place greater importance on legal compliance in the next two years, while only 10% disagree or strongly disagree. However, the largest segment (42%) remains neutral.

This distribution suggests that most organizations do not anticipate reducing their focus on compliance, but many are also not signaling a significant strategic shift.

Finding: Legal action remains the most significant concern associated with non-compliance

Legal action or lawsuits continue to rank as the top concern related to failing to comply with employment laws and regulations, cited by 58% of respondents. Financial penalties or fines (45%) and reputational damage (43%) follow as the next most frequently identified risks.

Operational disruption, increased employee turnover and concerns related to personal liability are each cited by 23%, while a quarter are concerned about a decrease in employee morale or engagement. These results are broadly consistent with last year’s findings, indicating that financial and reputational consequences continue to drive compliance priorities.

Finding: Evolving legal interpretations and jurisdictional fragmentation remain the primary barriers to compliance readiness

Changing interpretations of employment laws (36%) and fragmentation between federal and state requirements (34%) continue to rank as the most commonly cited challenges in keeping up with HR compliance-related changes. The growing scope of HR-related mandates follows at 34%.

Over a quarter (28%) also identify managing a geographically dispersed workforce as a key difficulty, while 26% point to the pace of regulatory change. Resource-related barriers remain present, with 24% citing lack of budget and 20% noting insufficient HR expertise or personnel. Persistent legal complexity, combined with resource constraints, may continue to limit organizations’ ability to maintain up-to-date compliance knowledge.

“It is actually impossible to comply with the employment laws in all jurisdictions at the same time because you'll have conflicting state laws or state laws require one thing, and the federal laws requires something else.” — Bryn Goodman

Finding: Most organizations rely on reading and advisory support rather than technology to stay informed about changes to laws and compliance

Organizations most often report staying abreast of compliance-related legal changes by reading relevant materials (63%) and joining membership organizations that track regulatory updates (58%). More than half also consult with outside counsel (55%), while about half conduct online research (48%) or subscribe to compliance-related services (44%).

Fewer rely on internal experts (26%) or HR compliance technology solutions (28%), and only 19% report using AI-enabled compliance services or tools. This reliance on informational and advisory sources is consistent with earlier findings showing limited adoption of integrated compliance technologies and reporting tools. Continued dependence on manual or externally guided monitoring approaches may contribute to the time burden associated with compliance activities and limit progress toward proactive compliance maturity.

Finding: Compliance leaders are more likely to leverage technology and internal expertise

Compliance leaders are more likely than laggards to use HR compliance technology solutions (37% vs. 24%) and consult with internal experts (37% vs. 13%) to stay informed of legal changes. This may reduce reliance on external or manual information sources and improve consistency in applying regulatory updates.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider these suggestions:

  • Move from reactive monitoring to risk-based planning. Legal action and fines are major concerns, yet many organizations rely on reading materials or outside counsel to stay informed. Create an internal process to identify which employment laws present the highest risk and review how they are managed across departments. This helps HR teams focus efforts on areas that could lead to legal or financial exposure.
  • Strengthen internal compliance expertise. Relying only on external advisors may delay action. Identify HR staff who can serve as internal compliance contacts and provide them with training on key regulatory areas such as pay practices, leave management, and workforce classification. Internal expertise can improve consistency in applying legal updates.
  • Coordinate compliance across locations. Fragmentation between federal and state requirements can create confusion, especially in dispersed workforces. Work with legal and operational leaders to review how compliance requirements differ across locations and ensure policies are applied consistently.
  • Use technology to support legal tracking. Manual monitoring of legal updates increases the time burden on HR teams. Evaluate whether compliance tracking tools or integrated systems could help streamline updates and reduce reliance on informal information sources.
  • Increase leadership visibility into compliance risks. Compliance may receive less attention when leadership is not aware of emerging risks. Share summaries of regulatory changes, audit findings, or compliance challenges with senior leaders so resource needs can be addressed proactively.

Key Takeaways

1. Expand proactive compliance practices.

Only 32% of organizations report operating with proactive monitoring and risk assessment practices. Implement structured legal risk reviews to anticipate regulatory exposure before issues arise by scheduling regular risk discussions, assigning ownership for reviewing legal changes, and updating policies, procedures, and manager guidance within defined timelines.

2. Validate compliance confidence through formal checks.

While 61% report being very or completely confident in their compliance status, many lack proactive processes. Conduct regular internal audits, track implementation of legal updates, review documentation for accuracy, and confirm that changes have been applied consistently across departments.

3. Align compliance investments with legal risk areas.

Legal action or lawsuits (58%) and financial penalties or fines (45%) remain the top concerns. Prioritize compliance resources in areas such as pay practices, leave administration, and workforce classification by updating processes, improving documentation, and providing targeted training to HR staff and managers.

4. Allocate sufficient funding for compliance initiatives.

Although 79% feel prepared, only 54% say compliance initiatives are well funded. Integrate compliance-related tools, training, and staffing needs into annual budget planning and ensure resources are available to support audits, policy updates, and training activities.

5. Formalize how legal updates are tracked and applied.

About a quarter rate their organization’s ability to stay current at six or lower out of 10. Establish a defined process for reviewing legal updates, sharing them with relevant stakeholders, updating HR policies and forms, and communicating operational changes to managers.

6. Benchmark compliance practices against leaders.

Compliance leaders are more likely to audit frequently and invest in tools and budget. Strengthen governance, increase audit frequency, and standardize processes across business units to improve consistency in applying compliance requirements.

7. Reduce the time HR spends on compliance tasks.

Nearly two-fifths of HR teams spend more than a quarter of their workweek on compliance. Standardize workflows, create templates for recurring tasks, clarify responsibilities across HR roles, and automate repetitive compliance activities where possible.

8. Modernize compliance technologies.

Many (62%) report using up-to-date compliance technologies. Develop a roadmap to improve automation, integrate systems, reduce manual data entry, and improve visibility into compliance activities across HR processes.

9. Improve compliance reporting capabilities.

Only 41% can quickly generate useful compliance reports. Define key compliance metrics, create dashboards for monitoring, and ensure HR leaders can generate timely reports to support risk mitigation decisions.

10. Prepare for increased AI use in compliance.

About 42% expect to incorporate AI into compliance tools within the next two years. Establish governance guidelines, provide training for HR staff, and implement safeguards to manage data privacy and bias concerns.
