HR.com’s Future of Career Development and Mobility 2026-27

Executive Summary

Career development is improving, but most organizations still lack the systems to make it effective. Only 27% have reached strategic or innovative maturity, and just 29% say development opportunities meet employee demand. Organizations encourage managers to develop their teams, but few hold them accountable for doing so. While 63% encourage managers to support employee growth, only 23% formally hold them accountable, and just 12% reward or recognize these efforts.

The good news is that organizations are making clear progress this year. The share of companies reaching the strategic and innovative levels of our maturity model rose to 27%, up from 17% in last year’s survey. The proportion of organizations stuck in purely ad hoc setups dropped by nearly half. At the same time, about half of HR professionals now rate their career development and internal mobility processes as effective. More employers are also expanding the number of career opportunities they offer (47%).

However, serious roadblocks remain. More than two-fifths admit their organization’s career opportunities cannot keep up with employee demand. The biggest issue is not a lack of interest from employees but how companies are structured. A lack of career paths for workers who do not want to manage people remains the top barrier to internal movement, while 35% of employers still do not offer career development to everyone.

Closing these gaps separates leading organizations from the rest. Career development leaders1 treat career growth and internal mobility as core business functions supported by clear pathways, manager coaching, modern technology, and measurable outcomes.

Over the next two years, successful organizations will strengthen manager coaching, establish clear career pathways, and use digital tools, including artificial intelligence (AI), to build a skilled workforce ready to adapt to change.

1 Career development leaders are organizations rated “Strategic” or “Innovative” on the career development maturity model, while career development laggards are those rated “Beginning” or “Advancing.” These labels reflect maturity levels, not overall organizational performance.

About the Survey

The HR Research Institute (HRRI) fielded the Future of Career Development and Mobility 2026 survey in mid-2026. We gathered responses from 152 HR professionals across a wide range of industries, company sizes, and job levels.

Respondents are located around the world but predominantly from North America. Participants span diverse industries, with 80% coming from midsized or large organizations.

Defining career development and internal mobility

For the purpose of this study and the survey on which it is based, we defined career development and internal mobility as follows.

  • Career development refers to helping employees build their skills through training, education, and on-the-job experience, usually with the goal of reaching a specific personal or professional milestone.
  • Internal mobility refers to employees moving to new jobs, roles, and project assignments within the same organization to support their career growth. These moves include both permanent role changes and short-term projects.

Major Research Findings

1. Career development maturity is improving, but most organizations still lack a fully strategic approach.

  • The proportion of organizations at the strategic or innovative stages of career development maturity increased to 27% in 2026, up from 17% in 2025. However, 49% remain at the beginning or advancing stages, where career development is largely informal or lacks a structured approach.
  • The share of organizations at the beginning stage declined from 30% in 2025 to 16% in 2026, while the proportion at the innovative stage more than doubled from 4% to 9%.
  • Reported effectiveness has improved alongside maturity. In 2026, 52% of HR professionals agree that their organization has an effective career development process, up from 39% in 2025. Similarly, 49% report effective internal mobility processes, compared with 39% the previous year.

2. Career opportunities still fall short of employee demand, and resources remain flat.

  • Only 29% of HR professionals agree that career development opportunities in their organization meet employee demand, although this has improved from 22% in 2025. Meanwhile, 42% disagree that available opportunities meet demand.
  • Organizations are expanding opportunities, but investment remains constrained. While 47% report increasing the number of career opportunities over the past two years, 55% say the time devoted to career development remained unchanged over the past year.
  • Identifying and addressing skills gaps remains a significant challenge. Only 21% of organizations report doing this effectively to a high or very high degree, while 34% report low or very low effectiveness.

3. Internal mobility remains constrained by unclear career paths and limited formal processes.

  • Although 55% of organizations prioritize internal candidates for open positions, only 27% report having a formal internal mobility process, and just 19% regularly track internal mobility metrics.
  • The most frequently reported obstacles to internal mobility are the lack of career paths for employees who do not want to become managers (46%) and insufficient dedicated development time (36%).
  • These gaps suggest that prioritizing internal candidates is not enough on its own. Organizations also need transparent transfer procedures, visible career pathways, and opportunities for employees to build the skills required for new roles.

4. Manager accountability for career development remains weak, despite widespread encouragement to support employees.

  • Although 63% of organizations encourage managers to develop their employees, only 23% formally hold managers accountable for doing so, and just 12% offer tangible rewards or recognition.
  • Only 23% of HR professionals believe managers have the coaching skills and resources needed to support employee growth. Furthermore, only 32% say HR has a clear understanding of employee skills, and 29% track development-related metrics.
  • Career development therefore remains heavily dependent on individual managers’ willingness and ability to help employees, rather than being consistently reinforced through organizational expectations, resources, and accountability.

5. Career development practices remain concentrated in familiar processes, and access is not universal.

  • Goal setting and performance reviews remain the most common development practice, used by 56% of organizations.
  • More structured approaches remain less common, such as written Individual Development Plans (36%), cross-functional project opportunities (32%), career path training (31%), and standardized skill guides (23%).
  • Although 65% of organizations report offering career development to all employees, 35% do not. Where access is restricted, programs are more likely to target managers and executives (51%) and technical or professional employees (44%) than frontline and operational workers (30%) or entry-level employees (30%).

6. Career development technology is advancing, but organizations still rely primarily on foundational tools.

  • Learning management systems (55%) and performance management systems (53%) are the most widely used career development technologies. By comparison, dedicated career management systems are used by 21% of organizations, skills assessment tools by 17%, and talent mobility platforms by just 8%.
  • AI-powered learning tools have gained ground in the past year, with adoption increasing from 12% in 2025 to 25% in 2026. Nevertheless, AI use in career development remains uneven, and more advanced applications such as personalized career guidance, skills identification, and internal job matching have considerable room to develop.
  • Technology effectiveness remains a concern. Only 36% of HR professionals rate their career development technologies as good or excellent, while 46% rate them as merely adequate. These findings highlight the need to connect technology investments to specific development needs and measurable outcomes.

7. Career development leaders consistently outperform less mature organizations across key practices and outcomes.

  • Compared with career development laggards, career development leaders are:
    • more than 8X more likely to identify and address employee skills gaps to a high degree
    • nearly 4X more likely to report effective career development processes
    • more than 5X more likely to have a formal internal mobility process
    • more than twice as likely to describe their career development approach as equitable and inclusive
    • more than twice as likely to personalize career development to individual employees
    • more likely to have increased both the time and financial resources devoted to career development over the previous year

Career Development Maturity

Finding: Career development maturity is rising, but half of organizations still lack formal structure

We asked respondents to assess their organizations using the HRRI Career Development Maturity Model. Progress is evident, with organizations at the beginning stage, where programs are nonexistent or informal, declining from 30% in 2025 to 16% in 2026. However, 33% remain at the advancing stage, where managers support career growth without a structured program, and 25% are at the defined stage, where career development is incorporated into standard performance review processes.

More organizations are integrating career development into broader talent strategies. The proportion at the strategic stage rose from 13% to 18%, while those at the innovative stage, where career mobility is embedded in long-term workforce planning, more than doubled from 4% to 9%. Overall, 27% now operate at these two highest maturity levels, up from 17% in 2025. Despite this progress, nearly half (49%) remain in the two earliest stages, indicating that structured career development is still lacking in many organizations.

The challenge extends beyond identifying employees with potential to understanding their readiness for future roles. Kathi Graham, CEO of XBInsight, highlighted this distinction:

“

“The gap: Identifying who could advance versus understanding who is ready, when they will be ready, and what it will take to get them there.”

- Kathi Graham, CEO, XBInsight

For the purpose of this report, large organizations have 1,000 or more employees, midsized organizations have 100 to 999 employees, and small organizations have 99 or fewer employees.

Differences based on organization size

Large organizations have an advantage in reaching higher maturity. Nearly one-third (32%) of large employers report reaching the strategic or innovative stages, compared to 28% of small organizations and 21% of midsized organizations. Dedicated HR teams and established systems may help large employers, while smaller organizations often face limited HR capacity and competing operational demands.

Career development-based cohorts

What differentiates organizations at higher levels of career development maturity from others? To examine this question, we divided our data set into two cohorts based on how respondents rated themselves on the career development maturity model presented in the previous finding. They are:

Career development leaders: those who responded “Strategic” or “Innovative” to the question, “How would you describe career development in your organization?”

Career development laggards: those who responded “Beginning” or “Advancing” to the same question.

Correlation does not necessarily indicate causation, of course, but these relationships may provide clues about possible best practices in the career development space.

Finding: Process effectiveness improves, with about half of organizations reporting effective systems

Organizations show clear progress in managing career development and internal mobility compared with last year. In 2026, 52% of HR professionals agree or strongly agree that their organization has an effective process for managing career development, up from 39% in 2025. Similarly, 49% now report having an effective process for internal mobility, compared to 39% in the prior year.

Despite these gains, 29% rate both career development and internal mobility processes as ineffective, while roughly one-fifth remain neutral. Formal structures are becoming more common, but consistent execution remains a challenge.

Finding: Career development leaders are nearly four times more likely to report effective processes

Career development leaders report substantially stronger execution. Almost all leaders (98%) agree or strongly agree that their organization has an effective process for managing career development, compared to only 25% of laggards.

The difference is just as large for internal mobility. Fully 92% of leaders report having an effective internal mobility process, compared to just 26% of laggards.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than the laggards to report effective career development and internal mobility processes.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, below are some suggestions:

  • Formalize career development through clear career frameworks.
    Nearly half of organizations remain at the Beginning or Advancing stages of career development maturity. Establish clear career pathways, define progression requirements, and assign implementation responsibilities to reduce reliance on informal, manager-dependent practices.
  • Connect career development with workforce planning.
    Review emerging business priorities, anticipated skills needs, and succession requirements to identify the capabilities employees will need in the future. Using this information to guide career pathways and development priorities can help prepare internal talent for changing workforce demands.
  • Set consistent standards for career development across departments.
    HR teams can establish minimum expectations for career planning, employee access to development, and manager support while allowing departments to adapt activities to their operational needs. This can reduce inconsistencies in how employees experience career development across the organization.
  • Build a regular process for reviewing career development effectiveness.
    HR teams can periodically assess the clarity of career pathways, employee participation, skill development, and internal progression. Establishing improvement targets and reviewing results can help organizations identify gaps and strengthen their career development practices over time.

Advancing Career Opportunities

Finding: Fewer than three in ten organizations have enough development opportunities to meet employee demand

Career development opportunities continue to fall short of employee demand. In 2026, only 29% of HR professionals agree or strongly agree that career development opportunities in their organization meet employee demand. In contrast, 42% disagree or strongly disagree, and 29% remain neutral.

While this is an improvement over 2025, when only 22% agreed and 49% disagreed, the shortfall is still large. Limited growth opportunities can frustrate employees and encourage them to seek external roles. Clear, accessible career pathways can support retention.

Finding: More organizations are expanding career opportunities, but day-to-day budgets stay flat

Encouragingly, employers are offering more ways to grow than they did in past years. Nearly half of organizations (47%) increased the number of internal career opportunities offered over the last two years. This is a strong turnaround from 2025, when only 22% reported an increase. Meanwhile, 44% kept offerings flat, and only 10% made cuts.

However, reported investments of time and money tell a more cautious story. Over the past year, 55% of organizations kept employee development time flat, and 50% kept financial budgets unchanged. About one-third reported increases in learning time (33%) and budgets (35%), while 12% cut development hours and 15% reduced spending.

While more organizations are expanding career development opportunities, the actual resources to support it are expanding at a much slower pace.

Leader-laggard differences

Leaders are more likely to have increased internal career opportunities offered over the last two years when compared to laggards (47% vs. 32%).

Finding: Career development leaders are far more likely to increase time and funding for career development

Career development leaders put real resources behind their priorities. Over the past year, 55% of leaders increased the time employees spend on career development, compared to only 16% of laggards.

The story is the same for funding. Nearly half of leaders (47%) increased their career development budgets, compared to just 20% of laggards.

Organizations with more mature career development practices understand that building an adaptable workforce requires dedicated development time and modern tools.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to have increased the time and financial resources devoted to career development over the last year.

Finding: Only one in five organizations effectively identify and address employee skills gaps

Limited visibility into skills gaps may undermine career development efforts. Only 21% of HR professionals report that their organization effectively spots and fixes employee skills gaps to a high or very high degree. The largest share, 45%, do so only moderately, while 34% say their efforts are low or very low.

This is a modest step forward from 2025, when only 14% reported high success and 41% reported low effectiveness.

Addressing this challenge requires more than identifying missing skills. Development plans also need to reflect employees’ career goals and the requirements of future roles.

“

“Moving from generic development plans to targeted development based on where employees want to go and what those roles require.”

- Kathi Graham, CEO, XBInsight

Differences based on organization size

Small organizations show higher confidence in spotting skills gaps, with 25% reporting high or very high effectiveness, compared to 20% in large organizations and 19% in midsized employers. It is possible that in smaller companies, leaders work closely with team members every day, making it much easier to notice skill gaps without complicated evaluation forms.

Finding: Career development leaders are more than eight times more likely to address skills gaps effectively

Leaders substantially outperform laggards in identifying and addressing skills gaps. Over half of career development leaders (51%) effectively find and fix skills gaps to a high or very high degree, compared to just 6% of laggards.

Among laggards, 57% rate their effectiveness at identifying and addressing skills gaps as low or very low. High-performing organizations are more likely to avoid this problem, perhaps by evaluating core skills regularly and using those insights to plan future hiring and training.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than the laggards to say their organization effectively identifies and addresses employee skills gaps.

Finding: Mentorship, online learning, and cross-training are the most common ways to close skill gaps

Organizations most often address skills gaps through mentoring and coaching (54%), online learning (53%), and cross-training programs at 49%.

Other common approaches include informal learning on the job (47%), paying for outside degree or certificate programs (45%), and hiring experienced people from outside (45%). However, more structured internal methods remain underused. Only 38% create succession plans, 33% use formal skill tests, 27% run internal skill-matching tools, and 25% offer temporary job rotations.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, below are some suggestions:

  • Identify priority skills gaps before allocating development resources.
    Few organizations report identifying and addressing employee skill gaps to a high or very high degree. HR teams can combine employee self-assessments, manager input, and role-specific competency requirements to identify the most important gaps. Comparing these findings with anticipated workforce needs can help organizations direct limited training resources toward capabilities that matter most.
  • Create more development opportunities without relying solely on promotions.
    Career opportunities often do not meet employee demand. Employers can expand access to cross-functional projects, job shadowing, temporary assignments, and cross-training. These options allow employees to build skills and explore different career directions even when permanent vacancies are limited.
  • Protect time for employees to develop new skills.
    Lack of time remains a persistent barrier to career development. Managers can schedule learning activities during working hours, incorporate development assignments into team plans, and adjust workloads when operational demands repeatedly prevent employees from participating.
  • Combine formal learning with practical application.
    Organizations commonly use mentoring and coaching, online learning, and cross-training to address skill gaps, but they use more structured approaches, such as temporary job rotations, less often. Organizations can pair courses and coaching with work-based assignments that allow employees to demonstrate new capabilities. Managers can then assess progress and identify further development needs.
  • Evaluate whether internal development can address emerging skill needs.
    Before recruiting externally for a skills-intensive position, organizations can assess whether current employees could acquire the required capabilities through targeted learning, mentoring, certifications, or stretch assignments. Comparing the time and resources required with external recruitment can help employers make informed workforce decisions and create additional career opportunities for existing employees.

Internal Mobility and Career Progression

Finding: Most organizations look to internal talent first, but formal internal mobility rules remain rare

Research suggests that promoting and moving people internally is great for employee morale. A strong majority of HR professionals (58%) say internal moves are common in their organizations, and 55% state that existing employees get first priority for open positions. In addition, 41% say executive leaders actively encourage employees to make lateral career moves.

However, formal processes and systems to support internal moves remain uncommon. Only 27% of companies have a formal internal mobility process, and just 27% use software to help manage internal transfers. Even worse, only 19% of employers regularly track and report internal mobility numbers. Without clear ground rules and tracking, internal hiring might come down to office politics and manager favoritism.

Finding: Leaders are more than five times more likely than laggards to have a formal process for internal mobility

Leaders make internal career transitions more structured and predictable. More than half of career development leaders (54%) maintain a formal internal mobility process, compared to only 10% of laggards.

Leaders are also three times more likely to measure internal movement regularly (36% vs. 11%), and three-and-a-half times more likely to use software that supports internal moves (56% vs. 16%). Furthermore, leaders are nearly ten times more likely to reward managers who help their team members advance into new roles (28% vs. 3%). By tracking internal transfers and rewarding supportive managers, leaders keep talent flowing freely across the company.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say the listed statements regarding development related practices are true in their organizations.

Finding: Job postings and employee referrals dominate internal mobility technology

Among the 27% of organizations using software to support internal mobility, basic tools do most of the work. Almost three-quarters (73%) provide digital postings of open jobs, 70% use employee referral systems, and 62% use an internal mobility platform.

Smarter matching tools are starting to appear, but they are still rare. Only 27% use dynamic skill-matching platforms, and just 19% use predictive analytics software.

Finding: Lack of non-management career paths and role shortages are the top barriers to internal movement

Structural problems inside companies continue to block employee movement. The single largest hurdle, cited by 46% of HR professionals, is a lack of career paths for workers who do not want to become managers. When advancement requires people management, specialists may leave their current organizations to progress in their careers. Parallel technical and management tracks allow employees to advance without changing career paths.

“

“When traditional promotions are limited, employees can still grow through increased responsibility, new skills, broader business exposure, and opportunities to contribute in different ways.”

- Kathi Graham, CEO, XBInsight

The second biggest obstacle is a shortage of open roles or hiring freezes (36%). An equal 36% say employees simply do not have enough dedicated time during the workday to learn new skills. Other common friction points include tight budgets (26%), unclear benefits of switching roles (16%), and employees preferring to stay where they are (16%). The share citing managers who block transfers fell from 30% in 2025 to 10% in 2026, suggesting greater openness to internal mobility despite limited formal pathways.

Differences based on organization size

The top roadblock changes depending on company size. For large (52%) and midsized (47%) employers, the biggest issue is the lack of career paths for non-managers. In small businesses, the main challenge is simply having no open roles to move people into (48%), which reflects smaller team sizes and fewer vacancies.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, below are some suggestions:

  • Develop dual-track career paths for individual specialists.
    Organizations can establish parallel technical and management career tracks, with defined progression levels, competency requirements, and compensation opportunities. This gives specialists a way to advance their expertise and influence without taking on people management responsibilities.
  • Establish transparent guidelines for internal transfers.
    Organizations can publish clear eligibility requirements, application procedures, selection criteria, and expected timelines for internal hiring. Communicating these guidelines through a centralized careers portal can make opportunities more accessible and help reduce perceptions of favoritism.
  • Track internal mobility indicators regularly.
    HR teams can monitor the proportion of vacancies filled internally, transfers across departments, time to fill roles internally, and employee progression across job levels. Analyze these measures by department to identify barriers and evaluate mobility initiatives.
  • Use project assignments to support growth when vacancies are limited.
    Managers can offer stretch assignments, cross-functional projects, job shadowing, and temporary rotations when promotion opportunities are scarce. These experiences help employees build transferable skills and explore career interests while preparing for future openings.
  • Use automated alerts to improve access to internal opportunities.
    HR teams can configure internal job portals to notify employees about relevant vacancies based on their skills, experience, and stated career interests. Where advanced matching technology is unavailable, employees could begin by subscribing to role-specific alerts or joining internal talent pools.

Career Development Culture

Finding: Companies encourage managers to support growth, but few offer rewards or accountability

Despite widespread support for career development, manager accountability remains weak. Nearly two-thirds of HR professionals (63%) say managers in their organization are encouraged to develop their teams. In addition, 49% agree that HR and managers work together to foster a culture of continuous learning.

However, this encouragement rarely comes with real accountability. Only 23% of companies hold managers formally accountable for developing their team members, and only 12% offer tangible recognition or bonuses for doing so. Furthermore, only 23% of HR leaders believe their managers have the skills and resources needed to help employees grow.

Workforce skills data is also limited. Only 32% of HR leaders report a clear understanding of employee skills, and 29% track development metrics. Without training, incentives, and accountability, managerial encouragement may not translate into effective coaching. Competing operational demands can further push career development down the priority list.

“

“Managers are often focused on meeting business goals and managing day-to-day responsibilities, so career development can become secondary.”

- Kathi Graham, CEO, XBInsight

Finding: A third of leaders hold managers accountable for developing their employees

Managers are encouraged to develop their teams in 74% of career development leader organizations. In 66% of these organizations, HR and managers foster learning and innovation, and in 50%, HR knows employee talents and skills.

Accountability is another area of difference. Thirty-two percent of career development leaders say managers are held accountable for developing employees, compared with 18% of career development laggards. Managers in leader organizations are also more likely to have the skills needed to develop employees (42% vs. 10%). In addition, leader organizations are more likely to reward managers for developing employees (26% vs. 3%) and to track development-related metrics (53% vs. 13%).

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to say the listed statements regarding HR and managers are true in their organizations.

Finding: More than half say employees are expected to figure out their own careers

A majority of HR professionals (54%) say employees are primarily responsible for navigating their own career growth. Employee initiative matters, but without clear pathways and managerial support, self-directed development can widen inequalities.

Additionally, only 25% of organizations personalize career planning for individual workers, and just 24% describe their development programs as fair and inclusive. Further, only 25% formally require managers to help employees grow, and regular career conversations take place in only 17% of organizations. However, career management should be a shared responsibility between employees and organizations, as organizational support plays an important role in effective career self-management.

Finding: Managers in leader organizations are more than four times more likely to be required to help employees develop their careers

In leading organizations, employee development is an explicit management responsibility. More than half of leaders (53%) formally require managers to help direct reports develop their careers, compared to only 12% of laggards.

Furthermore, leaders are more than three times more likely to have senior executives who actively champion a development culture (68% vs. 20%), and five times more likely to ensure regular career discussions take place (37% vs. 7%). Leaders are also more likely to personalize development plans (40% vs. 16%) and maintain fair and inclusive development practices (34% vs. 13%).

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to cultivate an active, inclusive development culture backed by leadership and manager expectations.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, we have the following suggestions:

  • Make employee development part of managerial performance.
    Incorporate career development goals into manager performance reviews. Track the frequency of career conversations, completion of employee development plans, and support for internal mobility. Recognize and reward managers who develop talent and help employees advance.
  • Equip managers with practical coaching skills.
    Train managers through coaching workshops, conversation guides, and development templates. Help them discuss employee aspirations, identify skill gaps, and agree on concrete development actions. Reinforce these skills through follow-up sessions and practical exercises.
  • Establish regular, structured career conversations.
    Require managers to hold dedicated career discussions several times a year, separate from performance and compensation reviews. Give managers simple discussion guides to help employees identify career interests, explore potential pathways, and agree on specific development actions.
  • Combine employee ownership with organizational support.
    Encourage employees to identify their career goals while asking managers to provide guidance, feedback, and access to relevant opportunities. Use individual development plans to document goals, required skills, learning activities, responsibilities, and follow-up dates.
  • Use workforce skills data to personalize development fairly.
    Maintain skills inventories that combine employee self-assessments, manager input, and demonstrated competencies. Review development opportunities and outcomes across teams and employee groups to identify overlooked talent, reduce reliance on informal manager nominations, and direct learning resources toward employees’ actual needs.
  • Secure visible leadership support for career development.
    Senior leaders can communicate clear expectations for developing talent, review progress during talent and workforce planning meetings, and recognize managers who support employee growth across departmental boundaries. This helps position career development as an organizational responsibility rather than an optional activity left to individual managers.

Career Development Practices and Access

Finding: Goal and performance review loops lead career development practices

Organizations continue to rely on familiar, review-driven development practices. The standard performance review and goal-setting loop remains the most common development practice, used by 56% of companies. Promoting a continuous learning culture follows at 50%, with structured mentoring and coaching used by 43%.

Written Individual Development Plans are used by 36% of employers, while 32% offer cross-team project assignments. Other formal practices are far less common. Under a third offer career path training (31%), succession planning (27%), formal skill tests (27%), and standardized skill guides (23%). Skill-based rewards remain rare, utilized by only 15% of companies. These results suggest that development remains concentrated in formal review processes rather than embedded in everyday work.

Finding: Career development leaders are far more likely to use written growth plans, skill assessments, and rewards

Career development leaders provide a much broader set of tools to help employees advance. Leaders are more than twice as likely to use written Individual Development Plans (50% vs. 20%), and nearly five times more likely to run regular skill reviews and gap checks (47% vs. 10%).

The difference is even more dramatic for career path training and tangible incentives. Leaders are six times more likely to offer training on career pathways (61% vs. 10%), and eleven times more likely to offer skill-based bonuses or raises (34% vs. 3%). By offering clear roadmaps and real rewards, leaders give employees strong reasons to learn and improve.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are more likely than laggards to deploy structured developmental practices across all categories.

Finding: Over a third of employers still do not offer career development to all employees

Fair access to career programs remains an ongoing concern in many workplaces. While 65% of HR professionals say their company offers career development to all workers, over a third (35%) do not.

In companies where career development is not open to everyone, programs heavily favor managers and executives (51%). Technical and professional staff follow closely at 44%.

Frontline and operational workers are included in only 30% of these selective programs, and entry-level new hires are included in just 30%. Dedicated development for underrepresented employee groups is virtually nonexistent, operating in only 7% of restricted programs.

Restricting access to career development can undermine morale, overlook internal talent, narrow the future leadership pipeline, and leave frontline employees feeling undervalued.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, here are a few suggestions:

  • Extend career development opportunities across the workforce.
    HR teams can review eligibility criteria and identify groups that have limited access, particularly frontline, operational, and entry-level employees. Flexible schedules, mobile-friendly resources, peer mentoring, and paid learning time can make development more accessible across roles.
  • Move beyond annual performance reviews as the main development mechanism.
    Employers can supplement annual reviews with regular development check-ins and documented action plans. This helps employees and managers revisit career goals, assess progress, and adjust development activities as needs change.
  • Make career pathways and progression criteria visible.
    Organizations can document potential career moves, the competencies required for different roles, and the learning experiences that can prepare employees for progression. Making this information accessible across job levels can help employees understand their options and identify practical steps toward future roles, including lateral moves and specialist career paths.
  • Connect development activities to demonstrated skills and career progress.
    Organizations can use development plans to record priority skills, agreed learning activities, and opportunities to apply new capabilities. Managers can review whether employees have demonstrated those skills and discuss appropriate next steps. This creates a clearer connection between participation in development programs and employees' ability to take on new responsibilities or pursue different roles.

Technology, AI and the Future of Career Development

Finding: Learning management systems lead the way in development-friendly technologies

Organizations rely primarily on foundational technologies for career development. Learning Management Systems remain the most common tool, used by 55% of organizations. Performance management systems follow at 53%, and online content libraries, such as LinkedIn Learning, are used by 42%. Team communication software like video calls and shared chat rooms are used by 28%.

Specialized career tools remain less common, although AI-powered learning adoption is rising. The use of AI-powered learning tools doubled over the past year, rising to 25% adoption compared to 12% in 2025. Meanwhile, 15% of employers use no career development technology at all.

Finding: Career development leaders are nearly five times more likely than laggards to use AI learning tools

Organizations with more mature career development practices are more likely to invest in modern learning technologies.

More than half of career development leaders (55%) use AI-powered learning tools, compared to just 12% of laggards.

Leaders also hold huge advantages in specialized talent software. They are nearly four times more likely to use dedicated career management portals (42% vs. 12%), more than four times more likely to use digital mentoring/coaching platforms (32% vs. 7%), and twenty times more likely to use skill assessment tools (40% vs. 2%). Only 3% of leaders lack development software, compared to 25% of laggards who operate with no tools at all.

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to deploy specialized, modern developmental technologies.

Finding: Almost two-thirds of organizations rate their career development technologies as okay or poor

HR professionals give their career development tools lukewarm ratings. The plurality, 46%, rate their organization’s career technologies as merely okay. Only 28% rate them as good, and 8% rate them as excellent. Meanwhile, 18% describe their software as poor or very poor.

Although positive ratings rose from 26% in 2025 to 36% in 2026, 64% rate their technologies as only adequate or worse.

Finding: AI adoption surges for routine admin work, but strategic uses remain in early stages

AI adoption in career development increased substantially over the last year. In 2026, forty-four percent of HR professionals report that AI is used to automate administrative tasks in career development, more than double the 19% recorded in 2025. In addition, 39% say employees are expected to build AI skills (up from 25% in 2025), and 31% say their companies provide training on how to use AI (up from 17% in 2025).

Strategic applications of AI remain uncommon. Fewer than one in five organizations use AI to suggest personalized career steps (19%), evaluate employee skills and potential (18%), offer real-time feedback (14%), or close specific skills gaps (14%). Only 12% use AI to help spot future leaders, and just 10% use AI to match employees automatically to open jobs.

Differences based on organization size

Large employers lead in strategic AI use, with 27% assessing employee skills and potential and 25% providing personalized career guidance. Among midsized employers, 24% use AI for career guidance, while small employers primarily use it for administrative automation (48%).

Finding: Career development leader organizations are more likely to prepare employees for AI and use it to support career growth

More than six in ten career development leader organizations (61%) expect their workforce to develop AI skills, double that of laggards (29%). Almost half (47%) support this demand by providing structured AI training, compared to just 15% of laggards. Leaders also use AI to eliminate routine administrative work (64%) while offering employees data-backed career suggestions (39%).

Results of Chi-square test

A chi-square test of independence shows that career development leaders are significantly more likely than laggards to deploy AI across both operational and strategic talent workflows.

Finding: Nearly half of poll respondents say their organizations encourage employees to use AI tools for self-directed learning and provide guidance

Separate from the main HR Research Institute survey, a poll of 335 webcast participants examined how organizations handle employees using AI tools such as ChatGPT to learn new skills independently. Nearly half (48%) say their organizations encourage this practice and provide guidance, while 21% allow this practice without formal guidance, 6% allow it with strict guidance and 2% restrict or discourage it. Another 17% do not know their organization’s approach, and 5% are unsure whether employees use AI for this purpose.

AI-supported self-directed learning is more commonly encouraged than restricted. However, the 21% of organizations that allow it without guidance could benefit from clearer expectations for accuracy, privacy, responsible use, and practical application.

Finding: Over the next two years, AI is expected to transform skill-gap identification and career planning

Over the next two years, HR leaders expect AI to reshape career development. Half of respondents expect AI to improve how companies spot skills gaps, while 46% anticipate automated development planning, 43% expect personalized career paths, and 42% expect better matching for open jobs. Crucially, 40% expect that AI will require managers to learn new skills.

Finding: More than half of respondents expect data-driven workforce planning to become a more important career development skill over the next two years

HR professionals expect data-driven workforce planning (52%), manager-led career discussions (49%), coaching and mentoring (48%), and internal talent identification (48%) to become increasingly important over the next two years.

To prepare for these changes, planned investments over the next 24 months focus on manager coaching and training (42%), AI-powered learning tools (35%), learning management system upgrades (34%), succession planning tools (32%), and specialized career pathing software (25%).

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, we have the following suggestions:

  • Prioritize technology investments that address specific career development gaps.
    Organizations can assess where existing tools fall short in skills assessment, career planning, mentoring, and internal mobility before investing in new platforms. Pilot solutions against defined needs to assess their practical value.
  • Equip managers to use data in coaching conversations.
    HR teams can train managers to interpret skills profiles, learning progress, and career recommendations, then apply these insights in structured development conversations. Practical coaching guides and follow-up discussions can help translate these insights into relevant development actions.
  • Provide hands-on training for safe AI adoption.
    Organizations can offer short, role-specific training sessions demonstrating how to use approved AI tools for everyday tasks and career development activities. Guidance on accuracy, privacy, bias, and human oversight can help employees build confidence while using these tools responsibly.
  • Pilot internal project-matching tools before wider adoption.
    Organizations can test AI applications for identifying skills gaps, recommending learning activities, or matching employees with projects and internal roles. Pilot evaluations should examine the relevance of recommendations, employee participation, and practical development outcomes. Human oversight and appropriate safeguards for accuracy, privacy, and bias should be built into implementation.
  • Review technology effectiveness and redirect investment.
    HR teams can periodically assess platform usage, employee feedback, and contribution to development outcomes. These reviews can guide decisions about improving existing systems, discontinuing underused tools, or redirecting investment toward effective technologies and human support, such as mentoring and coaching.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaway 1: Advance career development maturity to move past informal practices.

While 27% of organizations have reached the Strategic or Innovative stages, 49% remain at the beginning or advancing levels. Formalize career frameworks and integrate them with succession planning and long-term workforce needs.

Key Takeaway 2: Close the gap between employee demand and available career opportunities.

Only 29% of HR professionals say opportunities meet demand, while 42% disagree. Cross-functional projects and targeted learning can help employees build capabilities even when promotions are limited.

Key Takeaway 3: Build dual-track career paths for non-managers to unlock internal mobility.

A lack of career paths for workers who do not want to become managers is the top obstacle to internal movement, cited by 46% of organizations. Companies should establish technical and professional advancement ladders that provide competitive pay, recognition, and leadership influence, allowing top individual contributors to advance without taking on people management.

Key Takeaway 4: Strengthen manager accountability and coaching support for talent development.

Although 63% of organizations encourage managers to support employee growth, only 23% hold them accountable and 12% reward them. Leadership should weave talent development into manager reviews, celebrate supervisors who help staff advance, and offer short coaching clinics to give managers the skills they need to guide careers effectively.

Key Takeaway 5: Formalize internal mobility processes.

Although 55% prioritize internal candidates, only 27% have formal mobility processes and 19% track mobility metrics. Establishing open transfer policies that let employees apply without prior supervisor permission, along with reasonable transition timelines, helps remove friction and keeps talent flowing across departments.

Key Takeaway 6: Extend development opportunities across the workforce.

Career development is not available to all employees in 35% of organizations. Expand access for frontline, operational, and entry-level employees through flexible learning, microlearning tracks and mentoring to build bench strength from the ground up.

Key Takeaway 7: Improve skill gap identification.

Only 21% of organizations identify and address skill gaps effectively to a high or very high degree. Regularly assess employee capabilities against current and anticipated workforce needs. Conduct skill reviews twice a year where managers and employees assess core competencies together, ensuring limited training dollars are directed toward genuine business shortages.

Key Takeaway 8: Protect time for development.

While 55% of organizations kept development time unchanged, 36% cite insufficient time as a mobility barrier. Build learning into work schedules and provide project-based development opportunities.

Key Takeaway 9: Move AI beyond administrative automation.

Although 44% use AI for administrative career tasks, only 19% use it for personalized guidance and 10% for job matching. Pilot AI-supported skills assessment and internal matching with appropriate safeguards.

Key Takeaway 10: Integrate talent technology and strengthen data-driven coaching.

Only 36% rate career development technologies as good or excellent. Connect learning and performance systems and equip managers to use workforce data in career conversations.
