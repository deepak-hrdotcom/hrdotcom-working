HR.com’s Future of Compensation and Total Rewards 2026-27

Executive Summary

Compensation is moving from the back office to the center of business strategy. Organizations are using total rewards to retain talent, stay competitive, and respond to changing employee expectations. Yet despite this shift, relatively few have reached a high level of maturity. This year’s HR.com’s Future of Compensation and Total Rewards 2026–27 report reveals that only 27% report having an advanced or innovative approach1 to compensation and total rewards (TR).

The findings suggest that maturity makes a measurable difference. TR leader2 organizations are more than ten times more likely than TR laggard organizations to report highly effective compensation programs, with 63% rating their strategy highly effective compared with just 6% of TR laggards.

The pressure to act is clear. Retention is the leading compensation objective for 57% of organizations, and 59% have updated their compensation practices within the past two years. Most employers focusing on attracting and/or retaining talent through compensation and TR provide the foundations employees expect, including healthcare, fair pay, and retirement support. But among organizations that identify enhancing the employee experience as a top compensation objective, only 35% regularly evaluate reward-program effectiveness and 27% offer flexible or personalized benefits.

The path forward is taking shape. Mature organizations pair strong compensation fundamentals with fair-pay reviews, broader measurement, and a more employee-centered rewards experience. As 52% of organizations focus on maintaining market competitiveness, Artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging as a practical tool for automating processes and improving market-pay analysis. The following findings show where organizations are making progress, where gaps persist, and which practices most clearly distinguish mature total rewards strategies.

About the Survey

“HR.com’s Future of Compensation and Total Rewards 2026” survey ran between May and July 2026. We gathered responses from 208 HR professionals in numerous industry verticals. Respondents are from all over the world, with the majority from North America, especially the United States.

The participants represent a broad cross-section of employers by number of employees, ranging from small businesses to enterprises with 20,000+ employees. Over three-fourths of respondents are from midsized and large organizations.

Major Research Findings

1. Compensation is becoming more strategic, but relatively few organizations have developed highly mature total rewards programs.

  • Only 27% report advanced or innovative compensation and total rewards maturity.
  • Nearly two-fifths (39%) describe their approach as intermediate.
  • One-third remain in the beginning or undeveloped stages.
  • The proportion reporting an advanced approach has nearly tripled from 7% in 2022 to 20% in 2026.

2. Retaining talent remains the primary driver of compensation strategy as organizations continue modernizing their rewards programs.

  • Almost three-fifths (57%) identify retaining key talent as their top compensation objective.
  • Three-fifths (59%) have updated compensation practices within the past two years.
  • Attracting talent (37%), motivating performance (36%), and ensuring fair and equitable pay (36%) remain major strategic priorities.

3. Organizations continue investing in foundational total rewards, but personalization and continuous evaluation remain limited.

  • Among respondents who identified attracting or retaining key talent as a top objective:
    • Eight in ten offer employee healthcare plans.
    • Seven in ten identify fair and equitable pay as a core element of their rewards offering.
    • Two-thirds provide retirement and financial well-being benefits.
  • Among respondents who identified enhancing the employee experience as a top compensation objective, 35% regularly evaluate reward program effectiveness, while just 27% offer flexible benefits or lifestyle accounts tailored to employee needs.

4. Organizations are preparing for a more data-driven and AI-enabled future while focusing on maintaining competitive compensation.

  • More than half (52%) identify remaining competitive as their top compensation priority over the next two years.
  • Almost two-fifths (38%) plan to strengthen pay-for-performance alignment.
  • More than half (55%) see AI's greatest value in automating compensation processes, while 51% see opportunities to improve market analysis and competitive pay decisions.

5. TR leaders consistently outperform TR laggard organizations by adopting data-driven and employee-centric compensation strategies.

Compared to TR laggards, TR leader organizations are:

  • more than 10X more likely to report highly effective compensation strategies
  • 6X more likely to regularly evaluate the effectiveness of their total rewards programs
  • more likely to conduct comprehensive pay fairness reviews and communicate pay transparency beyond legal requirements
  • more likely to measure a broader range of compensation outcomes including offer acceptance, employee retention, market competitiveness, and pay equity
  • delivering a broader employee value proposition, investing more heavily in career development, wellness, retirement benefits, flexible work, tuition assistance, and ongoing total rewards communication than their lower-performing counterparts

Compensation Strategy and Organizational Maturity

Organizations are increasingly recognizing compensation as a strategic tool for attracting, retaining, and motivating talent. However, the findings suggest that while many have established structured compensation programs, relatively few have developed highly mature strategies that consistently deliver strong business outcomes.

Defining compensation and total rewards for this survey:

  • Compensation is the combination of salaries, wages, cash, bonuses and monetary incentives that employees receive.
  • Total rewards is the combination of financial and non-financial rewards.

In this survey, we typically emphasize the tangible (e.g., pay and benefits) more than the intangible (e.g., recognition and flexibility) aspects of total rewards, though we do touch on the latter at times.

Finding: Only 27% of organizations report advanced or innovative compensation maturity

Organizations have made meaningful progress in developing structured compensation and total rewards programs, but most have not yet reached advanced levels of maturity. The largest proportion (39%) describe their compensation approach as intermediate, indicating they have established structured pay programs, equity considerations, and benefits. By comparison, only 27% report having advanced (20%) or innovative (7%) compensation programs, while 33% remain in the undeveloped (8%) or beginning (25%) stages.

Organizations with less mature compensation programs are more likely to struggle with maintaining market competitiveness, demonstrating pay equity, communicating the value of total rewards, and responding quickly to changing labor market conditions. As employee expectations continue to evolve and pay transparency requirements expand, organizations with fragmented or developing compensation practices may find it increasingly difficult to attract and retain critical talent.

Defining organizational size

For the purpose of this report, large organizations have 1,000 or more employees, midsized organizations have 100 to 999 employees, and small organizations have 99 or fewer employees.

Differences by organizational size

Compensation maturity generally increases with organizational size. More than one-third of large organizations report having advanced or innovative compensation and total rewards strategies (38%), compared with 26% of midsized organizations and 23% of small organizations.

Total rewards cohort classification

To garner a better understanding of what may be driving effective compensation and rewards practices today, we wanted to take a closer look at what separates organizations with mature compensation and rewards practices from those with less mature practices. To do this, we divided respondents into two cohorts:

  • TR leaders: These organizations represent respondents who answered “advanced” or “innovative” to the survey question, “How would you describe your organization's compensation and total rewards approach?”
  • TR laggards: These organizations represent respondents who answered “undeveloped” or “beginning” to that same question.

Correlation does not mean causation, of course. However, based on these analyses, organizations that want to improve their compensation strategies might benefit from understanding what some possible best practices look like.

Longitudinal analysis: Organizations continue progressing toward more mature compensation strategies

Across the comparable years, organizations show an overall movement toward more advanced compensation practices; however, the pattern is not consistent in every year. Organizations reporting an "Advanced" approach rose from 7% (2022) to 20% (2026). Concurrently, "Undeveloped" reactive approaches decreased from 12% (2022) down to 8% (2026). While "Intermediate" performance- and equity-focused models initially accounted for 52% (2022) of organizations, they stabilized at 39% (2026) as more employers evolved into advanced or innovative structures.

This transformation may reflect heightened executive awareness of compensation and total rewards as a critical driver of organizational change, accelerated by economic pressures and shifting labor dynamics.

Finding: Talent retention remains the primary objective of compensation strategies

Compensation strategies are evolving as organizations respond to changing workforce and business needs. Over two-fifths (43%) report making significant changes to their overall compensation strategy within the past two years, including 18% within the past year and 25% within the previous one to two years. Changes to specific compensation practices have occurred even more frequently, with 59% updating these programs during the same period.

Talent remains the primary driver of compensation strategy. More than half (57%) identify retaining key talent as a top objective, followed by attracting key talent (37%), motivating employee performance (36%), and providing fair and equitable pay (36%). These priorities reflect the challenge of balancing workforce expectations with financial sustainability.

Finding: TR leaders are more than ten times more likely than TR laggards to report highly effective compensation strategies

Nearly two-thirds (63%) of TR leaders rate their compensation strategy as highly or very highly effective, compared with just 6% of TR laggards. Conversely, 44% of TR laggards report low or very low effectiveness, while none of the TR leaders fall into these categories.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that TR leaders are much more likely than TR laggards to have an effective compensation and total rewards strategy.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, consider the following suggestions:

  • Continue building a more strategic compensation program. Only a small percentage of organizations have advanced or innovative compensation strategies, yet these organizations are far more likely to report highly effective compensation programs. Assess your current approach, identify the biggest gaps, and develop a plan to strengthen your compensation program over time. Focus first on the areas that will have the greatest impact, such as aligning compensation with business priorities, improving market competitiveness, or strengthening decision-making.
  • Make retention and competitiveness central to your compensation strategy. The findings show that retaining employees and maintaining competitive pay are the top drivers of compensation decisions. Review your compensation strategy regularly to ensure it reflects current labor market conditions and supports your organization's ability to attract and retain talent. Use market data, turnover trends, and employee feedback to guide compensation decisions instead of reacting only when hiring or retention becomes a problem.
  • Review and update your compensation strategy regularly. Nearly six in ten organizations have updated their compensation strategy within the past two years, reflecting the need to respond to changing business conditions and employee expectations. Establish a regular review process, such as an annual or semiannual review, to evaluate whether your compensation strategy still supports organizational goals, reflects market conditions, and meets workforce needs.
  • Make compensation decisions using reliable data. Organizations with more mature compensation strategies are much more likely to report successful outcomes. Support compensation decisions with reliable information such as salary surveys, labor market trends, internal pay data, and workforce metrics. Using data consistently can help improve fairness, competitiveness, and confidence in compensation decisions.
  • Measure whether your compensation strategy is delivering results. A compensation strategy should be evaluated by the outcomes it achieves, not simply by whether salary budgets are met. Track measures such as employee retention, offer acceptance rates, turnover in key roles, pay competitiveness, and employee perceptions of fairness. Review these measures regularly and use the results to adjust your compensation strategy as business and workforce needs evolve.

Delivering a Competitive Compensation and Total Rewards Experience

Finding: Roughly seven in ten organizations ensure employees have easy access to their pay information

The employee experience extends well beyond compensation alone, and organizations are increasingly investing in initiatives that strengthen communication, well-being, and development. The most common initiative is ensuring employees have easy access to pay and benefits information (68%) followed by educating employees about their benefits and total rewards package (65%), offering wellness programs (65%) and promoting work-life balance through flexible work arrangements (62%).

At the same time, several important elements of a modern total rewards strategy remain less common. Fewer than half of organizations offer competitive compensation plans (46%), maintain transparency in wage and benefits calculations (35%), or regularly evaluate the effectiveness of their reward programs (35%).

Finding: TR leaders provide a broader and more employee-centered total rewards experience

TR leaders consistently offer a wider range of initiatives that enhance the employee experience. The largest gap is in regularly evaluating the effectiveness of reward programs, with more than half of TR leaders doing so (55%) compared with just 9% of TR laggards.

TR leaders are also substantially more likely to promote work-life balance through flexible work arrangements (91% vs. 55%), offer wellness programs (82% vs. 64%) and provide opportunities for professional development and career advancement (73% vs. 46%).

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that TR leaders are much more likely than TR laggards to offer the listed initiatives to create positive employee experience.

Finding: Just one in five organizations offer compensation above market rate to attract and/or retain talent

Organizations continue to rely on a core set of established rewards to attract and retain talent, with employee healthcare plans remaining the most widely offered component (80%). Fair and equitable pay ranks second (70%), highlighting the growing importance of pay fairness as a core element of the employee value proposition. Retirement and financial well-being benefits (66%) and learning and development opportunities (61%) are also widely offered, while more than half of organizations provide remote work or flexible scheduling (54%) and tuition support or reimbursement (53%).

Finding: TR leaders provide a broader and more competitive total rewards package

TR leaders are considerably more likely to offer a wide range of total rewards that support employee attraction and retention. Large differences are in learning and development opportunities (79% vs. 31%), tuition reimbursement (74% vs. 28%), retirement and financial well-being benefits (82% vs. 44%), wellness programs (76% vs. 25%), and flexible work arrangements (71% vs. 36%).

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that TR leaders are much more likely than TR laggards to offer the listed initiatives to attract and/or retain key talent.

Finding: Nearly nine in ten organizations provide medical insurance, but few offer specialized benefits such as crisis intervention

Organizations continue to invest heavily in employee health and wellness benefits but prioritize foundational health benefits, while more specialized wellness offerings remain less widespread. Medical insurance (88%) and Employee Assistance Programs (81%) are common, while many also provide medical leave policies (68%), health savings accounts (65%), and mental health support (63%).

Finding: Work-life benefits emphasize flexibility but provide limited support for caregiving responsibilities

Work-life benefits are similarly centered on flexibility and employee support. Paid time off (88%) is the most common offering, followed by flexible work arrangements (65%), assistance with insurance and benefits providers (54%), parental and caregiver leave (49%), and family or personal counseling services (43%). In contrast, benefits that provide more direct support for caregiving and remote work, such as caregiver referral services (17%), home office allowances (15%), and on-site childcare (9%), are uncommon, indicating opportunities to better support employees with diverse personal and family responsibilities.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following suggestions:

  • Build on your foundation by offering rewards that meet changing employee needs. Most organizations already provide the core rewards employees expect, such as healthcare, retirement plans, and paid time off. The next opportunity is to make rewards more meaningful by identifying where employees' needs are changing. Use employee surveys, focus groups, and workforce data to understand what employees value most, then adjust your rewards package where it will have the greatest impact.
  • Give employees more choice in how they are rewarded. Relatively few organizations offer flexible or personalized rewards, even though employee needs vary across different life stages and career paths. Where possible, introduce options such as flexible benefits, lifestyle spending accounts, voluntary benefits, or additional leave choices so employees can select rewards that best fit their individual needs.
  • Regularly evaluate whether your rewards programs are working. Although many organizations invest in total rewards, relatively few regularly evaluate their effectiveness. Establish an annual review process that looks at employee participation, satisfaction, retention, recruitment outcomes, and business results. Use employee feedback together with program data to identify which rewards provide the greatest value and where improvements are needed.
  • Help employees understand the value of their total rewards. Employees cannot fully appreciate benefits they don't understand. Communicate your total rewards package throughout the year, not just during enrollment periods. Consider providing total rewards statements, manager toolkits, employee information sessions, and simple explanations of available programs so employees recognize the full value of what the organization provides.
  • Make fairness and employee experience part of your rewards strategy. Organizations with more mature total rewards strategies are much more likely to support employee well-being, regularly evaluate their programs, and provide a better employee experience. Review your rewards programs regularly to ensure they are fair, accessible, and aligned with employee expectations. Pay equity reviews, transparent communication, and ongoing employee feedback can help strengthen trust and improve the overall employee experience.

Addressing Compensation Challenges Through Fair and Data-Driven Practices

Organizations face growing pressure to maintain competitive compensation programs while managing financial constraints and responding to increasing employee expectations. As compensation decisions become more complex, organizations are placing greater emphasis on fair pay practices, market data, and performance measurement to guide their strategies.

Finding: Budget constraints and rising employee expectations continue to challenge compensation decisions

Organizations face competing pressures when designing compensation programs. Budget constraints remain the most common challenge (44%), followed by weak links between pay and performance (30%), wage compression (28%), and difficulty personalizing rewards to employee needs (27%).

From the employee perspective, the greatest concern is the rising cost of living (82%), followed by insufficient wage growth (52%) and the affordability of healthcare (47%). Together, these findings highlight the growing challenge of balancing organizational constraints with employees' expectations for competitive, meaningful, and sustainable rewards.

Longitudinal analysis: Communication challenges have declined, while pay-for-performance remains a persistent concern

Organizations appear to be making steady progress in communicating compensation and total rewards programs. The proportion reporting that compensation is not understood or communicated well declined from 49% in 2023 to 25% in 2026. However, concerns about pay-for-performance alignment (30%) and transparency (17%) persist, suggesting that organizations should now focus on strengthening the fairness and effectiveness of compensation decisions, not just improving communication.

Finding: Nearly three-fifths of organizations track pay ranges

Many organizations have implemented practices to improve compensation fairness and consistency. Among respondents who identified providing fair and equitable pay as one of their organization's top three compensation and total rewards objectives, the most common initiative is complying with pay transparency laws (77%), followed by conducting periodic reviews to evaluate pay fairness (62%) and using real-time market data to benchmark and adjust pay (54%). Nearly half of organizations incorporate fair pay into their compensation philosophy (49%) and actively manage employee perceptions of pay equity (43%).

Organizations primarily measure metrics that support compensation planning and market positioning. The most commonly tracked metric is pay range (61%), followed by salary competitiveness (51%) and total cost of the workforce (48%). Many organizations also monitor compa-ratio (44%), range width (37%), and internal equity metrics (36%), indicating a focus on maintaining consistent pay structures and competitive compensation practices.

Differences by organizational size

Small organizations are more likely to track the total cost of the workforce (59%) than salary competitiveness (46%) or pay ranges (49%), reflecting a stronger focus on managing compensation costs. Larger organizations, however, are more likely to measure salary competitiveness, internal equity, market ratios, geographic differentials, and offer acceptance rates.

Longitudinal analysis: Organizations are broadening their analytics tracking, moving beyond foundational pay ranges to incorporate market ratios, internal equity metrics, and geographic differentials

Pay ranges have remained the most widely tracked compensation metric, measured by 69% of organizations in 2022, peaking at 75% in 2023, and settling at 61% in 2026, providing a strong foundation for compensation management. At the same time, organizations are gradually expanding the use of metrics such as internal equity (32% to 36%), market ratio (27% to 33%), and range width (23% to 37%), reflecting a growing emphasis on fair and competitive compensation.

Finding: TR leaders demonstrate a stronger commitment to fair pay practices

TR leaders are substantially more proactive in implementing initiatives that promote fair and equitable pay. The largest difference is in conducting periodic reviews to evaluate pay fairness, with all responding TR leaders reporting this practice (100%) compared with just 38% of TR laggards.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that TR leaders are much more likely than TR laggards to offer the listed initiatives to provide fair/equitable pay.

Finding: TR leaders rely on a broader range of compensation metrics to inform decisions

TR leaders are considerably more likely to measure metrics that evaluate both compensation effectiveness and workforce outcomes. The largest differences are in tracking offer acceptance rates (52% vs. 9%), market ratio (56% vs. 20%), internal equity metrics (54% vs. 16%), retention related to pay and benefits (48% vs. 14%), and geographic pay differentials (44% vs. 13%).

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that TR leaders are much more likely than TR laggards to measure and track the listed metrics.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following suggestions:

  • Find ways to balance financial realities with employee expectations. Budget constraints remain the biggest challenge, while employees continue to feel the impact of rising living costs and insufficient wage growth. Review your compensation strategy to identify where limited resources can have the greatest impact. When significant pay increases aren't possible, consider strengthening other parts of your total rewards package, such as flexible work arrangements, recognition programs, career development, or well-being initiatives to better support employees.
  • Make fair pay an ongoing practice, not a one-time review. Many organizations are taking steps to improve pay fairness, but more mature organizations go further by regularly reviewing pay equity, using current market data, and communicating their approach to employees. Schedule regular pay equity reviews, update market benchmarks annually, and clearly explain how pay decisions are made to help build employee trust and confidence.
  • Measure the impact of your compensation strategy, not just compensation costs. Many organizations track salary ranges and workforce costs, but fewer measure whether compensation is helping attract and retain talent. Expand your reporting to include measures such as retention related to pay, offer acceptance rates, internal equity, and employee perceptions of fairness. Reviewing these measures regularly can help identify where compensation programs are delivering value and where improvements are needed.
  • Use compensation data to make better decisions. Organizations with more mature compensation strategies rely on a broader range of compensation metrics to guide their decisions. Bring together salary survey data, internal pay information, workforce trends, and compensation metrics into simple dashboards that managers and leaders can use when making compensation decisions. Using reliable data can improve consistency, support market competitiveness, and reduce bias.

Preparing for the Future of Compensation and Total Rewards

The role of compensation is evolving beyond pay administration to become a strategic driver of workforce attraction, retention, and organizational performance. At the same time, advances in AI are creating new opportunities to improve compensation decisions. However, realizing these benefits will require organizations to balance innovation with responsible governance and human oversight.

Finding: Remaining competitive within the industry is the top compensation priority over the next two years

Organizations expect compensation and total rewards to become increasingly strategic over the next two years. Their top priorities are remaining competitive within the industry (52%), aligning pay to performance (38%), communicating the value of total rewards (33%), and supporting career development (29%).

Longitudinal analysis: Aligning pay to performance remains a leading future priority

Aligning pay to performance remains one of the highest future priorities, increasing from 42% in 2024 to 49% in 2025 before moderating to 38% in 2026. In contrast, paying transparently and equitably declined sharply from 42% to 23%, suggesting that organizations increasingly view pay transparency as an established business practice rather than a future priority. Meanwhile, paying according to skill levels remained relatively stable (29% in 2024 to 23% in 2026), indicating continued interest but less urgency than performance alignment.

Finding: Organizations see the greatest opportunity for AI in automating compensation processes

Organizations recognize AI's potential to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of compensation management. The greatest perceived opportunity is automating manual processes (55%), followed by analyzing industry and market trends to support competitive pay (51%), identifying compensation disparities (43%), and providing managers with better decision-making insights (42%). However, 14% of respondents are unsure where AI would provide the greatest benefit, suggesting that while interest in AI is growing, many organizations are still developing a clear vision for how it can create value within compensation and total rewards.

Differences by organizational size

Organizations of all sizes recognize opportunities for AI, but larger organizations place greater emphasis on more advanced applications. Compared with small organizations, they are more likely to see value in identifying compensation disparities, providing managers with better decision-making insights, and using predictive analytics to optimize rewards. Small organizations are comparatively more likely to be uncertain about where AI can create value, suggesting they are earlier in their AI adoption journey.

Longitudinal analysis: Organizations prioritize AI for process automation and market trend analysis over direct employee-facing personalization or predictive modeling

The perceived utility of AI in total rewards centers predominantly on operational efficiency and external market analysis. Automating manual processes to improve efficiency experienced a rebound to 55% (2026) after declining from 59% (2023) to 47% (2024) and 42% (2025). Analyzing industry and market trends for competitive pay leads AI applications, standing at 60% and settling at 51% in 2026.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research consider the following suggestions:

  • Develop a forward-looking compensation strategy. Organizations expect compensation to play an increasingly strategic role in attracting, retaining, and motivating talent. Review your compensation strategy regularly to ensure it supports changing business priorities, workforce expectations, and labor market conditions. Consider how compensation, career development, and total rewards work together to create a compelling employee value proposition that remains competitive over time.
  • Strengthen the connection between pay and performance. Aligning pay to performance continues to be one of organizations' highest future priorities. Review how performance outcomes influence compensation decisions and ensure managers have clear, consistent guidelines for recognizing individual and team contributions. Regularly evaluate whether performance-based rewards reinforce the behaviors and results that support organizational success.
  • Start with practical AI applications that improve efficiency. Organizations see the greatest opportunity for AI in automating manual processes, analyzing market trends, and supporting compensation decisions. Focus initial AI investments on routine, high-value activities such as market pricing, salary benchmarking, reporting, and compensation analysis before expanding into more advanced applications. Starting with practical use cases can help build confidence while delivering measurable efficiency gains.
  • Maintain human oversight when using AI. While AI can improve the speed and quality of compensation analysis, compensation decisions should continue to involve human judgment. Establish clear governance for AI use by defining where AI supports decision-making, validating outputs with reliable data, and ensuring managers retain responsibility for final compensation decisions. This approach can help improve consistency while reducing the risk of bias or unintended outcomes.
  • Build organizational readiness for AI in compensation. Many organizations are still determining where AI can create the greatest value, particularly smaller organizations. Before implementing new AI tools, assess your organization's readiness by identifying priority use cases, evaluating data quality, developing internal capabilities, and providing training for HR and compensation professionals. A phased approach will help organizations adopt AI more effectively while supporting long-term success.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaway 1: Treat compensation as a strategic business capability.

Only about one-quarter of organizations report having an advanced or innovative compensation strategy, yet these organizations are far more likely to achieve effective compensation outcomes. Position compensation as a strategic function that supports business objectives, workforce planning, talent attraction, and employee retention rather than viewing it solely as an administrative activity.

Key Takeaway 2: Regularly review and adapt your compensation strategy.

Business conditions, labor markets, and employee expectations continue to evolve. Establish a regular review process to evaluate whether your compensation strategy remains competitive, aligned with organizational priorities, and responsive to changing workforce needs. Continuous improvement is more effective than infrequent large-scale redesigns.

Key Takeaway 3: Deliver a total rewards experience that reflects employee needs.

Competitive salaries alone are no longer sufficient to attract and retain talent. Regularly assess employee preferences and strengthen the overall rewards experience through benefits, flexibility, career development, recognition, and well-being programs that address the diverse needs of today's workforce.

Key Takeaway 4: Strengthen the link between pay and performance.

Weak alignment between pay and performance continues to be a significant challenge. Review performance management and compensation processes together to ensure rewards recognize meaningful contributions, reinforce organizational priorities, and are applied consistently across the organization.

Key Takeaway 5: Make fair and transparent pay practices part of everyday decision-making.

More mature organizations are substantially more likely to conduct pay equity reviews, communicate their compensation philosophy, and actively manage employee perceptions of fairness. Regularly evaluate pay practices, communicate how compensation decisions are made, and use market data to support equitable and consistent outcomes.

Key Takeaway 6: Measure outcomes, not just compensation costs.

Many organizations monitor salary structures and workforce costs, but fewer evaluate whether compensation programs improve attraction, retention, or employee experience. Expand compensation dashboards to include workforce outcome measures such as offer acceptance rates, retention related to pay, internal equity, employee perceptions of fairness, and market competitiveness.

Key Takeaway 7: Use data to improve compensation decisions.

Organizations with more mature compensation strategies rely on a broader range of market, workforce, and compensation analytics. Integrate internal workforce data with external market information to support more informed, consistent, and evidence-based compensation decisions.

Key Takeaway 8: Adopt AI gradually and focus on practical business value.

Organizations see the greatest opportunity for AI in automating manual processes, analyzing market trends, and improving compensation analysis. Begin with practical applications that improve efficiency and decision support before expanding into more advanced uses.

Key Takeaway 9: Establish strong governance for AI in compensation.

As AI becomes more integrated into compensation processes, ensure that human judgment remains central to compensation decisions. Develop clear governance for AI use, monitor data quality, validate AI-generated insights, and regularly assess fairness, transparency, and compliance.

Key Takeaway 10: Build compensation capabilities for the future.

The organizations that achieve the strongest compensation outcomes combine strategic planning, fair pay practices, effective communication, analytics, and responsible technology adoption. Invest in developing these capabilities over time through ongoing learning, process improvement, and technology investments that support long-term organizational success.
