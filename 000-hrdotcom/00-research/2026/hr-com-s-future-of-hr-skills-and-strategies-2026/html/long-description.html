HR.com's Future of HR Skills and Strategies 2026

Executive Summary

HR teams lack needed AI skills

In 2026, the main question for HR is no longer whether to use AI but, rather, whether most HR professionals have the skills to use it well. And the answer, for now, is “no.”

That is, when we asked a group of HR experts about the most important skills gap that HR teams need to close in 2026, the top answer was “AI and automation proficiency,” cited by 83%.

AI represents both an opportunity and a threat

This gap is critical because automation is becoming a core part of how HR work gets done. Experts see a clear opportunity in using AI to handle administrative tasks.

In fact, 42% identified administrative automation as the top AI opportunity because it can reduce workload and free up time for more strategic work.

At the same time, however, 42% also identified loss of human connection as the biggest risk of AI adoption.

HR must improve efficiency without weakening trust or relationships

HR leaders need strategic business acumen. Not everything is about AI, however. When asked about what makes an effective HR leader, 67% cited strategic business acumen. This signals that HR’s value increasingly comes from linking people decisions to business results, not just running programs.

The Yin and Yang of HR in 2026

Overall, the findings suggest that HR’s future success in 2026 will depend on doing two things at once: leveraging AI and other technologies to automate routine work while simultaneously building stronger consulting, analytical, and leadership skills. If organizations invest only in technology and not in capability, HR will remain stuck in transactional work instead of becoming a true strategic partner.

This study is designed to help HR leaders close this gap between rising expectations and current readiness. The insights that follow can help organizations prioritize capability building, adopt automation more responsibly, and redesign HR work in ways that support both efficiency and human connection.

Research methodology and panel profile

This research used the Delphi Method, a structured approach designed to build consensus among experts over multiple rounds. The study took place between November 2025 and January 2026. The research process included three stages:

  • Round 1 (exploration): Experts answered open-ended questions to identify emerging trends.
  • Round 2 (prioritization): Responses were synthesized into themes, which experts evaluated and ranked.
  • Round 3 (resolution): Experts weighed trade-offs and selected priorities for action.

Advisory board discussions: Two meetings with industry thought leaders helped interpret findings and provide additional context.

The expert panel

To ensure a broad perspective, the study engaged 16 senior HR leaders and subject-matter experts representing multiple industries and areas of specialization, including HR transformation, healthcare HR, public-sector HR strategy, HR technology, consulting, manufacturing, life sciences, and professional services. Nearly all panelists have more than two decades of HR experience, providing a strong foundation for forecasting future HR capability and strategy.

About Quote Attribution

Throughout this report, you will notice two different styles of quoting our experts. To maintain the integrity of the Delphi method, which relies on unbiased consensus-building, all quotes drawn directly from the written Delphi survey rounds are kept completely anonymous. Conversely, quotes and insights drawn from our live Advisory Board video discussions are attributed to the specific thought leaders who shared them.

Major Research Findings

1. Strategic business acumen has become HR’s most critical leadership capability.

  • 67% of experts identify strategic business acumen as the most important non-negotiable capability for HR leaders in 2026.
  • This ranks ahead of AI and digital fluency (58%), adaptive resilience (50%), and human-centric change leadership (50%).
  • 75% strongly agree that business literacy is a defining indicator of a strategic HR function.
  • 50% say that designing future-ready structures, skills, and ways of working creates the most HR value in 2026.
  • 40% emphasize building leaders’ ability to manage people and culture as HR’s most important value-creation opportunity.

2. AI and automation proficiency represents HR’s largest skills gap.

  • Upskilling HR capabilities is identified as the most important priority for enabling strategic HR operation.
  • For HR teams, the biggest skills gaps to close are:
    • AI and automation proficiency (83%)
    • consulting and coaching skills (58%)
    • data literacy (58%)
  • Only about one-third identify traditional functional HR expertise as needing improvement.

3. HR faces a high-tech, high-touch paradox in AI adoption.

  • 42% of experts identify administrative automation as the top AI opportunity.
  • 42% also identify erosion of human connection as the top AI-related risk.
  • Experts rank AI implementation priorities as:
    • administrative automation
    • personalized employee experiences
    • predictive workforce planning
  • Conditions to be set in place before scaling AI in HR are:
    • clear rules on where human judgment must be retained (60%)
    • governance and accountability for AI-supported decisions (50%)
    • pilot evidence demonstrating business value (40%)

4. Employee value propositions are shifting from standardized benefits to need-based fairness.

  • Competitive pay and benefits are increasingly viewed as baseline expectations rather than differentiators.
  • Important elements of employee value proposition (EVP) in 2026 are:
    • strategic alignment between employee work and organizational goals (67%)
    • flexible work design (50%)
    • need-based fairness (50%)
  • For need-based fairness, most experts support tailoring employee support even if experiences differ across individuals.

5. Operational overload remains the primary barrier to strategic HR.

  • Major barriers preventing HR from achieving strategic goals are:
    • operational overload (58%)
    • lack of AI and data capability (50%)
    • rigid legacy systems (50%)
  • 83% of experts support aggressively scaling administrative automation.
  • 58% support transitioning toward skills-based operating models.

Redefining HR’s Core Capability

Finding: Strategic business acumen emerges as the top non-negotiable capability

The HR Research Institute’s Delphi study (for more on how the Delphi Method works, see the callout in the Executive Summary) shows a clear shift in what defines effective HR leadership. Strategic business acumen has emerged as the most important capability for HR leaders in 2026, selected by 67% of experts, ahead of AI and digital fluency (58%), adaptive resilience (50%) and human-centric change leadership (50%). This suggests that HR leadership is becoming less about managing HR programs and more about helping the business succeed.

Tracing the consensus across Delphi rounds

Round 1: HR must understand how the business works

In the first round of the Delphi study, experts repeatedly emphasized that HR leaders must understand how the business actually works. Panelists described the need for HR leaders who understand financial realities, operational pressures, and organizational performance, not just HR policies and programs.

Several comments from our expert panel captured this shift clearly:

"HR teams become strategic partners when they create visible business value beyond process delivery... They bring insights, not just activity."

"HR teams that become true strategic partners are obsessed with the business, not just HR. They know the P&L, speak in outcomes, and design people solutions that solve real operational problems."

Panelists also pointed to leadership readiness as a growing challenge. Many leaders are promoted based on individual performance rather than leadership capability. As a result, they may struggle to manage uncertainty, workforce change, and technology adoption at the same time.

Dr. Ken Keis, President and CEO at Consulting Resources Group, stated, "Deficiencies become more apparent under pressure, and many individuals promoted into leadership roles have never had leadership training."

This highlights the growing importance of leadership development as part of HR’s strategic role.

Round 2: Business literacy separates strategic HR from administrative HR

Round 2 confirmed this direction. Strategic business acumen, defined as connecting talent strategies to business outcomes such as value drivers and operational realities, was selected by 67% of panelists, making it the most frequently identified non-negotiable capability. Panelists also showed strong agreement about what distinguishes strategic HR from administrative HR. Seventy-five percent strongly agreed that business literacy is a defining indicator of a strategic HR function. This was defined as understanding business value drivers and operational constraints.

Organizations are managing multiple pressures at once, including AI adoption, hybrid work, skills shortages, and economic uncertainty. HR leaders are expected to help organizations respond to these changes while maintaining trust and psychological safety. This requires HR to anticipate workforce risks, translate talent issues into business impact, and support leaders in making decisions about organizational capability.

Round 3: HR must help design how work gets done

The final round clarified how this capability creates value. When asked what single capability would create the most value for HR in 2026, panelists converged around designing future-ready structures, skills, and ways of working (50%).

This reflects a deeper change in HR’s role. Strategic business acumen is not only about advising leaders. It is about helping design how work gets done in the organization.

Several expert comments illustrate this shift. For example:

"The difference is not about doing more work... it's about doing the right work and approaching HR with the mindset of business leaders, not administrators."

"[The goal is] to deliver stakeholder value as an HR function... to offer integrated HR solutions around talent, leadership, organization (what I call human capability)."

By the final round, panelists also linked leadership capability directly to organizational value creation. When asked what HR could do exceptionally well to create the most value in 2026, many responses emphasized building leaders’ ability to manage people, culture, and organizational change (40%).

Leadership capability is no longer separate from HR strategy. It is a key driver of execution and organizational resilience.

Longitudinal trends

The Delphi findings build on patterns identified in the Future of HR Skills and Strategies 2025 research. In 2025, only 44% of HR professionals reported being equal partners in strategic planning, down from 58% in 2021.

At the same time, the 2025 study found that 79% of HR teams still spend more than half of their workweek on transactional activities, limiting their ability to contribute strategically. This gap between expectations and reality helps explain why the Delphi panel placed such strong emphasis on business acumen.

Implications

HR earns strategic influence by helping design how the business works, not just how HR operates. Technical HR expertise remains necessary, but it no longer differentiates high-impact HR leaders. As AI reduces administrative work and organizations redesign roles, HR leaders are expected to help build leadership capability and design workforce systems that support continuous change.

HRRI Strategic Takeaways

Based on our research, consider the following recommendations:

  • Connect HR metrics to business results
    HR reporting should clearly show business impact. Metrics such as time-to-hire or training completion rates are useful, but leaders need to understand what those numbers mean for productivity, revenue, risk, or customer experience.
  • Build business literacy across the HR team
    Strategic business acumen cannot live only at the CHRO level. The Delphi panel’s findings suggest that HR teams at all levels need to understand how the organization creates value and how talent decisions affect performance.
  • Spend more time inside the business
    HR credibility grows when HR leaders understand operational realities firsthand. Being close to the work helps HR anticipate workforce challenges instead of reacting to them.
  • Frame HR recommendations in business language
    Workforce challenges become strategic issues when they are explained in terms of risk, cost, capacity, or execution impact. Translating talent issues into business language increases HR’s influence in decision-making.
  • Measure HR success by outcomes, not activity
    Strategic HR functions focus on organizational impact rather than process volume. This means shifting from measuring how much HR work gets done to measuring how HR work improves performance and capability.
  • Strengthen leadership capability during disruption
    Human-centric change leadership emerges as a core capability because organizations are operating in continuous change. Leaders need support in managing uncertainty, communication, and employee trust. Invest in leadership development focused on managing change and leading people through uncertain times.

The AI Paradox

Finding: Administrative automation is the top opportunity, and erosion of human connection is the top risk

HR leaders see AI as both a solution and a threat. Two priorities stand out in the data:

  • Administrative automation (automating repetitive tasks to free HR capacity for strategic work) emerged as the leading AI opportunity, with 42% of experts identifying it as the top AI priority.
  • Erosion of human connection (over-automation weakening trust and psychological safety) emerged as the top AI-related risk, also identified by 42% of panelists, ahead of compliance risk and algorithmic bias.

HR must automate enough work to escape operational overload but not so much that it weakens employee trust.

Tracing the consensus across Delphi rounds

Round 1: Automation should remove friction, not replace judgment

In the first Delphi round, experts described AI mainly as a way to reduce administrative burden. Many pointed to daily frustration with repetitive HR tasks and saw automation as a way to shift time toward higher-value work.

One respondent highlighted the primary opportunity as "streamlining Administrative and Repetitive Work... freeing HR teams to focus on higher-value strategic work."

Another expert stated, “The biggest opportunity is to use AI to build a more human-centered organization."

At the same time, experts warned against replacing human judgment with automated decisions. Advisory board discussions raised particular concerns about using AI in sensitive areas such as employee relations, coaching, and performance management.

One expert, responding to the question “As AI becomes more integral to work, what are the most urgent opportunities and risks for HR?,” stated there were "opportunities to reduce the amount of time spent on mundane tasks” but also “risks of overly relying on AI to 'do the work for us’.'"

Dave Ulrich, a leading thinker and author in the HR field, captured this balance clearly:

“AI expectations are so high, with press advocating that ‘AI’ will solve these hassles. Yet most use GenAI to source information more than agentic AI, which automates HR processes that overcome administrative hassles. And some of the work hassles require personal attention to exercise judgment not found in generic algorithms.” — Dave Ulrich, Partner The RBL Group; Rensis Likert Professor Emeritus, University of Michigan.

Christopher Lind shared a similar observation from practice: "[AI] is doing a good job of when narrowly focused on tasks... but if it's not repeatable it doesn't go well. In full blown replacement... I haven't had a single situation where it's stuck." — Christopher Lind, Executive Advisor, Business Transformation, Christopher Lind Co.

Together, these insights established an early theme that automation should remove administrative friction, not replace human relationships.

Round 2: Poor implementation of AI can damage trust

Round 2 confirmed administrative automation as the most immediate AI opportunity for HR. Experts consistently prioritized automating repetitive work so HR teams could focus on higher-value activities. At the same time, the panel identified several AI-related risks, with one writing, “If AI models are not monitored, they can unintentionally amplify bias... Poor implementation—such as using AI for surveillance or opaque decision-making—can damage culture.”

These comments illustrate a growing concern that AI can accelerate poor decisions if organizations lack governance and capability.

Round 3: Start with automation before moving to advanced AI use cases

The final round clarified how experts believe organizations should implement AI responsibly.

Panelists ranked the AI implementation roadmap as:

  • 1. Administrative automation
  • 2. Personalized employee experiences
  • 3. Predictive workforce planning

This sequencing reflects a practical insight: organizations must first reduce administrative workload before moving to more advanced AI applications.

The panel also identified conditions that must be in place before scaling AI across HR. The most important requirement was “clear rules on where human judgment must be used” selected by 60% of experts.

Other important conditions included:

  • clear governance and accountability for AI decisions (50%)
  • proof from pilots that AI delivers real business value (40%)
  • clean and integrated HR data (30%)

The relatively lower emphasis on data readiness suggests that experts tend to be more concerned with human judgment and governance rather than data-focused technology issues. One expert warned, "The biggest risk is to treat AI the same way organizations treated 'digital transformation.' When AI is framed purely as an IT initiative, HR loses ownership of the people impact."

Another states that HR professionals should “recognize that information from AI without human ingenuity (judgment, decisions, future focus, creativity, emotion) is critical."

Definitions (for reference):

  • Predictive workforce planning – Using AI to forecast turnover, mobility, and skill gaps.
  • Hyper-personalized HR services – Tailoring learning, support, and career guidance to individuals.
  • Administrative automation – Automating repetitive tasks to free HR capacity for strategic work.
  • The “IT initiative” trap – Treating AI solely as a technology project rather than one that transforms the ways people work.
  • Erosion of human connection – Over-automation weakening trust and psychological safety.
  • Algorithmic bias – Unmonitored systems creating inequity and legal risk.

Longitudinal trends

In 2025, 57% of respondents expected AI to increase automation of HR tasks, showing that organizations primarily viewed AI as a productivity tool. Fewer organizations (37%) reported improvements in personalization or employee experience through technology.

The 2026 Delphi panel extends this insight by highlighting a key risk: automation alone does not improve employee experience. Without intentional reinvestment in human connection, automation may simply shift risk rather than reduce it.

Implications

AI adoption in HR is a judgment, governance and sequencing challenge, not just a technology challenge. Automation can reduce administrative workload and create capacity for strategic work. However, HR must retain ownership of decisions that involve judgment, relationships, and employee trust. The goal is not to replace HR work with AI but to redesign HR work so that technology and human capability strengthen each other.

HRRI Strategic Takeaways

Based on our research, consider the following takeaways:

  • Automate administrative work first
    The Delphi panel made it clear that administrative automation should be the starting point for AI in HR. Reducing repetitive work creates the capacity HR needs to focus on strategy, workforce planning, and leadership support.
  • Define where human judgment must remain
    Experts consistently emphasized that AI should support human decisions, not replace the people making them. Organizations need clear rules about where human oversight is required, especially in sensitive areas like hiring, performance management, and employee relations.
  • Pair automation with reinvestment in people work
    Automation only creates value if the time saved is intentionally redirected toward strategic and relational work. Without this reinvestment, automation improves efficiency but does not strengthen HR’s strategic contribution.
  • Establish clear governance for AI in HR
    The Delphi panel highlighted governance as a greater concern than technical readiness. HR should play a leadership role in setting ethical boundaries, accountability structures, and decision guidelines for AI use.
  • Build decision literacy, not just AI literacy
    AI fluency is not only about using tools. It is about understanding what AI can and cannot see, how models are trained, and when human judgment must override automated outputs.
  • Pilot AI in safe environments
    Experts emphasized the importance of experimentation without organizational risk. Pilot programs allow teams to test AI tools, build confidence, and learn what works before scaling. But organizations need to beware being caught in “pilot mode” indefinitely.
  • Protect trust as automation expands
    The Delphi panel consistently identified erosion of human connection as the biggest risk of AI in HR. As automation increases, HR must intentionally maintain visibility, empathy, and relationship-based work.

The Skills Crisis

The previous sections showed that HR’s future influence depends on stronger business understanding and responsible use of AI.

However, both of these depend on one underlying factor. HR teams must have the skills to use AI effectively, interpret workforce data, and help leaders manage constant change.

Finding: AI and automation proficiency is the largest skills gap

The Delphi panel identified a major capability gap between what HR teams need and what they can currently do. Most panelists identified AI and automation proficiency as the biggest skills gap to close for HR teams, selected by 83%.

This gap was far larger than the gaps related to more traditional areas such as functional HR expertise, which was identified by only a third of experts. The message from the panel is clear: HR leaders understand the importance of AI, but most HR teams are not yet ready to use it effectively.

Tracing the consensus across Delphi rounds

Round 1: Many HR teams are using AI without the skills to evaluate it

In the first Delphi round, experts frequently described organizations experimenting with AI tools without the internal skills needed to evaluate outputs, manage risk, or integrate AI into daily workflows.

This concern connects directly to broader warnings about AI adoption:

"AI is not a magic pill; it still needs to be tracked and managed. The other risk is simply ignoring AI," stated one panelist.

Advisory board discussions raised an important concern. AI-generated outputs often appear credible. Without the skills to question results or understand data limitations, HR teams may accept flawed insights or reach incorrect conclusions.

"It's real easy to take this data and then throw it into things and make it look good... and not everybody's prepared to even ask the follow up questions of like what does that mean... is that the right conclusion," stated Christopher Lind, Executive Advisor, Business Transformation, Christopher Lind Co.

Panelists also noted that many HR teams struggle to move beyond reporting workforce metrics to using workforce data to influence business decisions.

One stated, "The biggest opportunity is to use AI to build a more human-centered organization... AI can finally help HR close the persistent Workforce Readiness Gap."

Round 2: HR must operate as advisors, not administrators

By the second round, the panel clearly identified capability development priorities across HR teams. In addition to AI and automation proficiency (83%), both consulting and coaching skills and data literacy were identified as major capability gaps by 58% of experts. Additionally, AI and digital fluency was chosen by almost three-fifths of panelists as a non-negotiable for HR leaders.

These findings reinforce the shift described earlier in the report. HR is expected to operate less as a process administrator and more as a business advisor and interpreter of workforce data.

At the same time, relatively few experts identified traditional HR expertise as a gap. Only about one-third of panelists selected functional HR expertise as an area needing improvement.

This suggests that the profession does not lack HR knowledge. Instead, it lacks future-ready capabilities such as data interpretation, consulting, and AI-enabled decision-making.

Round 3: Upskilling must come before strategic transformation

The final round clarified how panelists believe organizations should respond to these capability gaps. When asked what must change first for HR to operate more strategically, panelists identified upskilling HR capabilities as the most pressing priority.

However, developing new skills requires time and capacity. HR teams may struggle to build new capabilities if operational workloads remain high.

Dr. Ken Keis, President and CEO at Consulting Resources Group, summarized this concern: “Over 70% of AI initiatives fail within 18 months because they do not consider the human element.”

Longitudinal trends

In the 2025 research, relatively few respondents identified AI skills as a major capability need. By contrast, in the 2026 Delphi panel, AI and automation proficiency emerged as the largest capability gap. This suggests that AI adoption is moving faster than HR’s ability to build the skills needed to use it effectively.

Implications

HR teams are being asked to adopt AI, operate more strategically, and support continuous organizational change. Many teams still lack the technical, consulting, and leadership capabilities required to meet these expectations.

Operational workload, limited training time, and rapid technological change have created a growing gap between expectations and capabilities. The most useful thing HR leaders can do right now is look at their team and ask honestly: which roles will struggle most if we don't close this gap in the next 12 months? Organizations are unlikely to solve this gap through hiring alone. They must invest in building the skills of the HR workforce they already have.

HRRI Strategic Takeaways

Based on our research, consider the following recommendations:

  • Make capability development part of the job, not an extra task
    The Delphi panel made it clear that workforce readiness is now a strategic priority. Upskilling cannot be optional or occasional. It must be built into how HR work happens. Allocate dedicated learning time each week for HR staff and protect it from operational interruptions.
  • Focus AI training on judgment, not just tools
    Learning how to use AI tools is important but understanding when not to trust AI is just as critical. HR teams must be able to question outputs, identify bias risks, and recognize data limitations. Include risk scenarios, bias examples, and decision simulations in AI training programs.
  • Build practical data literacy across HR roles
    Data literacy should not be limited to analytics specialists. HR business partners and generalists must be able to interpret dashboards, ask better questions, and translate data into decisions. Train HR staff to interpret workforce data and explain insights to leaders.
  • Develop consulting and coaching capability
    As HR shifts from administration to partnership, consulting skills become essential. HR professionals must be able to diagnose problems, influence leaders, and guide performance improvement. Provide training in consulting skills, root-cause analysis, and influencing without authority.
  • Use reverse mentoring to accelerate skill development
    Capability gaps exist across generations and experience levels. Pairing leaders with digital fluency and leaders with business experience can accelerate learning in both directions. Pair senior HR leaders with early-career employees for mutual learning on AI and business strategy.
  • Hire for learning agility, not just experience
    Future-ready HR capability depends on curiosity and adaptability. Hiring decisions should reflect this shift. Prioritize candidates who demonstrate learning agility and problem-solving ability.

The New Employee Value Proposition

Finding: EVP shifts toward strategic alignment and need-based fairness

The Delphi panel identified a shift in how organizations think about the Employee Value Proposition (EVP). Historically, the traditional EVP was a "one-size-fits-all" package defined primarily by competitive pay, standard benefits, and generic perks. The focus is moving away from providing identical experiences for everyone and toward providing support based on individual needs.

Two priorities stand out.

  1. First, 67% of experts identified strategic alignment (employees understanding how their work contributes to the organization) as the most important EVP element.
  2. Second, 50% of experts supported “need-based fairness” and “flexible work design” for EVP, with most expecting organizations to tailor support to individual needs even if employee experiences differ. Only about 10% supported maintaining a mostly standard experience for everyone.

Together, these findings suggest that the traditional “one-size-fits-all” EVP is losing relevance. Competitive pay and benefits remain important, but they are increasingly viewed as baseline expectations rather than differentiators.

Tracing the consensus

Round 1: Fairness is being redefined

In the first Delphi round, panelists described fairness as evolving from treating everyone the same to providing employees with what they need to succeed.

As one respondent noted: “Stop treating fairness as sameness. Fairness isn't giving everyone the same experience; fairness is giving people what they need to thrive.”

Several experts also described EVP less as a benefits package and more as a promise of development, purpose, and meaningful contribution.

One participant states, “The traditional EVP is dead.... Employees stay because the organization makes a credible promise: you will grow here.”

Employees are more likely to stay when they see a credible path for growth, not just access to programs or perks.

Round 2: Organizations are trying to balance fairness and performance

By the second Delphi round, panelists emphasized trust, transparency, and growth as central EVP themes.

Advisory board discussions highlighted growing complexity in conversations about fairness, inclusion, and performance expectations. Some participants described tension between traditional Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (DEI) approaches and increased focus on merit-based systems.

One panelist noted the shift that organizations have to make. "They will have to be able to say no to DEI and yes to MEI (merit) if they want to be competitive and successful."

Dr. Keis stated, "A focus on merit-based systems is crucial... as mandated approaches can be demoralizing."

Others emphasized that fairness and performance are not opposing goals. Inclusion can support performance when employees have clear and equitable access to growth opportunities.

Greg Conner, VP of People and Culture at E-Comm 9-1-1, states, “DEI actually engages people and provides everyone with an opportunity to succeed... merit should be integrated into performance plans rather than seen as opposing DEI.”

Some advisory board members suggested reframing the conversation: Joya Bradley, Head of HR, Americas, Argus Media, Inc., said, “We should reframe the discussion to focus on ‘belonging and inclusion’ instead of the ‘weaponized’ term DEI."

Mark Vickers, HRRI’s Chief Research Analyst, agrees that the term DEI has been culturally weaponized but notes that, when DEI practices were conducted well, they were never “anti-merit.” He states, “In my view, that was always a strawman argument with disturbing undertones, but it’s true that many HR professionals have been trying to avoid cultural conflict by focusing on terms such as inclusion and belonging, both of which have long been crucial to DEIB practices. The point now is to build trust across the entire workforce.”

Christopher Lind, Executive Advisor, Business Transformation, Christopher Lind Co. said, “HR leaders struggle to navigate this climate without abandoning the principles of DEI, which has become a ‘poisonous’ or ‘dangerous’ term to use.”

Taken together, these perspectives suggest that organizations are not moving away from fairness. Instead, they are changing how fairness is communicated and applied in practice.

Trust and transparency also emerged as key EVP priorities. Clear communication about expectations, opportunities, and decisions can help maintain employee trust during periods of change.

Lauren Winans, CEO and Principal HR Consultant, Next Level Benefits HR Consulting stated, “To establish trust, you have to be transparently communicating with your organization." She noted that "without proactive communication, 'people fill in the gap,' which can lead to speculation and broken trust."

Panelists also warned that reducing benefits or focusing too heavily on cost control may weaken employee trust over time. “It just seems like organizations keep taking more away and employees just should be thankful for it," said Christopher Lind, noting that companies trying to figure out the "bare minimum" they can do for employees will likely see it "snap back really hard at some point."

Round 3: Flexibility and growth are becoming core EVP elements

In the final Delphi round, most panelists supported tailoring support to individual needs, even if employee experiences differ. Far fewer supported standardized employee experiences. This reflects a broader shift toward personalization in workforce strategy, similar to trends seen in learning, career development, and work design.

Advisory board participants emphasized flexibility and growth as core EVP components:

One stated, “The EVP of the future must combine flexibility for individuals, fair and transparent systems, a deep sense of purpose, and an inclusive, growth-oriented culture.”

Debbie Ternent, Deputy CHRO at the Rochester Institute of Technology, said, “Employees are citing ‘Flexible work design’ and ‘Growth pathways’ as their actual priorities.”

Together, these responses suggest that EVP is becoming less about standardized offerings and more about helping employees understand their contribution, grow their capabilities, and experience fairness in how opportunities are provided.

Longitudinal trends

In the 2025 research, HR professionals reported strong capabilities in employee-facing areas, with 61% citing "employee engagement and experience" as a specific area of expertise. However, the data also highlighted a disconnect between HR's strategic focus and the employee perspective: while 48% of HR professionals estimated their department met the organization's strategic needs "very well," they estimated that only 38% of employees would say the same. To bridge this gap between HR's focus on experience and employees' perception of HR's strategic value, the 2026 Delphi findings suggest a course correction. Rather than relying on traditional benefits and messaging, the EVP is shifting toward strategic alignment, need-based fairness, growth, and trust, ensuring that employee experience is directly connected to the organization's broader goals.

Implications

Organizations that rely only on compensation or wellness programs may struggle to retain talent if employees do not see alignment between their work, growth, and organizational purpose. A strong EVP now depends on whether employees believe the organization will support their development and provide fair access to opportunity.

HRRI Strategic Takeaways

Based on our research, consider the following recommendations:

  • Build flexibility into your policies
    As organizations move toward need-based fairness, treating everyone the exact same way no longer works. Fairness today means offering flexibility while still having clear guidelines. Review HR policies to see where standard rules may create unfair outcomes. For example, you can allow flexible work schedules for caregivers while keeping clear performance expectations in place. That said, employers must also be careful to uphold all anti-discrimination laws and policies.
  • Make career growth a key part of your EVP
    Opportunities to learn and grow are one of the strongest reasons employees stay. Many now judge employers based on whether they can build new skills or move into new roles internally. Make career paths visible and easy to understand. For instance, share skill maps that show how employees can move from one role to another within the organization.
  • Support fairness through transparency
    Need-based support works best when employees understand how decisions are made. People are more open to different experiences if the reasons are clear. Communicate how decisions about flexibility, rewards, promotions, and development are determined. For example, explain why some roles may have different flexibility options due to business needs.
  • Focus DEI on opportunity and belonging
    Even though views on DEI may differ, there is broad agreement that organizations must remove barriers to success. Emphasize belonging, opportunity, and fairness. Focus on improving access to growth and participation. For example, review promotion processes to identify hidden barriers that may prevent some employees from advancing.
  • Measure employee trust
    Engagement scores do not always reflect how much employees trust leadership. Trust and psychological safety are important parts of a strong employee experience. Add questions about trust to employee surveys. For example, ask employees whether leadership communicates honestly and makes fair decisions.
  • Personalize benefits in a simple way
    Benefits should reflect different employee needs without becoming difficult to manage. Flexible benefits allow employees to choose what matters most to them. Consider offering options like a wellness allowance that employees can use for health, caregiving, or learning.
  • Make sure EVP matches real experience
    Employees quickly notice when a company’s promises do not match reality. EVP messages should reflect what employees actually experience every day. Test your EVP messaging with employee feedback before sharing it widely. For example, gather input through focus groups or listening sessions.
  • Connect employee roles to organizational goals
    Employees want to know how their work supports the organization’s success. Help leaders explain how individual roles contribute to broader goals. For instance, include conversations about how each employee’s work links to company strategy during performance or development discussions.

Breaking The Barriers

Finding: Operational overload remains the primary barrier to strategic HR

Across all three Delphi rounds, one issue appeared consistently: that is, HR teams lack the capacity to operate strategically because too much time is spent on administrative and operational work. Operational overload was identified by 58% of experts as the biggest barrier preventing HR from becoming more strategic.

Other major barriers included:

  • lack of AI and data capability (50%)
  • rigid legacy systems (50%)
  • change fatigue (42%)

Tracing the consensus across Delphi rounds

Round 1: HR is caught in a cycle of administrative demand

In the first round, experts described HR teams as caught in a cycle of administrative demand. Compliance requirements, reporting expectations, manual processes, and routine transactions continue to dominate HR workloads.

Advisory board participants noted that this is not a productivity problem. It is a structural constraint. HR teams are often unable to focus on strategy because operational responsibilities consume most of their time. This creates a reinforcing loop:

One respondent captured this pattern directly: “Operational overload - HR is stretched thin dealing with compliance, admin, and firefighting, leaving little time for creativity or strategic experimentation.”

Round 2: Workload limits HR’s ability to build future-ready capability

Round 2 responses reinforced the connection between operational overload and capability development. Experts noted that administrative workload limits HR’s ability to build new skills, experiment with AI, and strengthen consulting capability.

This helps explain why earlier findings in the study emphasize automation and upskilling as linked priorities. HR cannot build future-ready capability without first creating capacity.

Operational overload therefore emerges as a foundational constraint that affects multiple areas of HR transformation.

Round 3: Automation and skills-based models can help create capacity

By the final round, the panel converged on upskilling HR’s capabilities as the most practical lever for breaking this cycle. Further, 83% of experts supported aggressively scaling administrative automation.

Experts also supported moving toward skills-based operating models, with 58% supporting aggressive scaling of this approach. These models allow organizations to deploy talent based on capability rather than job titles, increasing flexibility and reducing role rigidity.

Together, these two strategies reflect a broader redesign of HR work:

  • automation reduces repetitive workload
  • skills-based models increase organizational flexibility

Dave Ulrich captured the difficulty of making these changes: “Sometimes the most important things to do are the most difficult. Administrivia directly impacts everyone every day at work so gets enormous attention, and everyone can likely recall an administrative hassle so it is personal and relevant. HR overload is dealing with these inevitable hassles.”

This observation reflects the reality that breaking operational overload often requires stopping existing work rather than simply adding new initiatives.

Longitudinal trends

This finding mirrors the 2025 research, where lack of time was identified as HR’s biggest challenge. While the language has shifted from lack of time to operational overload, the underlying issue remains the same. At the same time, expectations for HR have expanded. Organizations increasingly expect HR to lead workforce transformation, support AI adoption, and operate as strategic business partners. These expectations have grown faster than HR’s structural capacity to meet them.

Implications

Operational overload is not simply a workload issue. It is a structural constraint on HR’s ability to evolve.

Across the study, operational overload connects directly to several earlier findings:

  • The AI paradox: automation is necessary to create capacity.
  • The skills crisis: capability development requires protected time.
  • Strategic business acumen: HR needs time to engage with business leaders.

Without reducing operational workload, HR teams cannot realistically meet expectations for strategic partnership.

Experts described a potential path forward as a “stable core, flexible edge” model. In this model:

  • compliance, governance, and organizational values remain stable
  • workflows, teams, and projects become more flexible
  • repetitive administrative work is automated wherever possible

This approach allows HR to maintain reliability while increasing adaptability.

The Delphi panel’s message is clear: HR cannot solve operational overload by working harder. The solution requires redesigning how HR work is structured, delivered, and prioritized.

Advisory board members reinforced this approach. As Christopher Lind noted, AI performs well when “narrowly focused on tasks... so long as they’re repeatable,” suggesting that repetitive administrative processes can form a stable automated core. Debbie Ternent added that reducing administrative burden allows HR to “connect more meaningfully... in organizational culture [and] strategic workforce planning with leaders.”

HRRI Strategic Takeaways

Based on our research, consider the following takeaways:

  • Stop doing work that no longer adds value
    HR cannot become more strategic by simply working harder. Many teams still run reports, meetings, or programs that no longer influence decisions. Reviewing these regularly with business leaders can help identify what is actually used. Eliminating unused reports, shortening meetings, or ending outdated programs can free up time for more important work.
  • Fix basic systems before expanding AI use
    AI works best when HR systems are connected. Adding AI tools to disconnected systems can increase workload instead of reducing it. Before expanding AI, make sure employee data moves easily between systems such as HRIS, ATS, LMS, and payroll, and use the same employee data definitions across platforms to avoid duplicate or manual updates.
  • Automate small, repetitive tasks first
    Automating everyday tasks can quickly reduce workload and create time for higher-value work. Tasks such as interview scheduling, answering common policy or benefits questions, or drafting routine emails can often be streamlined or automated.
  • Assign work based on skills, not job titles
    HR teams often assign work based on roles rather than expertise. Mapping team strengths, such as data analysis, consulting, facilitation, or technology use, and assigning projects based on these capabilities can improve flexibility without changing team structure. Instead, HR leaders can map team member capabilities and then assign projects based on their strengths, making the overall team more effective as well as flexible.
  • Protect time for strategic work
    Strategic work is often pushed aside by urgent tasks. Scheduling regular time for workforce planning, leadership coaching, or capability reviews with business leaders can help ensure that long-term priorities receive attention.
  • Assign responsibility for automation
    Automation projects often stall when no one owns them. Identifying team members interested in improving workflows and giving them responsibility for testing new tools or improving processes can support ongoing progress.
  • Use saved time to build new capabilities
    Efficiency gains should create time for learning new skills. Time saved through automation can be used for AI training, consulting skill development, or workforce planning work. Tracking how this time is used can help ensure it supports long-term growth.

Conclusion

The 2026 Delphi findings highlight a growing gap between what organizations expect from HR and what HR teams currently have the capacity to deliver. While HR is increasingly expected to guide workforce transformation, support AI adoption, and act as a strategic partner, many teams remain constrained by operational workload, skills gaps, and disconnected systems.

HR cannot meet future expectations by simply working harder within existing structures. Organizations must fundamentally redesign HR work by leveraging AI to reduce administrative burdens. However, this automation must be paired with an urgent reinvestment in future-ready capabilities. To drive true organizational impact, HR professionals must develop strategic business acumen, cultivate AI and data literacy, and build strong consulting and coaching skills.

Furthermore, as workforce expectations shift toward growth, fairness, and meaningful contribution, HR must adapt its strategy. Moving beyond standardized programs, successful HR functions will champion need-based fairness, flexible work design, and clear strategic alignment for employees. By combining technological efficiency with these high-touch human capabilities, HR can successfully guide organizations through continuous change.
