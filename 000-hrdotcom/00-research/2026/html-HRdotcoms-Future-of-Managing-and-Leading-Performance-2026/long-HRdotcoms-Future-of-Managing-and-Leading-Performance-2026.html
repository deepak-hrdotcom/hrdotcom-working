HR.com’s Future of Managing and Leading Performance 2026

Executive Summary

Ask any organization if their culture supports performance leadership, and you’ll get a yes. Ask if their performance management processes actually work, and that answer changes fast.

There may be an expectation among organizations that enthusiasm translates to impact, but the reality is much more sobering. While a majority (59%) agree or strongly agree their culture supports performance leadership, less than a third of organizations are highly or very effective in performance management (PM). And just a quarter give their performance leadership a high effectiveness rating.

Managers are stuck at the center of the problem. Nearly three-quarters of organizations say managers lack the skills to coach and develop employees, and only 36% say managers are skilled at setting goals.

Artificial intelligence adoption is poised to help the situation; 59% of organizations plan to use AI to help write performance reviews, though AI adoption alone can’t cure a broken system. To improve PM processes, organizations will need to invest in manager training, build accountability into the everyday process, increase the accuracy of performance reviews, and build trust in PM tools.

This report explores the trends in performance management, discusses the challenges organizations are facing and recommends strategies based on the findings to help organizations improve their performance management processes.

About the Survey

“HR.com’s The Future of Managing and Leading Performance 2026” survey ran from April to July 2026. We gathered responses from 177 HR professionals in multiple industry verticals.

Respondents are located all over the world, but most of them reside in North America, especially the United States. The participants represent a broad cross-section of employers by number of employees, ranging from small businesses to enterprises with 20,000+ employees. Two-thirds of the respondents represent midsized and large organizations.

Major Research Findings

The following are the major research findings from our study:

1. A clear divide remains between managing and leading performance.

  • Only 30% rate their organization as highly or very highly effective in performance management, and just 25% say the same for performance leadership.
  • Among organizations without a supportive culture, lack of leadership training (77%) and managers lacking the skills to coach and develop employees (74%) are cited as the top barriers.

2. Formal performance management processes are nearly universal, but few fully deliver on their objectives.

  • Two-thirds of organizations have a structured, formalized PM process, and just 2% have no process at all.
  • The top objectives of PM are to:
    • help employees learn and grow
    • boost communication between employees and managers
    • meet organizational requirements (e.g., goals, compliance)
  • Only 32% say their process accurately portrays employee performance, and fewer than half find it easy to understand.

3. Compliance-driven and company culture outcomes still outweigh growth-oriented ones.

  • Better legal protection and reinforced company culture are the two most common outcomes of performance management.
  • Just 43% report that their ability to manage and lead performance results in improved employee experience.
  • A large majority of organizations use performance management technology to facilitate reviews (74%) and for goal tracking (72%).
  • Far fewer use PM technology to support learning and development (31%).

4. Feedback happens often, but formal appraisals still anchor the performance cycle.

  • In over 70% of organizations, work-related feedback is given at least once a quarter.
  • Formal appraisals remain far less frequent, with 57% conducted annually, 19% semi-annually, and only 16% more often than semi-annually.
  • Two-thirds of respondents who don’t view performance management as relationship-building say it instead feels like an obligation to get through.
  • Nearly half (47%) point to a lack of manager skill in giving feedback as part of the problem.

5. Manager capability and accountability remain critical weaknesses.

  • Only 36% of respondents say managers are skilled at setting goals and just 29% say the same about having conversations about performance and behaviors.
  • However, just 37% of managers have received adequate training in performance management, and nearly a quarter of organizations still don’t hold managers accountable for PM activities in any specific way.
  • Tracking whether managers complete formal appraisals remains the top accountability method (47%), but it’s down from three-fifths last year.

6. Interest in AI for performance management is accelerating faster than actual adoption.

  • Half of organizations report they are not currently using AI for PM, though 43% expect PM to become more AI-enabled.
  • Concerns about accuracy or reliability (34%) and data privacy or ethics (32%) remain the top AI barriers, but adoption is still rising; 59% plan to use AI to help write performance reviews, up 21 percentage points from last year.
  • For a third of organizations, AI hasn’t reshaped performance expectations at all, and another third simply don’t know if or how it has.

7. Compared to laggards, performance management leaders are:

  • more than twice as likely to say PM improves relationships between managers and employees (82% vs. 38%).
  • much more likely to hold managers accountable through:
    • manager KPIs (50% vs. 12%)
    • employee feedback (54% vs. 13%)
    • engagement/retention tracking (54% vs. 17%)
  • more likely to use diverse performance metrics, including:
    • goal achievement (81% vs. 54%)
    • 360-degree feedback (50% vs. 12%)
  • more than twice as likely to plan on using AI for personalized performance feedback (47% vs. 20%).

Performance Management and Performance Leadership

Finding: Fewer than a third rate their organization highly in performance management, and even fewer say the same about performance leadership

Even though organizations have had performance management processes for decades, just 30% say it is effective to a high or very high extent in their organization. Performance leadership rates somewhat worse. Only 25% say it is highly or very highly effective, and almost a third say it has low or very low effectiveness.

This lack of effective performance is an ongoing problem. In 2024, Gallup reported that only 2% of CHROs strongly agree their performance management system inspires employees to improve, and Deloitte’s 2025 Global Human Capital Trends study found that 61% of managers and 72% of workers could not say they trust their organization’s performance management process.

Performance management and leadership-based cohorts

To better analyze the data from our survey on managing and leading performance, we broke our respondents into groups based on responses to an option in the survey question cited above.

  • Performance management (PM) leaders: respondents who answered the question “To what extent is your organization effective in performance management and performance leadership” as high and very high.
  • Performance management (PM) laggards: those who answered the same questions as moderate, low, or very low degree.

Within the PM leader and laggard sections, we will reveal findings that come from the examination of the data in a way that identifies correlations between various organizational factors and the perceived effectiveness of leading and managing performance. Correlation does not prove causation, of course, but it may point our attention toward possible best practices and potential strategies.

Finding: Just 14% strongly agree that their organization’s culture is conducive to performance leadership

Almost three-fifths of respondents agree or strongly agree that their culture supports performance leadership. What does a non-supportive culture actually look like in practice? Often, it’s not outright opposition but a gap between stated enthusiasm and follow-through. Lena Finch, Vice President of People & Culture at Allego, observes this pattern around learning and development:

"There is a lot of excitement and support for learning and development programs, and people genuinely want to participate. The challenge is that everyone is working hard and managing demanding day-to-day priorities, so development can easily get sidelined. When participation does not match this enthusiasm, it becomes discouraging and highlights the need to create more space and accountability for learning."

— Lena Finch
Vice President of People & Culture at Allego

The disconnect can also be traced to disconnected systems, as Edie L. Goldberg, President & Founder at E.L. Goldberg & Associates, points out, "Organizations encourage performance leadership, they support it, but their systems don’t support it in total. They give lip service to it rather than embedding it in how they operate."

For the purpose of this report:

Large organizations have 1,000 or more employees, midsized organizations have 100 to 999 employees, and small organizations have 99 or fewer employees.

Together, Finch’s and Goldberg’s observations suggest that a non-supportive culture often is one where encouragement exists, but there aren’t the systems or follow-through to back it up.

By Organization Size

Small organizations are the most likely to strongly agree that their culture supports performance leadership (24%), compared with 9% of midsized and 13% of large organizations. However, 90% of small organizations say managers lack the time to lead performance effectively, versus just 44% of large organizations. Large organizations are much more likely to identify managers’ coaching and development skills as the main barrier (89%).

Finding: More than three-quarters cite lack of leadership training as a key cultural barrier to performance leadership

Among organizations that do not have a culture conducive to performance leadership, lack of leadership training and development (77%) remains the biggest barrier. A large proportion also say managers lack the skills to coach and develop employees (74%), managers lack the time to effectively lead performance (71%), and there’s resistance to change from employees or management (71%).

But these training and time gaps may just be symptoms of a bigger root cause, that is, who gets chosen to manage in the first place. Catarina Edlund, Chief of HR, UNFCCC (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change), says:

"The real failure in performance management happens earlier in the HR process, at recruitment or promotion. We’re not testing for growth mindset or whether someone is genuinely interested in developing other people. That’s core to being a manager and a leader, and it often gets missed."

— Catarina Edlund
Chief of HR, UNFCCC (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change)

In other words, organizations may be trying to train people into skills and dispositions they were never screened for to begin with.

Edie L. Goldberg adds, "It’s the fundamental failure to select for the right attributes for a manager—someone who has the skills and the interest in doing the managing part of the job." The consequence shows up in how managers’ time gets spent, she goes on to say, "Managers spend 90% of their time in individual-contributor parts of the job and only 10% managing. It’s just the wrong math."

Given that organizations may face multiple, compounding barriers to successful performance leadership, it will be important to diagnose what sort of interventions, whether in training, in workload, or further upstream in hiring and promotion criteria are needed to overcome enough of these issues to drive a meaningful shift in culture.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Tackle multiple barriers at once, not just the most visible one.
    Performance leadership rarely stalls for a single reason. When several barriers are working against it simultaneously, a response aimed at just one won’t be enough to shift the needle. Design interventions broad enough to address the real mix of obstacles at play.
  • Push top leadership to back the important work.
    Culture-building and leadership development are easy to deprioritize when they compete with whatever feels most pressing in the moment. Senior leadership needs to actively protect space for this longer-term work, or it will keep losing out to short-term demands.
  • Close the gap between stated culture and actual follow-through.
    Almost 60% agree their culture supports performance leadership, but only 14% strongly agree, and the gap often isn’t opposition so much as encouragement without systems or follow-through to back it up. Audit whether your systems and workload actually reinforce the growth mindset the organization claims to value.

Aligning Performance with Business Outcomes

Finding: Nearly half indicate their PM process is structured and formalized

Two-thirds have a structured PM process; for some, it is standardized across the organization, while others vary it between units. Few organizations (2%) do not have a performance management process. Performance management processes are frequently criticized, yet almost all organizations believe they need them.

By Organization Size

Performance management becomes more structured as organizations grow. Large organizations are more than twice as likely as small organizations to report a structured process that varies across departments or regions (26% vs. 12%). Small organizations are also the only group where some respondents report having no performance management process at all (7%).

Finding: Learning, communication, and organizational requirements are among the top objectives of performance management

We asked respondents about the objectives of their performance management processes. One of the striking features of performance management is that it is meant to achieve many different goals. The top five objectives are:

  • help individual employees learn and grow
  • meet organizational requirements (e.g., goals, compliance)
  • boost communication between employees and their managers
  • help the organization improve overall performance
  • boost the individual performance of employees

These objectives tend to be mutually reinforcing, that is, progress on one typically drives progress on the others. Organizations don’t necessarily need to choose between these goals, but can instead design a single, well-integrated process that advances all five simultaneously.

Finding: Better legal protection and reinforced company culture are the two most common outcomes of performance management

Half of respondents in organizations with some form of PM process say their performance management and/or leadership approach strengthens legal protections to a high or very high degree. A similar number say that it strengthens company culture to a high or very high degree.

It’s worth noting, however, that 43% say the ability to manage and lead performance results in improved employee experience. Yet advisory board members caution that this connection isn’t automatic. Andrew Lane, Director, PM and Employee Engagement at George Mason University, says, "We’re starting to really try to link performance and engagement to employee experience. It’s a struggle, because supervisors are already in the weeds doing their own daily job, not the management component."

That struggle helps explain why employee experience, though a meaningful outcome for some organizations, hasn’t yet emerged as one of the objectives cited by half or more of respondents.

Finding: PM leaders have dramatically greater success across a variety of performance indicators

PM leaders are far more likely than laggards to rate high or very high on each of the six results assessed in the chart below. One notable difference is that PM leaders are about four times more likely to achieve better employee development to a high or very high degree (75% vs. 19%).

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to say their PM processes result in all listed outcomes to a high or very high degree.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Connect employee goals to enterprise priorities.
    Among organizations without a culture conducive to performance leadership, limited alignment between individual goals and business priorities is one of the most commonly cited barriers. Making that link explicit and visible is also how HR earns senior leadership’s confidence that PM is more than an administrative exercise.
  • Regularly diagnose where the PM process is breaking down.
    Among organizations struggling with performance leadership culture, barriers are numerous and often compounding—lack of training, manager time constraints, resistance to change, and poor communication all rank highly. Build in a regular audit of the process, by team, by manager, by outcome, so HR can target interventions.
  • Design a single, integrated PM process rather than a patchwork of initiatives.
    Five distinct objectives (learning and growth, communication, organizational requirements, org performance, and individual performance) are each cited by over 60% of organizations, and these goals tend to reinforce one another. Rather than running separate efforts for each, build one well-integrated process that advances all of them together.

Effective PM Elements

Finding: Most PM processes include both positive feedback and critique

The most common features of performance management processes are balanced feedback and a discussion of goals. Two-thirds include a conversation about development.

Fewer than half say their PM process is easy to understand and only 32% say the process accurately portrays employee performance, which is one of the primary goals of the process.

Part of the gap may be that development conversations, even when they happen, stop short of giving employees a concrete picture of what success looks like. As Kimberly Hodge, Talent Development Manager at MWM DesignGroup, describes it:

"With every development plan we have, we also have a success plan now—what does success actually look like for that person? That extra step helps managers go beyond 'here's the skill that's missing' to 'here's what you need to do to be successful.'"

— Kimberly Hodge
Talent Development Manager at MWM DesignGroup

In other words, pairing a development plan with a success plan may be one way to close the distance between having a PM process and having one that employees feel actually captures their performance.

By Organization Size

Small organizations are far more likely to say their process allows for continuous feedback (67%), compared with 40% of midsized and 44% of large organizations. They are also much more likely than large organizations to describe the process as easy to understand (51% vs. 27%).

Finding: Leaders are more than twice as likely as laggards to say PM improves relationships between managers and employees

PM leaders are over twice as likely (82% vs. 38%) to deploy a PM process that improves relationships between managers and employees. This focus on relationships is impressive given that any discussion of performance can be stressful for both employee and manager.

Another important difference between PM leaders and laggards is allowing for continuous feedback (85% vs. 29%). The continuous feedback takes some of the pressure off the final performance management discussion and reduces the likelihood of the employee being surprised by the results.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to say their PM process improves relationships between managers and employees and allows for continuous feedback.

Finding: About two-thirds say managers see performance management as simply an obligation to get through

Two-thirds of respondents who don’t view performance management as relationship-building between manager and employees say it feels like an obligation rather than a meaningful process. This result identifies a clear problem for those organizations to focus on.

Finding: Work-related feedback is given at least once a quarter in the majority of organizations

There is a great deal of variation in how frequently employees get feedback and have conversations about performance or career goals, ranging from “weekly” to “never.” There is less variation in how frequently employees get formal appraisals, with 57% getting annual appraisals and 19% semi-annual.

That variation often shows up not just in timing but in format, particularly around peer feedback. Lena Finch notes that a formal structure isn’t the only path:

"We do not have a formal 360 process, but we foster a culture where feedback is shared regularly across teams, among peers, and between employees and managers as an important way to learn, grow, and improve. 'You are expected to learn and grow' is one of our operating principles."

— Lena Finch
Vice President of People & Culture at Allego

Kimberly Hodge takes a more structured approach, but one that still spans multiple channels rather than relying on a single mechanism.

"There are so many ways to do peer-to-peer feedback, formal 360s, private notes only the manager sees, and public one-to-one recognition."

Between Finch’s informal, daily approach and Hodge’s layered, multi-channel system, it’s clear that peer feedback doesn’t have to wait for the annual or semi-annual appraisal cycle to happen.

Finding: Most organizations incorporate performance ratings into their PM process

Nearly three-fifths of organizations incorporate goal achievement into their performance management process. Over half also use self-rating. However, only about one-third use behavior-related scores. Some HR professionals like behavior-related scores to capture performance that doesn’t show up in goal achievement. The difficulty is that assessing behavior is typically highly subjective, which can create challenges when delivering a fair and defensible system.

Finding: PM leaders integrate diverse metrics into performance management far more than laggards

Performance management leaders adopt a broader and more data-driven approach, with 81% including goal achievement metrics compared to 54% of laggards. They also incorporate a wider range of insights, such as 360-degree feedback and engagement-related scores at 50% each, meanwhile only 12% of laggards incorporate 360-degree feedback and even fewer (7%) track engagement scores.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to use the listed PM metrics.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Modernize performance tools to reflect real contributions.
    Only 32% of organizations say their PM process accurately captures actual performance. Closing that gap means investing in tools and metrics that map more closely to what people actually do, not just what’s easy to measure.
  • Reposition performance management as a growth conversation, not a compliance task.
    Half of organizations already say PM strengthens legal protection, but far fewer say it improves output quality or organizational performance, and two-thirds of managers who don’t see PM as relationship-building describe it as an obligation to get through. Give managers the frameworks and prompts to turn reviews into real development conversations, documenting fairly and defensibly without letting compliance crowd out growth.
  • Normalize frequent, informal career conversations.
    Building both into the regular cadence of work strengthens team dynamics and manager-employee relationships alike, but only if managers have the skills to lead these conversations well.

Manager Effectiveness in PM

Finding: Just over a third of managers are skilled at setting goals

To be good at performance management, managers need to be good at setting goals and having conversations about performance and behaviors. Most managers are not strong in those areas. Only 36% are skilled at setting goals, and only 29% are skilled at having conversations about performance and behaviors. This lack of skill explains why only 31% of managers were rated as skilled in performance management.

One reason most managers lack the necessary skills is likely a lack of sufficient training; only 37% have received adequate training in performance management. SHRM research found that 61% of HR professionals say fewer than half of their managers effectively address underperformance with direct reports, and 41% say their organization does not provide adequate training and resources for managers to conduct effective performance reviews.

Finding: Compared to their laggard counterparts, PM leaders report vastly higher confidence in managerial performance skills

Only 10% of respondents in PM laggards agree that their managers are skilled at performance leadership, compared to 60% of PM leader organizations. And over 70% of PM leaders say most of their managers have received sufficient training in performance management.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to agree or strongly agree that their managers possess all listed skills required for PM.

Finding: Tracking formal appraisals remains the top accountability tool, though its use has declined

How do organizations hold managers accountable for performance management? Roughly half of organizations (47%) track whether managers complete formal appraisals. Use of employee feedback to evaluate managers has grown to 26%, up from 17% last year. Engagement and retention tracking held steady at 26%. Notably, nearly a quarter of organizations still don’t hold managers accountable in any specific way, suggesting that despite more organizations diversifying their accountability methods, a persistent minority still lack meaningful reinforcement altogether.

Finding: PM leader organizations hold managers accountable for PM more rigorously than their laggard counterparts do

Only 8% of PM leader organizations lack accountability measures compared to 33% of laggard organizations. PM leaders are also much more likely to hold managers accountable using engagement or retention rates of direct reports (54% vs. 17%), employee feedback (54% vs. 13%), as well as by incorporating the process into managerial KPIs (50% vs. 12%).

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to hold managers accountable in the listed ways. PM laggards are significantly more likely than leaders to say managers are not held accountable in any particular way.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Build managers’ core performance management skills.
    Only 36% of managers are skilled at setting goals and 29% at leading performance and behavior conversations, largely because just 37% have received adequate training at all. Targeted programs in goal setting, coaching conversations, developmental planning, and difficult conversations close these specific gaps directly, rather than relying on generic leadership training.
  • Close the baseline accountability gap before adding new metrics.
    23% of organizations hold managers accountable in no specific way at all. Before building out new KPIs or measurement systems, make sure every manager has at least one accountability mechanism in place. The gap between "some accountability" and "none" is one of the sharpest dividing lines between leader and laggard organizations.
  • Once accountability exists, measure outcomes and feedback, not just completion.
    Appraisal completion is still the most common accountability method (47%), but it only confirms tasks got done, not whether performance management is actually working. PM leaders go further: they build performance responsibilities into managers’ formal KPIs and promotion criteria, evaluate managers on their direct reports’ engagement and retention and incorporate employee feedback into manager assessments. These outcomes and feedback based measures capture real managerial effectiveness.

Leveraging PM Technology

Finding: Use of digital technology for performance management has grown

Nearly half of organizations (48%) now use digital technology to a high or very high degree for performance management, up from about two-fifths last year. Combined with moderate use (18%), this means the majority of organizations are leveraging some form of digital platform. Still, 18% report not using digital technology at all, and 16% use it only to a small degree, indicating that a meaningful share of organizations has yet to adopt these tools in any substantial way. There’s still room for many HR professionals to better leverage digital platforms to streamline processes, enable real-time feedback, support goal tracking, and generate actionable insights.

Finding: Most organizations rely on tools integrated into HR systems to support performance management

We asked respondents what digital tools their organization uses to support performance management. Performance management tools integrated into a broader HRIS platform are by far the most common choice, used by 70% of organizations, more than double the next closest option. Spreadsheet or database programs like Excel remain a notable fallback at 33%, suggesting many organizations still rely on manual or semi-manual processes alongside or instead of dedicated software.

Finding: PM technology is centered around reviews and goal tracking

Most organizations use technology to facilitate performance reviews (74%) and track goals (72%), but far fewer use it to support learning and development (31%). Analytics and reporting (49%) and evaluating performance against goals (47%) also rank fairly high, reinforcing that these systems are primarily built for evaluation rather than growth.

Notably, two of the top three goals of performance reviews are to help employees learn and grow and to boost communication, so there remains a serious disconnect between the goals of PM and the capabilities of most systems used by responding organizations. Closing this gap will likely require organizations to prioritize development- and communication-focused features when selecting or upgrading PM technology.

Craig Friedman, Sr. Talent Advisor and Skills Practice Leader at St. Charles Consulting Group, argues that this disconnect persists because organizations misdiagnose where the fix belongs:

"There’s got to be a multi-channel assessment optimized for leadership and managerial skills, because most of what we develop is not focused on tracking those skills, tying them to longitudinal career tracking by linking the skills to the job architecture, measuring outcomes by connecting them to business outcomes, and better reporting, using skills intelligence to tie it to workforce planning."

— Craig Friedman
Sr. Talent Advisor and Skills Practice Leader, St. Charles Consulting Group

The result, in his view, is a pattern of piecemeal fixes rather than integration. He says, "It’s too often just 'let’s build a training course,' and then performance management is rarely tied to what’s going on in those trainings."

His point sharpens the stakes: if development and communication are treated as separate initiatives rather than built into the PM system itself, technology upgrades alone won’t close it. Closing this gap will likely require organizations to prioritize development- and communication-focused features when selecting or upgrading PM technology, and to treat systems alignment as the underlying fix, rather than treating these capabilities as secondary to compliance and evaluation functions.

Finding: PM technology capabilities are more advanced among PM leader organizations

The technology systems of PM leader organizations are three times as likely to aid learning and development (54% vs. 18%) and more than twice as likely to track and facilitate meetings and check-ins (58% vs. 27%) compared to their laggard counterparts.

This indicates that leader organizations lean on technology to support ongoing development and ongoing dialogue, reinforcing a more continuous and growth-oriented approach to performance management.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to say their PM tech systems have all listed capabilities.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Fix the systems-alignment problem, not just the training problem.
    Two of the top three goals of performance reviews are helping employees learn and grow and boosting communication, yet the technology most organizations use rarely supports either. As one contributor put it, this gap tends to get treated as a training issue when it’s really an HR systems-alignment issue: adding a course doesn’t help if the PM system itself was never built to reinforce it.
  • Prioritize platforms enabling recognition, ongoing check-ins, and multi-source feedback.
    PM leader organizations are three times as likely to have technology that aids learning and development (54% vs. 18%) and more than twice as likely to support meeting and check-in tracking (58% vs. 27%), compared to laggards. This is one of the clearest technology differentiators between leader and laggard organizations.
  • Match technology investment to organization size and scalability needs.
    Realistically, many organizations won’t have a large budget specifically dedicated to PM technology, but that doesn’t mean they should compromise on quality. Smaller organizations especially should seek out scalable solutions that offer the same efficiency and development benefits as larger enterprise systems, minus the enterprise-level price tag. The right platform should be able to grow alongside your organization.

Future Trends in PM

Finding: Organizations most commonly say performance management in the future will include more manager training

What’s predicted to happen in the world of PM over the next two years? A majority of organizations anticipate performance management will include more manager training (60%). This will help address the issue where managers are often promoted for excelling as individual contributors, but aren’t prepared to lead, coach, or evaluate others. Without dedicated training, many are left to run reviews, feedback conversations, and goal setting on instinct rather than a defined skill set.

About half also believe PM will become more aligned with business goals (52%) and incorporate more frequent or continuous feedback (53%) over the next two years. Development-focused change (52%) follows closely behind, suggesting a strong shift toward proactive, growth-oriented practices rather than purely evaluative ones. AI-enablement (43%) and skills-based approaches (40%) also register meaningfully, pointing to emerging shifts in both technology and philosophy. Fewer organizations expect change centered on employee experience and well-being (32%) or individual personalization (28%), suggesting these areas may lag behind more structural or process-driven changes.

Only 5% expect no change at all, and just 1% believe performance management will disappear entirely, indicating near-universal consensus that evolution is coming, even if organizations differ on where to focus first.

AI and PM

Finding: Half of organizations have no plans to use AI in performance management

Half of organizations report they will not use AI in performance management at all over the next two years, while another 26% are only exploring or piloting the technology. Just 17% are using AI in a limited capacity, and a mere 8% are using it broadly across the organization, meaning only one in four organizations have moved past the experimentation stage.

This is a notable contrast to the previous finding, where many expect performance management to become more AI-enabled in the next two years. Together, these results suggest that while there’s directional interest in AI, actual adoption is still in the early stages. Organizations may be waiting for clearer use cases, proven ROI, or greater confidence in the technology before committing further.

The Talent Strategy Group’s 2026 Performance Management Report found that AI adoption in performance management remains nascent and largely experimental across every process area measured, from goal setting to compensation decisions.

Finding: For most organizations, AI has yet to reshape performance expectations, but early signals point to rising demands

For a third of organizations (32%), performance expectations haven’t changed due to AI, and another third (33%) simply don’t know if or how they have, suggesting many organizations haven’t yet connected AI adoption to how they evaluate performance. Where change is being felt, it centers on expecting employees to actually use AI in their work (20%), followed by expectations for higher quality (15%), rising concerns about data privacy or monitoring (14%), higher volume of output expected (12%), and faster turnaround (11%).

Finding: Trust and integration issues are the biggest barriers to using AI in performance management

Concerns about accuracy or reliability (34%) and data privacy or ethical concerns (32%) top the list of challenges organizations face with AI in performance management, suggesting that trust in the technology is the primary hurdle. Lack of integration with HR systems and insufficient training, both at 25% follow closely, pointing to practical, fixable gaps in infrastructure and preparedness. Notably, 24% report no significant challenges, showing a meaningful subset of organizations have navigated adoption smoothly.

Employee distrust or resistance (23%) outpaces manager distrust or resistance (18%), suggesting skepticism runs deeper among those being evaluated than those doing the evaluating. Leadership buy-in, cost, and measuring impact rank as comparatively smaller barriers, well behind concerns about trust and reliability.

Finding: Most say their organization will use AI to help write performance reviews

Uncertainty around AI use has dropped sharply, with just 10% now saying they don’t know how AI will be used, down from about a third last year. Adoption has also broadened considerably across specific purposes: 59% plan to use AI to help write performance reviews in the next two years—up by 21 percentage points since last year.

While personalized feedback is lower down on the list, it saw the largest relative jump, rising to 35% from just 16% last year. This suggests that we may see an even greater increase in adoption over the next two years. Gartner research found managers using AI in performance management saved an average of four hours across process steps, provided the organization paired it with clear guardrails and manager accountability.

Efficiency gains seem to follow organizations that treat AI as a support to managerial judgment rather than a replacement for it.

Finding: PM leaders are twice as likely to use AI to enable real-time tracking in the near future

PM leader organizations are twice as likely as laggards to plan on using AI to enable real-time tracking (41% vs. 20%) and are a little over two times as likely to offer personalized performance feedback (47% vs. 20%) over the next two years. They’re also nearly twice as likely to use AI to improve performance analysis and assessment (65% vs. 33%).

We can infer that leader organizations are approaching AI as a tool for deeper, more individualized support, further reinforcing the more development-focused, employee-centered approach that distinguishes them from laggards.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that PM leaders are significantly more likely than PM laggards to use AI for PM in all listed ways over the next two years.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Build organizational understanding and strategy around AI use in PM.
    Half of organizations still don’t plan to use AI in performance management at all, and another 26% are only exploring or piloting it. Develop a clear, deliberate plan for how AI supports, rather than replaces, human judgment before adoption pressure outpaces strategy.
  • Pair AI adoption with clear guardrails and accountability to address distrust.
    Accuracy and reliability concerns (34%) and data privacy or ethical concerns (32%) remain the top barriers to AI adoption in PM, ahead of integration and training gaps. This is compounded by a trust gap: Nearly a quarter of respondents believe employees distrust AI, and 18% believe managers feel the same way. Left unaddressed, this distrust will slow adoption and undermine the accuracy and fairness people expect from AI-assisted reviews.
  • Stay current as AI use in PM accelerates.
    Uncertainty about how AI will be used has dropped sharply, from about a third of organizations last year to just 10% now, while use across writing reviews (59%), goal tracking (52%), and personalized feedback (35%, nearly doubled) has risen quickly. Given how fast this is moving, keep monitoring developments both inside and outside the PM function so your strategy doesn’t fall behind actual practice.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaway 1: Close the gap between performance management and performance leadership

Treat leadership capability building, such as coaching, trust, and mentoring, as a distinct priority rather than a byproduct of the PM process, given that organizations rate performance leadership even lower than performance management itself.

Key Takeaway 2: Select and promote for coaching aptitude, not just individual performance

Screen for growth mindset and interest in developing others during hiring and promotion decisions, so managers arrive with the disposition to lead people, not just a track record as strong individual contributors.

Key Takeaway 3: Pair every development conversation with a concrete success plan

Go beyond naming a skill gap to co-defining what success actually looks like for that person, so employees leave conversations with a clear, actionable picture of what to do next.

Key Takeaway 4: Normalize continuous, informal feedback alongside formal reviews

Build habits of everyday peer- and manager-feedback conversations, since organizations that sustain frequent, informal check-ins see far stronger performance and engagement results than those relying solely on annual or semi-annual appraisals.

Key Takeaway 5: Align PM technology with what performance management is actually meant to achieve

Prioritize development- and communication-focused features, not just review automation and goal tracking, to close the gap between the stated goals of performance management and the systems used to run it.

Key Takeaway 6: Strengthen manager accountability beyond appraisal completion

Combine tracking of formal reviews with employee feedback, engagement and retention data, and manager KPIs, since a meaningful share of organizations still hold managers accountable in no specific way at all.

Key Takeaway 7: Move deliberately from AI experimentation to targeted adoption

Focus early AI use on high-value applications such as review writing, goal tracking, and training recommendations, while addressing the accuracy and data-privacy concerns that remain the biggest barriers to trust.

Key Takeaway 8: Diversify the data behind performance evaluations

Combine goal achievement with self-ratings, behavioral indicators, 360-degree feedback, and engagement data to build a fuller and fairer picture of performance.

Key Takeaway 9: Invest in manager training that targets specific, named skill gaps

Focus development on goal setting and performance conversations specifically, since fewer than four in ten managers report feeling adequately trained in either area.

Key Takeaway 10: Reinforce culture with follow-through, not just encouragement

Pair leadership training with clear communication of expectations and consistent follow-through, since a supportive culture, not stated enthusiasm alone, is what allows performance leadership to actually take hold.
