HR.com’s State of Rewards and Recognition 2026

Executive Summary

Rewards and recognition (R&R) have become near-universal, but doing them well remains the exception. In fact, while only 2% of organizations have no R&R program at all, just 30% rate their rewards programs as highly effective and 32% say the same of recognition. The gap between having a program and having one that works is where the real opportunity lies.

That opportunity matters because recognition pays off where organizations need it most: culture and people. The top advantages employers cite are improving company culture (65%) and enhancing the employee experience (60%), and employees agree the effect is real, with nearly nine in ten saying that feeling appreciated and valued improves their performance. Yet recognition often fails to reach them consistently. Just over a third receive meaningful recognition from their manager on a weekly basis, and many organizations measure their efforts so irregularly that they cannot tell what is working, with 40% having no fixed review schedule and 20% not evaluating effectiveness at all.

This study examines what is driving effective R&R today, the challenges organizations face, and the practices that set the organizations with strong organizations apart.

About the Survey

HR.com's “State of Rewards and Recognition 2026” survey ran from March 2026 to May 2026. We collected 156 usable responses from HR professionals across virtually every industry vertical.

The participants represent a broad cross-section of employers by number of employees, ranging from small businesses with fewer than 50 employees to enterprises with 20,000+ employees. Sixty-eight percent of our respondents represent mid-sized or large organizations.

We also included some data from our “Workplace Culture & Connection” survey conducted in the last quarter of 2025. This survey gathered 5,538 responses.

Defining Rewards and Recognition

For the purpose of this study and the survey on which it is based, we defined rewards and recognition (R&R) as the system organizations use to acknowledge contributions by individual employees or larger groups such as teams or departments.

  • Rewards tend to be tangible perks such as bonuses, gifts, paid events, etc.
  • Recognition tends to be public or private employee appreciation, thank you notes, etc.

Rewards and recognition tend to be grouped into a larger strategy or program, so for the purposes of this survey we will often approach them as a single strategic initiative.

Major Research Findings

1. Most organizations rate their R&R programs as only moderately effective, and recognition is most closely tied to values and performance.

  • About three-quarters (76%) rate their rewards program at least moderately effective, and 70% say the same for recognition.
  • However, relatively few rate their programs at the top: just 30% call rewards effective to a high or very high degree, and 32% say the same for recognition.
  • Recognition is most commonly tied to a high or very high extent to:
    • values (45%)
    • performance (42%)
    • tenure (31%)
    • professional milestones (30%)

2. While hybrid R&R programs tend to be most common, responsibility for these processes varies.

  • The most common R&R approach is a mixture of formal and informal programs (56%).
  • The CEO is most likely to be responsible for approving R&R initiatives (23%) while the CHRO is most likely to be responsible for implementing R&R (23%).
  • R&R nominations are most commonly made by managers (72%) and peers (64%).

3. R&R can have major advantages, but challenges also persist.

  • The top advantages of R&R programs and capabilities are:
    • improving company culture (65%)
    • enhancing the employee experience (60%)
    • improving employees' sense of belonging (51%)
  • However, respondents say the top challenges with offering R&R programs are:
    • cost (49%)
    • inconsistent application of R&R, e.g., concerns about favoritism (46%)
    • loss of engagement among those not receiving R&R (38%)

4. Organizations measure R&R effectiveness in various ways, but often with no fixed schedule.

  • Many organizations (40%) have no fixed schedule for reviewing R&R effectiveness, 9% never review it at all, and 20% do not evaluate effectiveness in any way. Just 23% review at least quarterly.
  • The most widely used methods to measure R&R effectiveness are:
    • annual or periodic engagement surveys (45%)
    • observing trends in employee participation rates (42%)
    • observing improved employee confidence or enthusiasm (39%)

5. Organizations expect technology and AI to take on a growing role in enhancing R&R.

  • The top ways respondents expect technology to support R&R are:
    • improving the ease of tracking and reporting (55%)
    • allowing for better insights through analytics (51%)
    • reducing administrative burden through automation (46%)
  • AI is expected to improve R&R initiatives by:
    • tracking what employees recognize others for (36%)
    • tracking employee performance (34%)
    • enabling faster, in-the-moment recognition (32%)

6. When compared to the less effective R&R laggard organizations, the more effective R&R leader organizations are:

  • four times more likely as laggards to have formal R&R programs — in fact, leaders are the only group with fully formal programs (22% vs. 0% of laggards)
  • more than three times more likely to examine the impact of R&R on employee performance metrics, while 43% of laggards do not measure effectiveness at all
  • more than twice as likely to review their program's effectiveness continually
  • nearly three times more likely to believe technology will help personalize rewards

The State of R&R Programs

Finding: Most rate the effectiveness of their organization’s R&R program as moderate

We asked respondents to rate the effectiveness of their organization’s R&R programs. Respondents most commonly say these programs are just moderate. About three-quarters rate their rewards program as at least moderately effective, and 70% say the same for recognition. However, relatively few rate these programs at the high or very high level, suggesting significant room to strengthen R&R initiatives.

Key Drivers of R&R

Finding: The top advantage of R&R programs is helping employees feel valued

Improving company culture (65%) and enhancing the employee experience (60%) are the top-cited advantages of R&R programs. These two benefits have consistently ranked highest, suggesting that leaders have internalized recognition as a culture-building tool.

The next cluster of advantages reflects the interpersonal and relational dimensions of recognition. Improving sense of belonging (51%) and improving employee attitudes (47%) show how R&R can positively impact employee well-being. Boosting feelings of trust with leadership (41%) demonstrates how recognition functions as a bridge between employees and those who lead them.

Operational benefits follow closely behind, with reducing turnover (39%), improving employee well-being (35%), and increasing productivity and performance (35%) all notably cited. Together, these reflect a growing awareness that R&R programs carry measurable business outcomes.

Finding: Nearly nine in ten employees believe feeling appreciated and valued improves their performance

In our second study on the impact of culture on the workplace, we asked employees whether feeling appreciated and valued improves their performance, and the response was nearly unanimous, nearly all agree or strongly agree. When this large a majority of a workforce links appreciation to their own output, under-recognition is a serious productivity risk. Gallup finds employees who feel fulfilled by the recognition they receive are about four times as likely to be engaged.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Move programs beyond moderate. With only 30% rating rewards and 32% rating recognition as highly effective, most organizations have room to climb. Identify what separates a moderately good program from a highly strong one in the context of your organization. Typically, it’s consistency, frequency, and relevance, and target those specific gaps rather than expanding the program wholesale.
  • Define what "effective" actually means for your organization. Effectiveness should be measured against your own goals, whether that is retention, engagement, or cultural alignment. Establish two or three concrete outcomes you expect R&R to influence so you can judge the program against intended impact rather than activity alone.
  • Make the business case with operational benefits. Reducing turnover (39%), improving well-being (35%), and increasing productivity (35%) are all notably cited. Use these outcomes to justify continued investment and to position R&R as a driver of measurable results, not just morale.

Today’s R&R Program Structures and Processes

Finding: Most organizations use a mixture of formal and informal R&R programs and processes

We asked respondents about the formality of their R&R programs, the most common response was a mixture of formal and informal (56%). These hybrid approaches allow organizations to maintain consistency through structured programs while still enabling spontaneous, timely recognition.

Formal-only programs were reported by just 14% of organizations, and informal-only by 18%. Service-award-only programs accounted for 9%. The data suggests a continued shift toward flexibility, with hybrid approaches increasingly the norm across organizations of all sizes.

R&R cohort classification

To garner a better understanding of what may be driving effective R&R and rewards practices today, we wanted to take a closer look at what separates organizations with highly effective R&R practices from those with less effective practices. To do this, we divided respondents into two cohorts:

  • R&R leaders: These organizations represent respondents who answered “high” or “very high” to the survey question, “How would you rate the effectiveness of your organization’s R&R programs and/or capabilities?”
  • R&R laggards: These organizations represent respondents who answered, “very low,” “low”, or “moderate” to that same question.

Of course, correlation is not the same as causation. While we cannot state that any particular practice will lead to success in rewards and recognition, we do see intriguing relationships that might result in greater success.

Note: Effective means your organization is getting the desired impact based on its goals.

Finding: R&R leaders are four times more likely than laggards to have formal R&R programs and processes, while laggards are more likely to have informal ones

While the majority of R&R leaders (66%) employ a mixed approach to recognition, they are investing in fully formal programs (22%). Laggards lean heavily on informal, impromptu recognition, with nearly half (45%) relying solely on ad-hoc "job well done" messages compared to just 7% of leaders.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to have formal or a mix of formal and informal programs and are less likely to have informal programs.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Blend formal and informal recognition. Pair structured programs with frequent, in-the-moment appreciation to keep momentum between formal milestones. Public shout-outs, certificates, and peer-driven recognition are low-cost levers that often carry as much weight as larger awards, so use them to sustain motivation without straining the budget.
  • Standardize to protect fairness and clarity. Build consistent criteria and processes so recognition is applied evenly rather than at the discretion of individual managers. Standardization reduces the risk of bias, sets clear performance expectations, and ensures employees understand exactly what earns recognition.

Finding: The CEO is most commonly responsible for approving R&R initiatives, though no clear industry standard exists

Who’s actually responsible for approving R&R initiatives? Organizations take different approaches, with no clear industry standard. The most commonly cited decision-maker is the CEO, responsible for approving R&R in 23% of organizations. The Chief HR Officer (CHRO) follows at 17%, while heads of departments are cited by 15% and HR business partners by 8%.

Finding: CHROs remain most responsible for implementing R&R programs

When looking at who’s most responsible for implementing R&R programs, the CHRO takes top spot (23%). Given the CHRO’s strategic role, it makes sense that they oversee implementation to ensure alignment with organizational goals. That said, execution typically requires collaboration; immediate supervisors play a key role in embedding recognition into day-to-day team dynamics, while HR business partners help ensure consistency and effectiveness across the organization.

Finding: R&R are most likely to be closely tied to organizational values and performance

Values and performance lead as the strongest anchors for R&R programs. This suggests that the most established organizations have moved toward rewarding behaviors that reflect cultural alignment.

Performance also emphasizes the importance of recognition, as we can correlate it back to the earlier finding suggesting that nine in ten employees believe recognition improves their performance.

Personal milestones trail at just 21% combined. This gap likely reflects the challenge of tracking and acknowledging life events at scale, as well as uncertainty among organizations about the appropriate boundaries of workplace recognition.

Finding: R&R nominations are most often made by an employee’s manager

Who nominates employees for R&R? The only two options selected by a majority are:

  • manager nomination (72%)
  • peer nomination (64%)

Managers have the clearest insight into employee performance, regularly checking in on goals, leading one-on-ones, and conducting performance reviews. This makes them well-positioned to recognize accomplishments and nominate employees for R&R.

Results-based nominations (36%) represent the only other method with meaningful adoption, capturing roughly one in three organizations. This suggests that while outcome-driven recognition retains relevance, it is increasingly complemented by more qualitative, relationship-driven approaches rather than standing alone as the primary mechanism.

Differences by organizational size

Small organizations are most likely to rely on manager nominations, midsized organizations show a similar pattern, and large organizations are the only segment where peer nominations slightly surpass manager nominations. Large organizations are also more likely to use a results-based nomination criteria.

Throughout the report, we look at the findings based on organizational size. We deem organizations with 1 to 99 employees as small, those with 100-999 as midsized, and those with 1,000 or more employees as large.

Finding: R&R leaders are far more likely to use results-based criteria

R&R leaders are more likely than laggards to also use results-based nomination (53% vs. 29%) and manager nomination (81% vs. 57%). And R&R leaders use a method that none of the laggards use: system-generated nominations based on criteria.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Clarify who owns R&R. Responsibility is split, the CEO most often approves initiatives (23%) while the CHRO most often implements them (23%), with no clear standard. Assign explicit accountability for both approval and implementation so initiatives don't stall between strategy and execution.
  • Anchor recognition to values and performance, then deepen it. Recognition is most often tied to values (45%) and performance (42%), with professional milestones (30%) and tenure (31%) trailing. Build on that foundation by extending the same intentionality to milestones, and by making recognition more frequent and personal rather than simply broader.
  • Broaden nomination sources. Peer nominations (64%) and results-based criteria (36%) complement manager input and surface contributions that might otherwise be missed. Consider system-generated nominations based on defined criteria.

Measuring R&R Effectiveness

Finding: Many organizations have no fixed schedule for reviewing the effectiveness of their R&R programs

Many organizations (40%) report having no fixed schedule for reviewing the effectiveness of their R&R programs, and another 9% say they never review effectiveness at all. Just 22% review annually, and fewer than one in four (23%) review at least quarterly. This lack of regular cadence makes it difficult to catch issues early and course-correct R&R programs over time.

Finding: R&R leaders more than twice as likely as laggards to review the effectiveness of their programs continuously

R&R leaders review their programs more frequently than laggards do. In fact, many laggards have no fixed schedule for reviewing program effectiveness or never review it at all. However, more than half of R&R leaders review their programs more than once a year.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to review the effectiveness of R&R programs frequently.

Finding: Organizations measure R&R effectiveness in various ways, but one-fifth don’t measure effectiveness at all

The most common method for measuring R&R effectiveness is conducting annual or periodic engagement surveys (45%), followed by observing trends in employee participation rates (42%) and observing improved employee confidence or enthusiasm (39%).

While some organizations track more formal metrics, it’s far from common. Only 30% are monitoring manager engagement levels, even fewer are conducting pulse surveys (25%), or analyzing impact on employee retention (23%).

Notably, 20% of organizations report that they do not evaluate R&R effectiveness at all, a concerning gap given the significant investment many organizations make in these programs.

Finding: R&R leaders are more than three times more likely as laggards to examine the impact of R&R on employee performance metrics

R&R leaders are far more likely than laggards to measure the effectiveness of R&R programs and/or capabilities. This gap between leaders and laggards shows up in all the survey methods of measuring effectiveness. Also, 43% of laggards do not measure the effectiveness at all.

Finding: Just over a third of employees receive meaningful recognition from their manager on a weekly basis or more

Only 35% hear meaningful recognition from their manager weekly or daily, and 38% receive it that often from peers. Notably, peers are the slightly more frequent source of day-to-day recognition, edging out managers on daily acknowledgment.

At the other end, recognition reaches a sizeable share of employees only sporadically from either direct managers or peers. For these employees, recognition is an occasional event rather than an ongoing habit.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Incorporate industry benchmarks. Compare R&R program effectiveness and identify areas for improvement. Research best practices and adjust programs to stay competitive and to keep employees engaged and happy.
  • Analyze the impact of R&R initiatives on employee retention to ensure they contribute to long-term engagement. Track turnover rates before and after program changes to measure effectiveness.
  • Track employee participation trends to measure program adoption and make necessary adjustments. Use recognition platform data or survey feedback to identify engagement patterns.
  • Leverage pulse surveys to gather structured feedback and align R&R programs with employee needs. Design surveys with targeted questions to assess program impact.
  • Strengthen data-driven decision-making by linking R&R initiatives to performance metrics. Compare productivity, satisfaction, and financial returns to evaluate success.

What Employees Want in R&R Programs

Finding: Monetary incentives are the most popular rewards with employees

What incentives are most popular with employees? When we asked respondents of this study which rewards are most popular with employees, it is understandable monetary-related incentives top the list. Gift cards follow closely, likely because they are versatile and makes them an appealing choice, especially when tailored to individual preferences.

Finding: Nine in ten employees say paid time off and flexibility are highly important to their job satisfaction

When employees were asked which perks and rewards matter most to their overall job satisfaction, paid time off and flexibility was by far the top response, with 91% rating it very important or essential. This reinforces what HR professionals already identify as the leading advantages of R&R, that is, improving company culture (65%) and enhancing the employee experience (60%).

Professional development (66%) and cash-based rewards (63%) follow as the next most valued. Development opportunities help keep talent engaged, particularly where upward mobility is limited, while cash-based rewards offer a tangible way to show employees they are appreciated. When personalized, these rewards represent a relatively small investment that can yield significant returns for culture and perceived program effectiveness, especially in difficult economic times.

Finding: R&R leaders are twice as likely as laggards to say team bonding events are a popular reward with employees

R&R leaders are much more likely than laggards to say team bonding events and experiences are popular rewards. Laggards, on the other hand, are much more likely to say additional paid time off is a popular reward. None of the laggards say earned reward points are popular, possibly because they do not have a points program at all.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to say the rewards in the chart are popular with employees.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Use team-based rewards for broader impact. Favor shared experiences like team bonding events over purely individual rewards when the goal is collective morale. Group rewards reach more people at once and reinforce the relationships and collaboration that individual incentives tend to overlook.
  • Give employees flexibility through earned points. Let employees accumulate reward points they can redeem for the things that actually matter to them, rather than dictating a one-size-fits-all reward. Pair this with an easy-to-use platform so accruing and redeeming points stays simple and doesn't become a barrier to participation.
  • Offer experience-based rewards. Provide experiential options such as event tickets to create recognition moments that feel memorable rather than transactional. Survey employees first to learn which experiences they value most, so the rewards land as genuinely meaningful rather than generic.

Finding: Employees most value recognition from top leadership

The three most popular recognition types are all human and relational in nature, that is, recognition from top leadership (56%), manager expressions of gratitude (56%), and peer-to-peer recognition (46%), reinforcing that employees value acknowledgment from people over programs. Visibility matters too, with public communication via social media or newsletters (39%) rounding out the top tier.

The middle tier employee appreciation days (32%), social gatherings (31%), award nominations (25%), and trophies or certificates (24%) reflects enduring appetite for collective and symbolic forms of recognition, even as more personal approaches dominate. Employee of the month (23%) and written notes (23%) trail but retain a foothold, suggesting that classic formats still hold value when executed meaningfully.

Differences by organizational size

Small organizations are most likely to rely on manager expressions of gratitude as a popular form of recognition, while midsized organizations continue to favor leadership recognition. Large organizations, however, are most likely to highlight peer-to-peer recognition, suggesting recognition becomes increasingly decentralized as workforce size grows.

Finding: R&R leaders are more than twice as likely as laggards to say employee appreciation day is one of the most popular types of recognition

More than half of R&R leaders say recognition from top leadership and peer-to-peer recognition are among their most popular types of recognition. R&R laggards are far less likely to say these are popular rewards. Almost half of R&R leaders say employee appreciation day is popular, while just one-fifth of laggards say the same.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to use the listed forms of recognition.

Finding: One-third of organizations take employee feedback into account to a high or very high degree when introducing new R&R initiatives

Gathering and acting on employee feedback is essential for effective rewards and recognition programs. We found that one-third of organizations take employee input into account to a high or very high degree when introducing new initiatives. The most common response is at a moderate degree (39%), while about one in three organizations (29%) take feedback into account only to a low degree or not at all. These findings suggest there is significant room for organizations to more meaningfully incorporate employee voices in shaping their R&R programs.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to use the listed forms of recognition.

Finding: R&R leaders are more than four times as likely to take employee feedback into account to a high or very high degree when introducing new R&R initiatives

Two-thirds of R&R leaders ensure their R&R program stays on track by taking employee opinion into account to a high or very high degree when introducing new reward and recognition initiatives. Fewer than one in five R&R laggards do so.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to take employee feedback into account when introducing R&R initiatives.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Diversify the reward mix. Offer a range of reward types rather than relying on a single format, since employee preferences vary widely. A varied menu lets people gravitate toward what resonates with them, which makes recognition feel personal rather than perfunctory and broadens the program's overall appeal.
  • Involve senior leaders directly. Have recognition come straight from top leadership rather than only through managers or HR. When employees hear appreciation directly from senior leaders, it signals that their contributions are visible at the highest level and carries a weight that routine recognition often can't match.
  • Establish an annual employee appreciation day. Set aside a dedicated day each year to celebrate the entire workforce at once. A shared moment of organization-wide recognition reinforces a culture of appreciation and ensures every employee feels acknowledged, regardless of whether they were singled out during the year.

Finding: Organizations most commonly rely on annual surveys and team meetings to gather employee feedback on R&R initiatives

Annual/periodic engagement surveys (59%) and one-on-ones with managers (48%) lead as the most widely used feedback methods, reflecting a preference for structured, scheduled touchpoints over real-time listening. Exit interviews (51%) and team meetings (45%) are also popular. Though the prominence of exit interviews is notable, gathering R&R feedback at the point of departure is inherently retrospective, limiting an organization's ability to act on it in time to make a difference.

Pulse or continuous surveys (39%) show meaningful adoption, suggesting a growing recognition that periodic snapshots alone may be insufficient. The overall picture mirrors the review frequency findings: feedback collection, like program evaluation, tends to be episodic rather than embedded, leaving organizations with delayed signals on whether their R&R efforts are landing.

Finding: R&R leaders are more than twice as likely to use team meetings to get feedback on R&R initiatives

R&R leaders use more methods than laggards to get feedback on their R&R initiatives. They are more than twice as likely to use team meetings, open discussions in town hall meetings, exit interviews, stay interviews, and focus groups.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to use most of the following methods to get employee feedback with the exception of exit interviews.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Use team meetings to gather feedback on R&R initiatives and encourage open discussion.
  • Implement ongoing feedback channels like Slack to collect continuous input from employees. Set up dedicated channels where employees can share suggestions and reactions to R&R programs.
  • Conduct stay interviews and focus groups to understand what motivates employees and how R&R initiatives impact retention and engagement. Ask targeted questions about recognition preferences and satisfaction and identify areas for improvement.
  • Schedule meetings at a regular cadence to review the feedback and make adjustments to the program.

Challenges Impacting R&R Success

Finding: The biggest challenges organizations face are cost and inconsistent application of R&R programs

We asked respondents to identify their top five challenges with R&R programs. The most commonly cited issues are cost (49%) and inconsistent application of R&R (46%). Without equitable and consistent application, recognition programs can feel arbitrary, leading to concerns about favoritism and undermining employee trust. Cost remains a perennial barrier, particularly for organizations that want to invest in meaningful R&R but face budget constraints.

Loss of engagement among those not receiving R&R (38%), lack of leadership involvement (34%), and a lack of measurable performance goals (28%) round out the top five. This is a reminder that even well-designed programs can fall flat without effective communication and capable managers.

Differences by organizational size

Small organizations are most likely to identify cost as their biggest challenge (64%), midsized organizations are most concerned about employee disengagement among those not receiving recognition and inconsistent application (both 50%), while large organizations are equally concerned with cost, inconsistency, and poor visibility of programs (41%).

Finding: Nearly two-thirds of organizations spend $700 or less per employee per year on R&R, with nearly half spending $300 or less

Nearly half of organizations spend $300 or less per employee per year and a further 11% spend between $301 and $500. Meanwhile, 7% of organizations do not spend any money on R&R. A strong budget is not always necessary for effective recognition. Thoughtful, well-structured programs can have meaningful impact even with modest resources.

The Future of R&R Initiatives

Finding: Nearly half say their organization will nurture a culture that encourages rewards and recognition over the next two years

Change is inevitable, but how are rewards and recognition expected to evolve in the next two years? HR professionals believe it will most commonly change by:

  • nurturing a culture that encourages R&R (47%)
  • providing greater visibility into individual accomplishments (37%)
  • becoming more personalized (31%)

Over one in four (28%) plan to invest in better manager training on R&R. This is a meaningful shift given that lack of managerial training was identified as a key challenge.

Finding: R&R leaders are twice as likely as laggards to say they will provide greater visibility into company wins

R&R leaders are more likely than laggards to be improving visibility of individual accomplishments and company wins in their R&R programs. They are also working to make the programs more inclusive. R&R laggards, however, are less likely to expect a change in their R&R programs over the next two years. In fact, nearly a third do not expect their organizations to make any changes.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to say R&R in their organizations will provide greater visibility into individual accomplishments, company wins and become more inclusive over the next two years.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on the research, consider the suggestions below:

  • Recognize the importance of creating a culture that supports your R&R program.
  • Help people understand what is behind rewards by providing greater visibility into individual accomplishments.
  • Use the rewards program to remind people of collective goals by increasing the visibility of company wins.

Future of Technology and AI in R&R

Finding: Technology will most likely aid R&R by improving ease of tracking and reporting and allowing for better insights through analytics

We asked respondents to identify the top five ways technology will support their organization’s R&R program in the near future. The top two responses are “improve ease of tracking and reporting” (55%) and “allow for better insights through analytics” (51%). Technology enables those without a data background to interpret insights more easily, streamlining both tracking and reporting.

Other top responses include reducing administrative burden through automation (46%) and aiding in instantaneous or real-time recognition (38%). Reducing administrative tasks frees HR professionals to focus on more strategic and fulfilling work. Real-time recognition tools help organizations capitalize on moments that matter, reinforcing positive behaviors as they happen.

Finding: Nearly three-quarters of R&R leaders think technology will improve ease of tracking and reporting

Seven in ten R&R leaders are optimistic that technology will improve the ease of tracking and reporting, while allowing for better insights through analytics. Fewer than half of the R&R laggards feel that way. Even more dramatically, R&R leaders are almost three times as likely as laggards to believe technology will help with personalization of rewards.

Results of Chi-square Test

A chi-square test of independence shows that R&R leaders are significantly more likely than R&R laggards to use technology in the ways listed below.

Finding: More than one-third believe AI will help track what employees are recognizing their colleagues for

Artificial intelligence is set to transform rewards and recognition programs. The most commonly cited belief is that AI will help track what employees are recognizing others for (36%), providing data-driven insights into recognition patterns and culture. A further 34% expect AI to track employee performance, while 32% believe AI will facilitate faster, in-the-moment recognition.

HRRI Strategic Recommendations

Based on our research, please consider the following suggestions:

  • Use technology to reduce administrative burden. Respondents expect technology to help most by easing tracking and reporting (55%) and automating administrative work (46%). Tools that streamline these tasks free HR to focus on the strategic, human side of recognition.
  • Turn analytics into insight. Half of respondents (51%) expect better insights through analytics. Use reporting to identify recognition patterns, surface gaps, and make the program's impact visible to leadership in terms they value.
  • Pilot AI for recognition equity. A quarter of respondents (26%) see AI flagging teams that aren't getting recognition. Start with focused, low-risk applications, such as identifying under-recognized groups or enabling faster in-the-moment recognition (32%) before scaling.
  • Keep human judgment in the loop. While AI is expected to track recognition (36%) and performance (34%), recognition is fundamentally relational. Use AI to inform and prompt managers, not to replace the personal acknowledgment that makes recognition meaningful.

Key Takeaways

Based on the findings from our research, below are some suggestions for organizations that wish to improve their rewards and recognition programs.

Key Takeaway 1: Define a clear R&R strategy anchored to values and performance.

Our research shows recognition is most often tied to organizational values (47%) and performance (41%), while milestones and tenure trail behind. Establish a structured approach that consistently connects recognition to what your organization cares about most, while allowing flexibility for team-specific initiatives. A defined strategy also makes it easier to judge whether the program is achieving its intended impact.

Key Takeaway 2: Combine formal structure with informal, in-the-moment recognition.

Most organizations (56%) already use a hybrid approach, and R&R leaders are four times as likely as laggards to run formal programs. Pair structured systems that ensure consistency with the flexibility for spontaneous, timely appreciation. This balance keeps recognition both fair and frequent rather than forcing a choice between the two.

Key Takeaway 3: Apply recognition consistently to avoid favoritism.

Inconsistent application, including concerns about favoritism is among the top challenges organizations cite. Standardized criteria, clear eligibility, and transparent processes help ensure recognition feels earned rather than arbitrary and prevent the disengagement that can occur among employees who feel overlooked.

Key Takeaway 4: Train managers to recognize employees effectively.

Managers make the majority of nominations (72%) and have the clearest view of day-to-day performance, yet recognition reaches many employees only sporadically. Provide guidance on delivering timely, meaningful recognition, balancing public and private appreciation, and recognizing contributions beyond the usual high performers. Clear guidelines and allocated budgets help make recognition a routine habit.

Key Takeaway 5: Balance monetary and non-monetary rewards around what employees value.

Offer a mix of financial incentives and meaningful non-monetary rewards such as flexibility, career development, and public recognition. Employee feedback points strongly to paid time off and flexibility, professional development, and personalized cash recognition as the rewards that matter most, aligning programs with those priorities improves both satisfaction and perceived effectiveness.

Key Takeaway 6: Establish a regular cadence for measuring effectiveness.

Many organizations (40%) have no fixed schedule for reviewing R&R, 9% never review it at all, and 20% do not evaluate effectiveness in any way. Commit to assessing programs at least a few times a year, and pair sentiment measures like engagement surveys with hard metrics such as turnover and performance to understand what is genuinely working.

Key Takeaway 7: Prioritize just-in-time recognition.

Timely recognition has a greater impact than delayed rewards. Acknowledging achievements as they happen reinforces positive behaviors, increases motivation, and makes recognition more meaningful. Encourage managers to offer real-time appreciation rather than waiting for formal reviews or annual awards, particularly important given that feeling valued is strongly linked to how well employees perform.

Key Takeaway 8: Use technology and AI to enhance recognition and rewards.

Organizations expect technology to help most by improving the ease of tracking and reporting (55%), enabling better insights through analytics (51%), and reducing administrative burden through automation (46%). AI offers further potential to track recognition patterns, surface teams that aren't being recognized, and support faster, more personalized recognition, provided it informs and prompts managers rather than replacing the human judgment that makes recognition authentic.
